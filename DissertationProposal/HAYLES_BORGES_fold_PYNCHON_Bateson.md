# PART A. Borges through the Leibnizian fold

## A1. Deleuze got there first, and says so on the page

This is not a reading that has to be constructed. **Deleuze reads Borges through Leibniz explicitly, in *The Fold*, pp. 62-63**, in the chapter "Incompossibility, Individuality, Liberty." The index entry is "Borges, Jorge Luis, 62-63, 81."

> "All these Sextuses are possible, but they are part of incompossible worlds. A bifurcation, like the exit from the temple, is called a point in the neighborhood of series' divergence. **Borges, one of Leibniz's disciples, invoked the Chinese philosopher-architect Ts'ui Pen, the inventor of the 'garden with bifurcating paths,' a baroque labyrinth whose infinite series converge or diverge, forming a webbing of time embracing all possibilities.**"

And then what Deleuze says Borges does **to** Leibniz, which is the sharper half:

> "**It is clear why Borges invokes the Chinese philosopher rather than Leibniz. He wanted** . . . **to have God pass into existence all incompossible worlds at once instead of choosing one of them, the best.** And probably it would be globally possible, since incompossibility is an original relation, distinct from impossibility or contradiction."

> "Leibniz, who strongly distrusts the Cartesian argument of the nonmalevolent God, gives him a new basis at the level of incompossibility: **God plays tricks, but he also furnishes the rules of the game (contrary to Borges's and Leblanc's game without rules).**"

So Deleuze's Borges is a Leibnizian who has removed the rule. Leibniz's God calculates an infinity of possible worlds and brings one into existence; Borges's God brings them all in at once. The difference between the two is a single constraint, not a different ontology.

**Incompossibility defined,** pp. 59-60, because the word is doing all the work: it is "**a vice-diction, not a contradiction**," and "Leibniz gives the new relation the name of incompossibility." Compossibles are "the totality of converging and extensive series that constitute the world"; incompossibles are "**the series that diverge**, and that from then on belong to two possible worlds." Divergence is the whole of it: "**The eventual divergence of series is what allows for the definition of incompossibility.**"

**And the Whitehead turn, p. 81**, the second Borges page, where Deleuze takes the Leibnizian constraint off himself:

> "For Whitehead (and for many modern philosophers), on the contrary, bifurcations, divergences, incompossibilities, and discord belong to the same motley world that can no longer be included in expressive units, but only made or undone according to prehensive units and variable configurations or changing captures. **In a same chaotic world divergent series are endlessly tracing bifurcating paths. It is a 'chaosmos' of the type found in Joyce, but also in Maurice Leblanc, Borges, or Gombrowicz** . . . Even God desists from being a Being who compares worlds and chooses the richest compossible. **He becomes Process**, a process that at once affirms incompossibilities and passes through them. The play of the world has changed in a unique way, because **now it has become the play that diverges**."

Three things follow for the proposal, and the third is the one that matters most.

## A2. The fold is an etymological argument, and it is about labyrinths

Deleuze's definition, in the first chapter, "The Pleats of Matter":

> "**A labyrinth is said, etymologically, to be multiple because it contains many folds. The multiple is not only what has many parts but also what is folded in many ways.** A labyrinth corresponds exactly to each level: the continuous labyrinth in matter and its parts, the labyrinth of freedom in the soul and its predicates. **If Descartes did not know how to get through the labyrinth, it was because he sought its secret of continuity in rectilinear tracks**, and the secret of liberty in a rectitude of the soul."

Two notes on this. First, it is an **etymological argument used as method**, which the reading plan already records as a precedent in five other places; this is a sixth, and it is the one that defines the term. Second, Descartes fails at the labyrinth for the same reason Hayles says articulation fails at the field: he looks for continuity **in straight lines**. Hayles's sentence at p. 59 is that the medium is "inherently linear, fragmented, and unidirectional." Deleuze's diagnosis of Descartes is the same diagnosis, named as a failure of a person rather than of a medium.

And Leibniz's own version of the part containing the whole, from the held *Monadology*:

> §65: "each portion of matter is not only infinitely divisible, as the ancients observed, but is also **actually subdivided without end**, each part into further parts, of which each has some motion of its own; otherwise **it would be impossible for each portion of matter to express the whole universe**."
> §66: "**in the smallest particle of matter there is a world of creatures, living beings, animals, entelechies, souls.**"
> §67: "**Each portion of matter may be conceived as like a garden full of plants and like a pond full of fishes. But each branch of every plant, each member of every animal, each drop of its liquid parts is also some such garden or pond.**"
> §56: each simple substance "has relations which express all the others, and, consequently, **it is a perpetual living mirror of the universe**."
> §61: "**all is a plenum** (and thus all matter is connected together) . . . every body feels the effect of all that takes place in the universe, so that **he who sees all might read in each what is happening everywhere**, and even what has happened or shall happen, observing in the present that which is far off as well in time as in place."

**§61 is the Aleph.** Hayles's chapter is about a sphere an inch across that contains "all space . . . actual and undiminished," and Leibniz's §61 is a sentence saying that whoever could see properly would read everything that happens anywhere in any part. 1714 and 1945, with no line of influence claimed by anyone.

**And §61 ends with Hayles's own epigraph, in Greek.** Latta's translation closes the section with the phrase Leibniz quotes there, **sympnoia panta**, all things breathing together. Hayles opens her Introduction with two epigraphs that say the same thing in two other languages: **Saussure, "Tout se tient"**, and **Pynchon, "Everything is connected"**. Three statements of one sentence, 1714, 1916 and 1973, printed in Greek, French and English, two of them on the same page of her book and the third in a text she never cites. Worth knowing before the sentence gets attributed to anybody in particular.

## A3. The two infinities are not the same infinity

This is the one place where the pairing has to be made carefully rather than simply asserted, and the distinction is in the held Deleuze.

Hayles's Borges chapter runs entirely on **Cantor**: actually infinite sets, transfinite numbers written with the aleph, one-to-one correspondence, the result that "the part is no less copious than the whole," and the Axiom of Choice. Her phrase for what Borges gets from it: "**infinities can be opened within any element, no matter how small, simply by suggesting that it is part of an infinite series.**"

Deleuze's Leibniz does not run on transfinites, and Deleuze says so:

> "**The mathematician Abraham Robinson has proposed considering Leibniz's monad as an infinite number quite different from transfinites**, as a unit surrounded by a zone of infinitely small numbers that reflect the convergent series of the world."

And Deleuze's account of what infinity becomes in Leibniz: "infinity also changes meaning. It acquires a fourth and still current dimension: it is no longer defined either by itself or by the 'limit' of a series, but by **a law of order or continuity that classifies limits or transforms series into a 'totality'** (the presently infinite totality of the world, or the transfinite)."

So the two readings of Borges reach the same figure by two different mathematics: **Cantor's transfinite**, where the part is as copious as the whole because the sets can be matched element for element, and **Robinson's infinitesimal**, where the monad is a unit with a zone of the infinitely small around it. Hayles's part-containing-whole is a counting result; Leibniz's is a nesting, a garden inside each drop. **Recorded and left there.** The divergence is where a claim could be made, and it is not a dispute to settle: Hayles is reading the mathematics Borges read (he reviewed Kasner and Newman in 1940), and Deleuze is reading the mathematics Leibniz invented.

## A4. What the fold gives Hayles's chapter that Cantor does not, and the counts

**Counts over the full extracted text of *The Cosmic Web*, 78,802 words, word boundaries where the form allowed:**

| term | count in Hayles 1984 |
|---|---|
| Leibniz | **0** |
| monad | **0** |
| baroque | **0** |
| Deleuze | **0** |
| fold, folds, folding, folded | **5** |
| Whitehead | 3 (one of them the "climate of opinion" phrase, p. 22) |

So the fold is not a term in this book. But **two of its five appearances are at exactly the places where the fold concept belongs**, and both are quotations or summaries rather than her own vocabulary:

1. **Bohm's implicate order, p. 57**, quoted by her: "In the implicate order, 'each part . . . contains the whole in some sense. **The whole is folded into each part.**' In this view 'points are not the fundamental notion any more as in the Cartesian system. Rather, what is fundamental is some region which contains, in some sense, the order of the whole.'" That is *Monadology* §66-67 in the vocabulary of 1978 physics, and Hayles prints it without noticing.
2. **Borges's strategy, p. 143**, her own summary: "then **the sequence is folded back on itself**, so that closure becomes impossible because of the endless, paradoxical circling of a self-referential system."
3. The other three are Lawrence's "folded centres of darkness" and the imagery "folding in upon itself in increasingly opaque convolutions" (chapter 3), and one incidental use.

**Three things the fold supplies that the transfinite reading does not (mine, not hers):**

- **A positive account of divergence.** In Hayles the Strange Loop is a predicament: Cantor found self-reference "an embarrassment," Borges finds it "an embarrassment of riches," and the chapter ends with the subverter subverted and no resting place. In Deleuze the divergence is not a failure of the series but the definition of a relation, incompossibility, which is "an original relation, distinct from impossibility or contradiction," a vice-diction and not a contradiction. The same object read twice, once as a trap and once as a relation.
- **A reason why the labyrinth is the right figure.** Hayles's chapter uses labyrinth throughout as Borges's own image, and at p. 148 as the test of artifice: "if we may decipher its principles, then it is not reality but a labyrinth, that is to say, an artifact." Deleuze's etymology says a labyrinth is multiple because it is folded, so the labyrinth is not a maze to be solved or an artifact to be unmasked but a count of folds. Nothing in Hayles asks why a labyrinth rather than a maze or a net.
- **A way to hold the part-whole result without the Platonism.** Hayles shows Cantor retreating to Platonism to defend the alephs, and Borges refusing it ("I think I'm Aristotelian, but I wish it were the other way"). The Leibnizian version gets the part containing the whole without any ideal realm: §57, the same town looked at from various sides, "so many different universes, which, nevertheless are nothing but aspects of a single universe, according to the special point of view of each Monad."

## A5. Three things to check

1. **Does Hayles ever take up Leibniz or the fold later?** She has Bohm's implicate order in 1984 and no Leibniz. *How We Became Posthuman* is held and can be searched. The question is whether the fold arrives in her work at all, or whether it reaches her project only through Deleuze and Manning.
2. **Deleuze's page 81 and the "chaosmos" list.** The sentence puts Borges with Joyce, Leblanc and Gombrowicz, and the word chaosmos is Joyce's. Worth tracing once if the word is going to be used.
3. **Entelechy.** *Monadology* §66 lists "entelechies" among what is in the smallest particle of matter, and the Yergeau close read recorded **entelech- 101 times** in *Authoring Autism*. Whether Yergeau's entelechy has anything to do with Leibniz's, or whether both go back separately to Aristotle, is open and should not be asserted either way until checked. Logged here only because the word count is striking.

# PART B. Pynchon through Bateson's difference that makes a difference

## B1. What Bateson actually says, in the held text

Six occurrences of the phrase in *Steps to an Ecology of Mind*. Four of them matter, and they do not all say the same thing.

**The definition as an identity:**

> "**A difference which makes a difference is an idea. It is a 'bit,' a unit of information.**"

**The definition with a future tense in it:**

> "**The technical term 'information' may be succinctly defined as any difference which makes a difference in some later event.** This definition is fundamental for all analysis of cybernetic systems and organization."

**The definition as exclusion, which is the one that bites:**

> "**Information, in the technical sense, is that which excludes certain alternatives.** The machine with a governor does not elect the steady state; it prevents itself from staying in any alternative state; and in all such cybernetic systems, corrective action is brought about by difference."

**And the definition as selection from an infinitude, which is the passage to keep:**

> "I suggest that Kant's statement can be modified to say that **there is an infinite number of differences around and within the piece of chalk**. There are differences between the chalk and the rest of the universe, between the chalk and the sun or the moon. And within the piece of chalk, there is for every molecule an infinite number of differences between its location and the locations in which it might have been. **Of this infinitude, we select a very limited number, which become information.** In fact, what we mean by information, the elementary unit of information, **is a difference which makes a difference, and it is able to make a difference because the neural pathways along which it travels and is continually transformed are themselves provided with energy. The pathways are ready to be triggered. We may even say that the question is already implicit in them.**"

Two further pieces of Bateson that the Pynchon chapter walks straight into.

**The circuit, and the refusal of the cutting agent:**

> "This self-corrective (i.e., mental) process is brought about by a total system, tree-eyes-brain-muscles-axe-stroke-tree; and it is this total system that has the characteristics of immanent mind. More correctly, we should spell the matter out as: (differences in tree) - (differences in retina) - (differences in brain) - (differences in muscles) - (differences in movement of axe) - (differences in tree), etc. **What is transmitted around the circuit is transforms of differences** . . . But this is not how the average Occidental sees the event sequence of tree felling. He says, '**I cut down the tree**' and he even believes that there is a delimited agent, the 'self,' which performed a delimited 'purposive' action upon a delimited object."

**No unilateral control:**

> "we know that **no part of such an internally interactive system can have unilateral control over the remainder or over any other part. The mental characteristics are inherent or immanent in the ensemble as a whole** . . . In the steam engine with a 'governor,' the very word 'governor' is a misnomer if it be taken to mean that this part of the system has unilateral control."

**And the map, credited to Korzybski:**

> "the origin of what Korzybski has called the map-territory relation: the fact that a message, of whatever kind, does not consist of those objects which it denotes ('**The word "cat" cannot scratch us**')."

## B3. The reading: equal distance against the unit of information

Hayles's Pynchon chapter has two sentences that sit on Bateson's definition without touching it.

**The first is nearly Bateson's own,** p. 174: "the 'irrelevant' details define the background against which a figure can be discerned. Though the figure is usually the focus of our attention, the contrast with the background is what makes the figure visible. **Meaning is made possible, indeed, defined, by its distinction from non-meaning.**" Bateson's version is that a difference becomes information only by being selected out of an infinitude of differences, and that information "is that which excludes certain alternatives." Same claim, one framed as a condition of meaning in a text, the other as a definition of a unit.

**The second withdraws it,** pp. 174-175: Pynchon "refuses to make the distinction between the meaningful event and the 'irrelevant' detail. In *Gravity's Rainbow* **everything is placed at an equal distance from the reader**, so that background and foreground collapse into the same perceptual plane . . . **With everything at an equal distance from the reader, meaning can no longer be achieved by discerning difference. Rather, it emerges as a result of seeing that everything is at the same distance. Meaning thus becomes a function not of difference but of similarity**, arising not from distinguishing parts but from seeing the interconnection of the whole."

Read through Bateson, the two sentences describe one operation and then its removal. If information is a difference selected out of an infinitude, and if what makes the selection possible is that the pathways "are ready to be triggered" so that "the question is already implicit in them," then **a text at equal distance supplies no selection and asks no question**: every difference is admitted, nothing is excluded, and in Bateson's technical sense nothing is information. Hayles names who reads this way without flinching: "**a paranoid, or someone dropping acid, or a religious visionary who believes in Providential design** . . . in these views, **there are no irrelevancies**."

That is the join, and it is exact: **Bateson's definition makes "there are no irrelevancies" a description not of maximal information but of none.** Hayles arrives at the same place by a different route and calls it a gestalt: meaning "arrives as a gestalt, precipitating into awareness; either one sees the whole design, or one doesn't see it at all." Two accounts of what happens when nothing is excluded, one from cybernetics and one from gestalt psychology, and the chapter already carries the gestalt machinery (the equivocal figure, pp. 175-176, where "only one figure can be brought into focus at a time"). **Both are recorded; neither is corrected by the other.**

## B4. Four more places where the two texts touch

**Control.** Bateson: "no part of such an internally interactive system can have unilateral control over the remainder," and the governor is a misnomer. Hayles's Pynchon, p. 173, quoting the novel: Rathenau demands "what is the real nature of synthesis? And then: what is the real nature of control?"; and Feldspath from beyond: "**the illusion of control. That A could do B. But that was false. Completely. No one can do. Things only happen, A and B are unreal, are names for parts that ought to be inseparable.**" Bateson's sentence about the governor and Pynchon's sentence about A and B are the same sentence in two registers, one about a steam engine and one from a seance. And Hayles's Nabokov chapter, p. 136, supplies the third: "the desire for control is predicated on the assumption that it is possible to make an **unambiguous separation** between the one who controls and that which is controlled."

**The cut, across two chapters.** Bateson's Occidental says "I cut down the tree" and believes in "a delimited agent, the 'self,' which performed a delimited 'purposive' action upon a delimited object." Hayles's Pirsig chapter, p. 71, quotes Pirsig: "**The application of this knife, the division of the world into parts . . . is something somebody does.** From all this awareness we must select, and what we select and call consciousness is never the same as the awareness because the process of selection mutates it. We take a handful of sand from the endless landscape of awareness around us and call that handful of sand the world." **Pirsig's handful of sand and Bateson's piece of chalk are the same example.** Both say selection from an infinitude; Pirsig calls what is left over awareness, Bateson calls it the differences we did not select. Pirsig insists the selector must be visible in the picture ("a figure in the middle of it, sorting sand into piles"); Bateson dissolves the selector into the circuit. That difference is worth keeping rather than settling.

**The interface and the map.** Hayles's Pynchon, pp. 180-181: "'reality' exists on neither side, so that penetrating the interface merely takes us from one kind of reconstruction to another," and then "**interfaces are not barriers, but points of exchange, surfaces through which two orders of being can interpenetrate** . . . the **permeable membrane** it can become in the field view." Bateson's map-territory relation, credited to Korzybski, says the message does not consist of the objects it denotes: "The word 'cat' cannot scratch us." The two are not the same claim. Korzybski's map and territory are two levels with a traffic of denotation between them; Hayles's interface has no territory on either side. **A permeable membrane is not a map.**

**Negative entropy, the one term they share.** Bateson: "available energy (i.e., negative entropy) is a function of a difference between two temperatures. In this classical instance, '**information**' and '**negative entropy**' **overlap**," and the analyst of the creatura "will call this difference which makes a difference 'information' or 'negative entropy.'" Hayles's Pynchon chapter uses entropy six times and all of them thermodynamically: the heat death, the Second Law, Prigogine on structure-building far from equilibrium, Gold on entropy decreasing in a contracting universe. So the bridge Bateson builds between the two senses of entropy is available in her chapter's own vocabulary and is not crossed.
