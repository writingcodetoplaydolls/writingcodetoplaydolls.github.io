## 2. The connection that matters most

**p. 161, the opening sentences of Chapter 11.** Verified against the page image.

> It is probably true quite generally that in the history of human thinking the most fruitful developments frequently take place at those points where two different lines of thought meet. These lines may have their roots in quite different parts of human culture, in different times or different cultural environments or different religious traditions: hence if they actually meet, that is, if they are at least so much related to each other that a real interaction can take place, then one may hope that new and interesting developments will follow.

This is the claim about nodes, stated by Heisenberg in 1958 and about the history of thought rather than about physics. He does not ask for a line of transmission between the two lines of thought, or for one to have read the other, or for them to be contemporaries. He asks only that they be **"at least so much related to each other that a real interaction can take place."** That is a threshold of capacity, not of contact.

It sits in a chapter about atomic physics meeting cultural traditions that did not produce it.

### 3.1 Observer and observed, pp. 57 and 75

p. 57, verified against the page image:

> what we observe is not nature in itself but nature exposed to our method of questioning. Our scientific work in physics consists in asking questions about nature in the language that we possess and trying to get an answer from experiment by the means that are at our disposal. In this way quantum theory reminds us, as Bohr has put it, of the old wisdom that when searching for harmony in life one must never forget that in the drama of existence we are ourselves both players and spectators.

p. 75, verified against the page image:

> Natural science does not simply describe and explain nature; it is a part of the interplay between nature and ourselves; it describes nature as exposed to our method of questioning. This was a possibility of which Descartes could not have thought, but it makes the sharp separation between the world and the I impossible.

**Note what the Bohr sentence on p. 57 is.** It is a theatre figure: players and spectators in the drama of existence. Heisenberg reaches for a performance metaphor at the exact point where the separation of observer from observed fails.

### 3.2 Potentia, and the Whitehead link that is Northrop's and not Heisenberg's

**Whitehead appears three times in the whole book, at pp. 14, 27 and 28, and all three are in Northrop's introduction.** Heisenberg himself never writes the name. At p. 14 Northrop says:

> the most novel and important thesis of this book is its author's contention that quantum mechanics has brought the concept of potentiality back into physical science. This makes quantum theory as important for ontology as for epistemology. At this point, Heisenberg’s philosophy of physics has an element in common with that of Whitehead.

So the Heisenberg-to-Whitehead line runs through Northrop, in print, in 1958, in the book Hayles quotes. Hayles's own call on *Cosmic Web* p. 22 reaches for Whitehead's "climate of opinion". The two Whiteheads are not the same Whitehead, and that difference is worth holding rather than resolving.

### 3.3 Closed systems of concepts, pp. 78 and 85 to 91

Heisenberg lays out four finished conceptual systems: Newtonian mechanics, the theory of heat, electrodynamics with special relativity, and quantum theory. He describes what makes a system closed (p. 91), how a new one forms when the old concepts will not stretch (p. 88), and how two systems can both stand without one reducing to the other.

p. 85, on Newton:

> Newton begins his Principia with a group of definitions and axioms which are interconnected in such a way that they form what one may call a ‘closed system’. Each concept can be represented by a mathematical symbol

p. 78, on what a meaningless sentence can do:

> it opened the way to the establishment of new connections which would have been impossible if the sentence had a meaning.

### 3.4 Ordinary language outlasting precise language, p. 171

> the concepts of natural language, vaguely defined as they are, seem to be more stable in the expansion of knowledge than the precise terms of scientific language, derived as an idealization from only limited groups of phenomena. This is in fact not surprising since the concepts of natural language are formed by the immediate connection with reality; they represent reality. It is true that they are not very well defined and may therefore also undergo changes in the course of the centuries, just as reality itself did, but they never lose the immediate connection with reality.

### 4.1 This is the source of the epigraph to *The Cosmic Web* Chapter 1

**Hayles opens Chapter 1, "Spinning the Web", on printed p. 31, with a block quotation from *Physics and Philosophy*.** She gives no page. It is pp. 97 to 98 here.

The passage, verified against the page images:

> are the different styles of art an arbitrary product of the human mind? Here again we must not be misled by the Cartesian partition. The style arises out of the interplay between the world and ourselves, or more specifically between the spirit of the time and the artist. The spirit of a time is probably a fact as objective 98 PHYSICS AND PHILOSOPHY as any fact in natural science, and this spirit brings out certain features of the world which are even independent of time, and in this sense eternal. The artist tries by his work to make these features understandable, and in this attempt he is led to the forms of the style in which he works.

and the sentence it leads into, p. 98:

> Therefore, the two processes, that of science and that of art, are not very different. Both science and art form in the course of the centuries a human language by which we can speak about the more remote parts of reality, and the coherent sets of concepts as well as the different styles of art are different words or groups of words in this language.

**One discrepancy to check, not to settle.** Hayles's epigraph reads "The artist tries **in** his work to make these features understandable." **This copy reads "The artist tries by his work."** It may be an Allen & Unwin against Harper & Row difference, or it may be hers. The Harper & Row text is the one to check it against, since that is the edition she cites.

The sentence on p. 98 is the one that bears hardest on practice as research: science and art as **two forms of one process**, both of them languages built over centuries for the parts of reality that are out of ordinary reach, with styles of art and sets of concepts as words in the same language. It is not an analogy between art and science. It is a claim that they are the same kind of thing.

### 4.2 The rest of p. 97

> In this respect they may be compared to the different styles of art, say of architecture or music. A style of art can also be defined by a set of formal rules which are applied to the material of this special art. These rules can perhaps not be represented in a strict sense by a set of mathematical concepts and equations, but their fundamental elements are very closely related to the essential elements of mathematics. Equality and inequality, repetition and symmetry, certain group structures play the fundamental role both in art and in mathematics.

and:

> Art is always an idealization; the ideal is different from reality—at least from the reality of the shadows, as Plato would have put it—but idealization is necessary for understanding.

### 4.3 Complementarity reaching aesthetic experience, p. 155

> We realize that the situation of complementarity is not confined to the atomic world alone; we meet it when we reflect about a decision and the motives for our decision or when we have the choice between enjoying music and analyzing its structure.

**The choice between enjoying music and analyzing its structure** is complementarity applied to the practitioner's own position. Heisenberg puts aesthetic experience and structural analysis in the relation of two measurements that cannot be made at once. He also names poetry on the same page, as the ordinary use of language that the physicist's vague use resembles.

### 4.4 Where the book ends, p. 175

> there must always be a fundamental complementarity between deliberation and decision. In the prac- tical decisions of life it will scarcely ever be possible to go through all the arguments in favour of or against one possible decision, and one will therefore always have to act on insufficient evidence.

## 6. My reading, which is mine and is not a tag on anything

Four things, and the first is the one that may be useful.

1. **p. 161 is a found statement of the claim about nodes.** Heisenberg asks for capacity for interaction and nothing more: not contact, not transmission, not a shared period. He is talking about the history of human thinking, which is the register the claim needs.

2. **The art passage is structurally placed, not decorative.** It closes Chapter 6, the chapter on quantum theory's relation to the other sciences. Heisenberg reaches for art at the end of an argument about how incompatible conceptual systems coexist. Art enters where the question is how different closed systems can both be true.

3. **Hayles's chapter on field models opens on this passage.** The chapter that builds toward the p. 22 call for a field theory of culture begins with Heisenberg saying style arises from the interplay between the spirit of a time and the artist, and that the spirit of a time is as objective as any fact in natural science. That is already a field account of how art gets made, sitting in the epigraph slot.

4. **This book supplies Bohr at second hand only.** Bohr appears 32 times but always as reported by Heisenberg, and the disagreement between them that Barad makes central is not visible here. For Bohr himself the book is not a source.
