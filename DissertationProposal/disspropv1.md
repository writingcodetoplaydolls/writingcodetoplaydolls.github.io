I sit down to write angry. Irritated. The words I need to type were already composed, pouring out since I woke up this morning. So typing the words after the words were so carefully composed already is a second order version, an imperfect recollection of a perfect story, because all stories are singularly perfect through each frame/time/space through/with which they were composed.

As a photographer, my first art form/medium/expression with explicit tool, I was vehemently opposed to the crop. Maybe this was an attitude adopted from a professor I admired or was well considered originally in my own mind, but nonetheless I hold to it. A famous image of Irving Berlin was used as an example of the perfect crop of an image originally composed through the lens differently. Berlin sits at his huge black piano and the crop is tight around his body and the edges of the piano, with the rest of the behemoth of a thing left out of the printed black and white image. I believe the polemic was whether the photographer, who I can’t even remember, was a cheat or a genius.

Not so much with my 35mm camera, but religiously with my 6cm twin lens reflex, the camera that became an extension of my thoughts, my eyes, through which I situated myself and considered/pondered existence in three dimensional space, I never cropped. Never. Frequently not even the position of the camera was moved from the exact place I first put it down, heavy on its sturdy metal tripod. My unspoken/unwritten process/rule was that my first intuition of where to set the camera down, how to adjust the knobs and the legs of the tripod, just where to point the double stacked lenses of the clunky camera box, was the correct place/time/space/crop of the first frame of the 12 frame roll of medium format film.

I feel the same way about thinking, about writing, remembering, wondering, building networks/weaving together. Editing, or remembering afterwards and attempting to reconstruct the original through stream, is like cropping. Lazy. And also aggressive, disrespectful, unseemly, and maybe a bit haughty, as if the thing originally created could be wiped out, as if it didn’t exist, or didn’t deserve to exist somewhere/time/why in the way it was first conceived/or conceived itself.

So this will be the second time I have told this story/made this accounting/with homage and respect to the original thoughts, now gone, fleeting, flown out and not captured as they flew.

My camera sits beside me now, on the long high table that I use as a desk, that my husband made to use as a tool bench, but I sequestered, as I frequently do with things. The camera is similar in shape to a somewhat squatter saltine cracker box, black (in some places still shiny, in other places dull because the material has worn more over time and use), heavy, heavier than you would imagine a thing with a neck strap to be. I might have bought it in 1995, maybe 1996, while I was an undergraduate student at Louisiana State University. This camera, a Mamiya C3,  is my second iteration of a twin lens model, the first was much lighter, a Yashica D, and less robustly built. I don’t remember exactly when it died, but for a very long time I carried around its parts, thinking I might someday revive it, as it had been my first introduction to the medium format image and I owed some allegiance to it. My memory of that time is deeply marked by where I was living and who I was living with, as the years between high school and moving to Arizona in 1999 were splotchy/not tumultuous, that word implies more smoothness/gradient between events/places/people. I had taken black and white darkroom photography courses while I was in high school with Dr. Bill McBride, a professor at the local university who graciously also shared with us his passion for cameras and film. I have never been one for precision or meticulousness, so it took me two semesters to realize that I kept getting double exposures on the photo paper because I turned the enlarger bulb on and then placed the paper under the projected image. At some point an upperclassman pointed out my error, it bothers me that I can’t remember if his advice was unsolicited or not. My photo of a small spotted dog/maybe a puppy, shot from the perspective of me lying down on the ground to be level with the dog’s gaze, the owner’s legs/shows, and with the fence lines in the background maintaining a retrospective perfect parallel (I could check because the resulting art portfolio/printed anthology/book from high school is still on my shelf, but I won’t, because I prefer to remember my lines as perfectly parallel). I took pride in that image making the cover of something created at an art school (math, science, and the arts) because I did not consider myself a bonafide member of that student camp. I was a lurker, across subjects/people/ideas/ideologies. I also remember (maybe erroneously) a feeling that the self appointed real art students were not happy about my photo being on the cover when there were other more accomplished (in their perception) mediums like paint/paintings available. I was an interloper and not welcome.

I started college as a German/Russian language major, then anthropology (falling in love with cultural anthropology after the first intro course), and then later adding a photography/art minor, not a major because I could never get thru the required fastidiousness of the design courses which required Dantean levels of iteration through exacting lines in pencil, ink, gouache, and eraser. My first photography course was certainly black and white and most likely still with 35mm, but quickly shifted to my still now 30 years later color medium format 6cm square, sometimes positive, sometimes negative film.

Both the Mamiya and the Yashica were purchased at a camera shop that happened to be in the geography locus of my then world, on Government Street, near the garden district in Baton Rouge, across the street from the high school for gifted/talented students. The Yashica came first, then the Mamiya, and last the heavy sturdy steel tripod that I used to carry with me everywhere, running through airports, when you could still get to the gate at the last minute, with the legs tucked close in front of my body, I always imagined like a rifle would be carried, running into or from battle.

The twin-lens is a special camera for multiple reasons, but most pointedly because of the unique peculiar (even to me now 30 years after first looking through at the viewing glass) way it elongates and separates space, resulting in an image that feels three dimensional. I do not know if it is because of the lens mechanism, that there is one lens through which the viewer composes the scene, and another lens through which the shutter allows light to hit the film, or because of the resulting shape of the image, a perfect square. I do not know and nor have I ever even thought to investigate whether the square is a necessary companion to the twin-lens or some other configuration of image could be fashioned. Knowledge might kill the wonder.

Imagine the spatial feeling of looking at multiple layers of an old-fashioned animation cell, where each layer is given its own distance from the others. Imagine a diorama you made as a child or as an adult helping a child, because I don’t recollect any adults in my sphere of existence making a diorama that was not demanded of them. The multiple objects placed in the showbox scene are viewed through the front of the box and each object seems to have its own space just so. Cameras (I won’t say all) have an aperture and a shutter that together fashion the spatial illusion/resulting reality on the film. By opening up and closing down the aperture, the light is made to enter the hole either more directly, in parallel rays, or more diffusely/diffractively. When the aperture is smaller the resulting distance that can be in focus is longer. When the aperture is larger, the diffuse/diffracting light causes only a smaller portion of the depth of field to be in focus. My perception of a 35mm camera and film, and its rectangular composition, is that regardless of the aperture and the focal range/depth of field, the resulting image spatial illusion feels flat. In stark contrast, to my visceral impression, a twin-lens square, regardless of the aperture, creates spatial resonance/depth/and a feeling that the composition is always swirling into itself, like light into a black hole.

My inclination to use the camera is driven by a perception in a space that there is already a swirling/a vortex, some kind of harmonious constellation of objects/color/light that viscerally feels complete, attuned, vibratory, resonating. Immanence? I feel that swirling, I pull my camera case from the shelf, press the metal push button latch that releases the top of the cracked faux leather veneered case, check the side of the hefty awkward camera box for a tiny number on a silver dial that indicates whether there are frames left. I pull open the unwieldy tripod legs, click the camera into its perch on top, and open the viewing box on top of the camera. The twin-lens has a viewing glass on top of the camera, more similar in feeling to looking at a phone camera screen now, than looking through the viewfinder of a typical through the lens (TTL) style camera. The image floats, ephemeral, delicate, flipped horizontally backwards from the view from your naked eye. Most cameras have/had a pop up contraption, like the flaps of a cracker box, that shield the image and the eye from intruding light. If you were to watch someone take a photo from a twin-lens camera, they would frequently be holding the camera, cradled between both hands, held tight to their abdomen between their belly button and sternum. They would be looking down as they directed you to move a little left/right, and fiddling with a knob/knobs, a crank, and a number of long sliding pin-like apparatus that were surprisingly buttons. I always thought, when on occasion I was the watcher and not the photo taker, that users of the twin-lens camera seemed privy to some other/maybe even otherworldly perspective. They aren’t casual users, who just wanted to snap a quick photo, there was some affinity with the object that made the image possible, some connection of body, eye, contraption. If I didn’t have my own twin-lens camera, I might be envious.

*Pasted from comps v35, The Researcher as Apparatus, pp. 29–31 (cut from the final)*

The critique of researcher bias has traveled a specific arc across fields. The positivist ideal -- the view from nowhere, the neutral observer, the God's trick Haraway names -- gave way, under pressure from feminist, postcolonial, and interpretive traditions, to the demand for positionality disclosure. The researcher was asked to name her location: her race, gender, institutional affiliation, theoretical commitments. This disclosure was framed as a corrective -- a way of flagging potential distortion so that readers could adjust. The assumption remained that bias was a contamination to be acknowledged and, where possible, minimized. Research was still understood as something that would ideally happen without a situated subject in the way.

Posthumanist and new materialist frameworks reject the premise. If the world is always-becoming through intra-action, if there is no phenomenon prior to the apparatus that produces it, if matter and meaning co-constitute each other through specific material-discursive arrangements -- then there is no view from outside. The researcher is not adjacent to the research. She is part of the apparatus. Her situatedness does not introduce error into an otherwise clean process; it is constitutive of the process itself. Barad is unequivocal: the apparatuses that produce phenomena are not separate from what they produce. The agential cut that makes a phenomenon legible is always already enacted from within the entanglement, never from outside it. On this account, the demand that a researcher minimize her bias is a demand that she pretend to be something the ontology says she cannot be.

This move -- from situatedness as contamination to situatedness as constitutive -- was not invented by posthumanism. It was worked out first in feminist practice. Rich's "politics of location" is its founding gesture: the demand to begin not with a pretense of neutrality but with the body, the specific geography closest in, as the only honest epistemological starting point. Braidotti traces the line from Rich directly into new materialism's situated epistemology (*New Materialism*, p. 22), and Mąkowska (2022) shows that what Rich was enacting in her poetics -- mapping her own flesh as medical history, sociopolitical inscription, and epistemological ground simultaneously -- is structurally identical to what Barad will later formalize as the researcher's position within the apparatus. The apparatus argument is not a posthumanist novelty. It is feminist body theory formalized. Haraway's "God's trick" critique is the same critique Rich was making in 1984 when she insisted that there is no speaking from outside one's location, only from within it. What changes between Rich and Barad is vocabulary and philosophical frame. The underlying claim -- that the knower is always already inside what she studies, that this is not a problem to be corrected but a condition to be worked -- is continuous.

The artist-researcher takes this one step further, but the move is not toward greater centrality -- it is toward a more precise account of her position within a distributed apparatus. Braidotti's cartography is not the map one neutral observer makes of a pre-existing terrain. It is the specific trajectory of a specific nomadic subject moving through a specific configuration of forces, in intra-action with the other human and nonhuman actants that constitute the apparatus alongside her. The knowledge produced is not generalizable in the traditional sense. It does not claim to represent what any researcher would find. It claims something more precise and more honest: this is what became thinkable through this particular assemblage -- this subject, this material, this set of encounters, this configuration of time/space/matter -- and the research artifact produced through it is offered as evidence to other entities capable of perceiving it. The situated cartography is not the packaging around the findings. It is the findings.

What the artist-researcher brings specifically is a transposition framework -- the unique mediation through which the assemblage produces a research artifact that can be shared as evidence with other perceiving entities. She is one actant within the apparatus, not its sovereign. She does not produce the research phenomenon alone; she enters into intra-action with materials, spaces, other bodies, institutional structures, and the accumulated force of prior encounters, all of which participate with equal ontological weight in what gets made. Her situatedness is the particular node through which the apparatus becomes this inquiry, generating this knowledge, for this community of reception. Specificity is not a limitation on generalizability. It is the condition of possibility for any knowledge at all.

Positionality and accountability for one's unique nomadic position is not a methodological add-on. It is constitutive of how artists especially do research and participate in knowledge work. The artist-researcher is always already an embodied transpositional apparatus -- as Rauschenberg was, as Cage was, as Cézanne was -- enacting the ontological ground before and beyond any vocabulary available to name it. What changes with the vocabulary is not the practice but its legibility: to other practitioners, to institutions, to the broader knowledge community.

The distinction between posthumanism and new materialism established across Part One is a theoretical distinction -- it maps different emphases, genealogies, and methodological centers of gravity. What confirms it from the direction of practice is that the protocols researchers have developed under the posthumanist and new materialist umbrella reproduce exactly the division of labor the theoretical account predicts. Lambert (submitted) uses NM concepts -- intra-action, assemblage, spacetimemattering -- to operationalize the PH ethical stance of refusing the humanist reflective practitioner. Hill (2017) frames the three modes of becoming-diffractive in explicitly PH terms -- the decentered subject, the refusal of the individual teacher as autonomous agent -- but operationalizes them through NM conceptual tools: intra-action, becoming-with, assemblage, affect. Martin, Sayers, and Carroll (2025) develop their six transposition practices on the PH side of the distinction -- critique of Man, nomadic subject, affirmative ethics -- but anchor them in a NM ontology of relational becoming. In each case, PH provides the critical-ethical vector that says why the practice matters and what is at stake, and NM provides the material-ontological vector that says what the world is made of and how phenomena are constituted. Neither framework can do the other's work. The practice texts confirm the theoretical distinction not by citing it but by enacting it.

This is where Part One opens onto Part Two. If posthumanism and new materialism together establish that the world is relational, processual, and virtual before it is actual, then the question becomes how to inhabit and work within that world as a researcher and practitioner. Diffractive practice is the response to that question -- and it is the subject of what follows.

A new materialist art practice as research is a generative process of co-creating with material a diffraction grating that produces patterns that would not appear through other methods/frames. The boundaries are the consequences of the grating's design, providing a tool for research accountability for the "constitutive exclusions."(Barad, 2003\_Posthumanist) Each decision is a cut that together produces a locally determinate diffractive pattern.

The insistence on "co-creating with material" is not metaphor. It is an ontological commitment grounded in what Barad calls the mutual entailment of the material and the discursive: "The relationship between the material and the discursive is one of mutual entailment. Neither is articulated/articulable in the absence of the other; matter and meaning are mutually articulated. Neither discursive practices nor material phenomena are ontologically or epistemologically prior. Neither can be explained in terms of the other. Neither has privileged status in determining the other" (Barad, 2003, p. 822). For new materialist art practice as research, this means that the material the artist works with is not a passive medium awaiting the inscription of the artist's idea. It is a co-constitutive force whose specific resistances, affordances, textures, and behaviors shape what becomes thinkable and sayable through the encounter. The diffraction grating is not designed and then executed. It emerges through the intra-action.

The Enchantment of Textiles gives a full-scale case study of this apparatus logic in an interdisciplinary lab setting. It begins with museum research into historic embroidered metalwork --- materials, techniques, cultural meanings --- and then translates those histories into responsive textile systems using sensors, microcontrollers, textile antennas, and embedded circuits. The catalog explicitly frames the lab as a collaborative assemblage of artists, engineers, and researchers, with textiles as performative tools rather than passive media. Agency is distributed explicitly: viewers can "play the sorcerer," but quickly discover that power is in fact distributed and accumulates only through the specific arrangement of surrounding objects, citing Bennett's heterogeneous assemblage claim directly. The textiles are not merely objects to be looked at; they "solicit human perception" while also "perceiv\[ing\] and interact\[ing\] with one another," broadcasting and receiving via antennas and responsive components. Research-creation of this kind, the catalog insists, "cannot be deemed successful in simple or finite terms" and does not yield a singular discovery but instead "opens up new fields of understanding" --- a research outcome to be assessed through unfolding interactions and capacities rather than through closure. The "Critical Methodologies" section of the same catalog makes the research method explicit: sensory ethnography as an experimental iterative-inductive process for studio/lab environments, producing knowledge through embodied sensory engagement and explicitly refusing universality. Neither human nor material agency is privileged; what matters is the temporally emergent influence of ongoing practice. "Tuning" becomes the descriptor for shared adjustment across the research team and the researcher's active role in learning --- goals redefined, decisions shared, embodiments of practice emerging in real time through sensory engagement with material systems.

SymbioticA provides a different inflection of the same apparatus logic, one in which the institutional entanglements of lab-based art practice are not incidental to the work but constitute it. SymbioticA is described as an artistic research laboratory inside a university, where artists work in scientific laboratories with scientific techniques and staff. What counts as the "work" in a SymbioticA project is not only the material outcome but the processes that make it possible: securing permissions from ethics committees, negotiating agreements with lab managers, arranging ongoing care responsibilities. The work consists, then, of these processes --- and it produces a difference to science precisely by following scientific methods while staging that difference as the research contribution. This is a strong formulation of new materialist ethics as infrastructure: responsibility is not a moral sentiment attached to the research after the fact, but a network of material practices --- protocols, caretaking, institutional permissions, risk exposure --- that condition what research can occur and what lives are involved. The political context is not bracketed: the vulnerability of biology-based art practice to state intervention (the Kurtz case) and the framing of contemporary biotechnology institutions as a "society of control" are explicit stakes of the work.

Transposition names the recomposing side of this: not merely reading or seeing patterns, but building the conditions that make new patterns possible. Transposition is the logic of a shift itself — what is shifted becomes a transponent, a hybrid agent of change whose communication is transformative, actualizing latent/virtual affinities and transforming the field it enters. Diffraction and transposition together constitute the full research movement: diffraction traces the interference patterns the apparatus produces; transposition stages the conditions that make new patterns possible. Neither can do the other's work.

#### 4.1 The Ground

Once the figure of Man and the figure of inert matter are both refused, what is left is not nothing “(while not being some thing), but it may in fact be the source of all that is, a womb that births existence” (Barad, 2012, p. 9). It is a world that has been there all along, now reportable because the frames that obscured it have been set down.

The Wild. The void. The absolute. Non-identity. A life. The primordial swerve. Zoe. The virtual. The plane of immanence. These terms do not collapse into one another; each carries the weight of the theoretical tradition from which it comes. They resonate as names for a ground that exists before and after any specific intra-action, outside any particular agential cut, beyond the reach of any apparatus that would make it measurable. It is the field of virtual potential from which phenomena emerge when conditions for their emergence are met, and into which they dissolve when the apparatus that held them in place is released (Bennett, 2010; Braidotti, 2013) (Deleuze, as cited in Cheah, 2010).

In quantum physics, before measurement, before the agential cut that produces a phenomenon, there is superposition: not multiple things coexisting but a state of ontological indeterminacy in which the boundaries and properties of the thing have not yet been enacted. “At the heart of quantum physics is an inherent ontological indeterminacy” (Barad, 2012, p. 7). Barad reads physicist Niels Bohr as having advanced an ontic (ontological) interpretation against the epistemic interpretations: entities “do not determinately exist apart from the particular, physical measuring apparatuses that constitute them one way to the exclusion of others” (Gamble et al., 2019, p. 122).

Barad’s apparatus does not reveal what was already there. It brings forth what was not yet determined. Between measurements, between intra-actions, what exists is this indeterminate field, the real-but-virtual, the condition from which specific configurations of time/space/matter emerge and into which they return (Barad, 2007). Bohr’s intervention, as Barad reads him, was to relocate the problem from uncertainty, understood as a limit on knowledge, to indeterminacy, understood as a feature of the phenomenon itself (Barad, in Dolphijn & van der Tuin, 2012, p. 63). “Space, time, and matter do not exist prior to the intra-actions that reconstitute the entanglements” (Barad, 2007, p. 74). Phenomena, Barad’s primary unit of analysis, “do not merely mark the epistemological inseparability of ‘observer’ and ‘observed’; rather phenomena are the ontological inseparability of intra-acting ‘agencies.’ That is, phenomena are ontological entanglements” (Barad, 2007, p. 333).

Other theorists name this ground from their own location. Deleuze, as Cheah (Cheah, 2010) reports, calls it the transcendental field and the plane of immanence: “the real ‘condition of possibility’ of the actual,” and “made up of pre-individual and impersonal singularities” (pp. 84– 85). Matter on this account is “the unformed, unorganized, nonstratified, or destratified body and all its flows: subatomic and submolecular particles, pure intensities, prevital and prephysical free singularities” (p. 87). The virtual is real: “every real object has a virtual content . . . The virtual must be defined as strictly a part of the real object” (p. 85). Actualization does not unfold a preformed possibility. It determines a virtual field that is already real.

Braidotti calls the force that moves through this ground Zoe: life as a cosmic force that precedes and exceeds any individual subject, any bounded organism, any particular moment of consciousness. The subject is a temporary intensification of this force, not its owner. “This nonanthropocentric view expresses both a profound love for Life as a cosmic force and the desire to depersonalize subjective life-and-death. This is just one life, not my life. The life in ‘me’ does not answer to my name: ‘I’ is just passing” (Braidotti, 2010, p. 210).

Haudenosaunee and Anishinaabe scholar Vanessa Watts names the same ground through Place-Thought: “the non-distinctive space where place and thought were never separated because they never could or can be separated. Place-Thought is based upon the premise that land is alive and thinking and that humans and non-humans derive agency through the extensions of these thoughts” (Watts, cited in Anderson, 2020, p. 211). Zoe Todd argues that Indigenous thinkers deserve recognition for “their millennia of engagement with sentient environments, with cosmologies that enmesh people into complex relationships between themselves and all relations, and with climates and atmospheres as important points of organization and action” (Todd, cited in Anderson, 2020, p. 210). Anderson, following Braidotti, argues that placing different orders of knowledge in dialogue “does not mean creating false equivalences” but instead “aims at amplifying possible resonances between them” (Braidotti, cited in Anderson, 2020, p. 205). Indigenous frameworks and new materialist ontology resonate at important points, and Anderson (Anderson, 2020) reports that Indigenous scholarship has been omitted from standard origin stories of new materialism.

Trinh T. Minh-ha articulated the same ground in her 1989 text, Woman, Native, Other: Writing Postcoloniality and Feminism. Writing from Taoist, Zen, and Vietnamese frames against the Western mirror ontology, in the book section titled “The infinite play of empty mirrors,” she writes that “reality is not reconstituted, it is put into pieces so as to allow another world to rebuild (keep on unbuilding and rebuilding) itself with its debris” (Minh-ha, 1989, p. 23). The mirror is not the symbol of a pre-given vision but, in the Asian traditions she draws on, “the ‘symbol of the very void of symbols’”; “the perfect man’s mind is like a mirror,” Chuang Tzu observed, “it reflects things but does not retain them” (Minh-ha, 1989, p. 23) (citing Chuang Tzu). Trinh’s formulation, written from within artistic practice rather than from the philosophy of matter, is contemporaneous with Haraway’s 1988 “Situated Knowledges” and predates by more than a decade the posthumanist/new materialist vocabulary that would name what she was already articulating. Like Watts’s Place-Thought and Todd’s account of Indigenous engagement with sentient environments, Trinh’s relational ontology is part of the genealogy the standard origin story has tended to omit.

#### 4.4 Affirmative Ethics

If the world is as § 4.1 reports it, and we inhabit it as § 4.2 reports, and we know it from inside as § 4.3 reports, then the question of what to do follows from the question of where we are. Borgdorff, Peters, and Pinch generalize the commitment: “research methods do not only observe and represent materials, issues, and events but in fact act upon and intervene in these materials, issues, and events” (2020, p. 7). For this commitment, I will use Braidotti’s term affirmative ethics as a location to read through.

For Braidotti, the raison d’être of the whole project is affirmative ethics, and its ontological ground is zoe. Because the individual “I” is not the center of life but a temporary passing of it, the logic of retribution, rights-claims, and compensation: all premised on a stable individual subject: dissolves. What replaces it is an “ethics of affirmation” that “suspends the quest for both claims and compensation and resists the logic of retribution or rights” (Braidotti, 2010, p. 214). In Transpositions, she writes that “an ethics of sustainability” structures her argument and that “this transformative ethics includes a critical or reactive and an affirmative or active phase” (Braidotti, 2006, p. 5). She asks how to cultivate “the political desire for change or transformation” and describes this as a change in both “our schemes of thought” and “our ways of inhabiting the world” (Braidotti, 2006, p. 5). In later work she states that “our politics must be guided by an affirmative ethics” and defines ethics as “a discourse about forces and relations, and not as a set of normative moral injunctions” (Braidotti, 2024, p. xiii). Bodily materialism, in this account, produces “a new empirical-discursive methodological approach that combines critique with the creation of alternatives” (Braidotti, 2024, p. xiii). Braidotti defines affirmative ethics directly as “the establishment of mutually empowering relations based on cooperation and the productive combination of the specific degrees of intensity or potentia of each living entity,” which “aims at increasing each entity’s capacity to preserve themselves against adverse forces” (Braidotti, 2022, p. 97). She also names it as “the affect that binds together the heterogeneous components of the complex zoe-geo-techno-subjects” (Braidotti, 2022, p. 97).

Haraway frames the same orientation through response and relation. In Staying with the Trouble, she describes the task as “learning to stay with the trouble of living and dying in response-ability on a damaged earth” (D. J. Haraway, 2016, p. 2). On the opening page, she writes that the task is “to make kin in lines of inventive connection as a practice of learning to live and die well with each other in a thick present” (D. J. Haraway, 2016, p. 1). Haraway names the task as cultivating “the capacity to respond to worldly urgencies with each other” (D. J. Haraway, 2016, p. 7).

Bennett does not use the phrase affirmative ethics in Vibrant Matter, but she explicitly presents her project in ethical and political terms. The political project, she writes, is “to encourage more intelligent and sustainable engagements with vibrant matter and lively things.” She asks how political responses might change if we took seriously “the vitality of (nonhuman) bodies,” defining vitality as the capacity of things not only to impede human designs but also to act “as quasi agents or forces with trajectories, propensities, or tendencies of their own” (Bennett, 2010, p. viii). She also writes that “alongside and inside singular human agents there exists a heterogeneous series of actants with partial, overlapping, and conflicting degrees of power and effectivity” (Bennett, 2010, p. 23). Later in the preface, she describes the ethical task as cultivating “the ability to discern nonhuman vitality” and becoming “perceptually open to it” (Bennett, 2010, p. xiv).

Barad makes ethics a matter of accountability for the cuts that enact inclusion and exclusion (Barad, 2007). “Objectivity,” Barad writes, means “being accountable for marks on bodies, that is, specific materializations in their differential mattering.” Barad continues: “We are responsible for the cuts that we help enact,” and “the cuts that we participate in enacting matter” (Barad, 2007, p. 179). Because “intra-actions cut ‘things’ together and apart,” ethics is not about a radically exterior other, since “‘they’ and ‘we’ are co-constituted and entangled through the very cuts ‘we’ help to enact” (Barad, 2007, p. 179). Agency, in this framework, includes “the possibilities and accountability entailed in reconfiguring material-discursive apparatuses of bodily production, including the boundary articulations and exclusions that are marked by those practices” (Barad, 2007, p. 214). Acknowledgment of “nonhuman agency” does not reduce human responsibility. It requires “that much more attentiveness to existing power asymmetries” (Barad, 2007, p. 219).

The cut is therefore, as Barad puts it, the site at which responsibility and possibility meet: “particular possibilities for acting exist at every moment, and these changing possibilities entail a responsibility to intervene in the world’s becoming, to contest and rework what matters and what is excluded from mattering” (Barad, 2003, pp. 826–827).

Braidotti’s raison d’être, Haraway’s response-ability, Bennett’s perceptual discipline, and Barad’s agential cut resonate across the four accounts of affirmative ethics.

When the studio is understood as a flux condenser and the workshop as a fog chamber, new materialist art practice as research becomes transferable as a pedagogical methodology — not a curriculum but a set of conditions. Franinović (2016) provides the most explicit articulation of what this looks like in practice. Her chapter in *Raw Flows* is a methodological manual for new materialist practice-research: the central research-design problem is how to activate materials and activate people's thoughts and actions "with and through materials," via conditions and constraints in a workshop setting. Her guiding question is: "Can materials be seeds for people... and people seeds for materials?" (Franinović, 2016, p. 129).

The method she develops is seeding: mapping concepts, materials, and techniques onto each other, then choreographing the timing of processes so that material and conceptual processes co-emerge rather than one governing the other. A seed-space is "a set of constraints or conditions permanently open to change and new iterations" (Franinović, 2016, p. 129) — not a predetermined outcome but a set of generative pressures. The goal is not to predict participants' desires or needs but to "flirt with existing behaviors to produce new ones" (Franinović, 2016, p. 129). Knowledge arises after setting the seeds and allowing them time to evolve, through direct and participatory observation that explicitly values often-surprising discoveries as legitimate epistemic outputs.

The core concept emerging from this practice is fluid affordance: with dynamic materials, affordances are unstable and transform as the material's state changes. This instability invites exploration rather than use. Where the affordances of everyday objects tend to constrain imagination through habitual instrumental responses — brainstorming pressure yields "the most obvious and habitual affordance" (Franinović, 2016, p. 140) — active materials destabilize that model in an embodied way, making affordances emergent and open-ended. The question shifts from what is active to how is it active: the research contribution is defined as designing conditions where material processes and conceptual processes co-emerge, generating surprising insights, and producing shareable methods — seeding, choreographing, constraining — rather than only artworks.

This is why new materialist APAR is genuinely pedagogical and not merely expressive. It teaches by creating conditions. It shares by transmitting the structure of a situation rather than the content of a finding. What is learned is not a conclusion but a capacity — for attunement, for noticing, for staying with the encounter long enough to let the material's own logic begin to surface. Andersen describes what this produces in the practitioner: the process of making is a process of changing ourselves, "becoming the person who built that thing" (Andersen, 2016). New thoughts are acted out and created "in a complex collaboration between the object, the situation and a performative moment." The knowledge is not deposited in the object. It is held in the changed relation between the practitioner and the world that the making produced.

A note on how theorists are situated across these three questions. New materialism is a specific movement within the posthumanist theoretical scenario (Ferrando, 2013, p. 30; Ibrisim, 2020, p. 11, citing Ferrando, 2013), not a parallel or competing framework. This means the three questions are not sequenced as three separate fields but as three levels of resolution of the same theoretical and methodological project: from the broad ontological commitments shared across the field (Q1), to their operationalization as diffractive practice (Q2), to their instantiation in art practice as research (Q3). Because new materialism is nested within posthumanism rather than external to it, theorists appear across all three questions. Barad is the central figure of Q2 even though she identifies primarily as a new materialist, because NM is part of the posthumanist theoretical scenario. Braidotti, Haraway, and Bennett appear across all three, because their work operates at every level of resolution simultaneously. Practitioners in the APAR field — Bolt, Borgdorff, Hill, Lambert, Hillary, Andersen — are included under Q3 not because they self-identify as new materialist theorists but because their accounts of art practice as research are grounded in or accountable to NM ontological commitments, whether or not they deploy NM vocabulary. The question structure comes from the committee; the answer recognizes that the field does not partition along those lines, and says so explicitly here rather than negotiating the classification at every turn.

### 2.1 Theory: posthumanism and new materialism

*Pasted from comps final, Part One §§1–2, pp. 7–11*

#### 1. Theory, Methodology, Method

Theory, methodology, and method are situated first at the meta-level through art theorist, educator, historian Graeme Sullivan. According to Sullivan (Sullivan, 2006, p. 22), “research is a transformative act that has an impact on the researcher and the researched.” Sullivan, drawing on Morrow, situates theory as the conceptual framework through which inquiry orients itself. Theories make broader ontological and epistemological claims that drive how research is conducted. Methodology is the principled approach to inquiry that follows from the theory. The research methodology guides decisions on what methods are necessary to accomplish the research goal. Methods are particular procedures developed by particular practitioners in particular sites, and they enact the methodologies. The chosen methods have consequences for what evidence the researcher can collect.

This distinction maps onto the three questions the document answers. Part One treats posthumanism and new materialism as theories in Sullivan’s sense: conceptual frameworks making ontological and epistemological claims about what the world is and how it can be known. Part Two treats diffractive practice, transpositions, and making strange as methodologies: principled approaches to inquiry that follow from those theoretical commitments. Part Three treats specific methods enacted by named artist-researchers whose practices produce knowledge under the conditions Parts One and Two describe.

#### 2. Field Positions

Posthumanism and new materialism are two distinct critical traditions that developed in opposition to the same historical, cultural, and economic conditions: the Cartesian-Newtonian settlement that rendered matter inert, Enlightenment humanism that installed the sovereign rational subject at the center of knowledge and value, the industrial and postindustrial orders that depended on both. They share a non-dualistic, non-representationalist, entangled understanding of nature and culture, a more vitalist understanding of matter, and an empirical field that no longer takes human exceptionalism as a point of departure (Siffrinn & Coda, 2025, p. 191) (citing Coole & Frost, 2010; Bennett, 2010; Braidotti, 2013). Posthumanism focuses on the figure of “Man”: the human universal, the species hierarchy that placed one version of the human above all other forms of life and matter. New materialism focuses on the reduction of the material world to passive substance, available for inscription, extraction, and instrumentalization by an external force. Over time, their unique identities have become more entangled. According to Francesca Ferrando (Ferrando, 2014, p. 169), new materialism has its own origin story unique from posthumanism, but by the end of the 1990s it had emerged as a specific branch of the posthuman field, and the two now function as necessarily twinned frames through which to navigate the world (Militsi, 2021, p. 11) (citing Ferrando, 2013, p. 30). They are emerging continuums of difference, co-constituted, and their combined momentum pushes toward new ethics of what, who, where, when, and why counts as the real, counts as agency, and counts as knowledge.

Ferrando (2019) shows the commitments they share are not recent and traces them back to Aristotle, Plato, and the Old Testament, where the world was separated into hierarchies of the ideal and the profane, and where thinkers who remained with complexity, flux, and porous boundaries were repeatedly absorbed into frameworks that preserved binary order (p. 170). Spinoza’s monism, Whitehead’s process philosophy, and phenomenology all resisted that reduction in different ways. Indigenous knowledge systems long predate the Western academic traditions that later marginalized or omitted them from the standard genealogy (Anderson, 2020; Kontturi & Tiainen, 2024; Pennycook, 2024; Torosyan et al., 2021). These commitments did not first appear in the 1990s. The 1990s mark the beginning of their consolidation into a shared vocabulary (Ferrando, 2013). Haraway marks the threshold clearly: “bounded individualism in its many flavors in science, politics, and philosophy has finally become unavailable to think with, truly no longer thinkable, technically or any other way” (D. J. Haraway, 2016, p. 4).

Philosophically, posthumanism and the new materialisms presuppose that knowing and being are inseparable: “epistemological practices are necessarily ontological practices; to know is to be part of the world’s continual construction” (Siffrinn & Coda, 2025, p. 192) (citing Barad, 2007, p. 379). As Wolfe insists, to become posthuman is to find a mode of thought adequate to “processes which can never be entirely reduced to patterns or standards, codes or information” (Wolfe, 2010, p. xviii) (citing Rutsky). This is the logic Clarke names when quoting Bateson’s suggestion that “the whole of logic would have to be reconstructed for recursiveness” (Wolfe, 2010, p. xviii) (citing Clarke, citing Bateson, 1972): if the world is recursive, relational, and non-deterministic, then method cannot remain representational, as if it were a neutral mirror standing outside what it studies.

Rosi Braidotti’s neo-materialism, grounded in both posthumanism and new materialism, is a way to address the paradox of the simultaneous over-exposure and disappearance of the human. Both theories together create a transversal field of knowledge (Braidotti, 2019). Posthumanism can apply to “any field of enquiry, starting from our location as a species, to the individual gaze” (Ferrando, 2014, p. 168); Wolfe demonstrates this reach across animal studies, disability studies, bioethics, literary and cultural studies, architecture, systems theory, and poetry (Wolfe, 2010). Van der Tuin and Dolphijn (2010) identify new materialism’s strength as “this nomadic traversing of the territories of science and the humanities, performing the agential or non-innocent nature of all matter” (pp. 158–159). Alaimo and Hekman find material feminism operating across “science studies, environmental feminisms, corporeal feminisms, queer theory, disability studies, theories of race and ethnicity, environmental justice, (post-)Marxist feminism, globalization studies, and cultural studies” (Alaimo & Hekman, 2008, pp. 9–10, cited in van der Tuin & Dolphijn, 2012, p. 103). Siffrinn and Coda’s (2025) survey of posthumanist and new materialist research in applied linguistics finds that “these theories are being operationalized by scholars in the field on a larger scale than is being returned in research databases” (p. 194).

Art practice as research is one of the sites in which posthuman and new materialist methodological commitments were enacted. Kontturi and Tiainen argue that contributions from art practice are frequently ignored in the most visible literature, specifically noting Barad’s accounts, when art practice was not simply a later application of new materialist thought but “one of the very milieus in which the call to rethink the involvement of matter in human practices and in the more-than-human world arose” (Kontturi & Tiainen, 2024, p. 259).

#### 2. Differences that make a difference

Diffraction is the mapping of effects of difference rather than the locating of difference in things (Barad, 2003). This distinction, between difference as a property of objects and difference as the pattern produced by their intra-action, is what separates diffractive practice from comparative analysis. Comparison asks: how do these things differ? Diffraction asks: what interference pattern is produced when these things are read through one another, and what does that pattern make visible that neither could produce alone?

Gregory Bateson: anthropologist, cyberneticist, and participant in the Macy Conferences from 1942 onward supplies the methodological anchor for how knowledge is read. Mark Engel, a student of Bateson’s who helped assemble the 1972 collection, identifies in the preface what Bateson’s ontology commits the researcher to. “The central idea in this book is that we create the world that we perceive, not because there is no reality outside our heads … but because we select and edit the reality we see to conform to our beliefs about what sort of world we live in” (Engel, 1971, p. vi). “For a man to change his basic, perception-determining beliefs: what Bateson calls his epistemological premises: he must first become aware that reality is not necessarily as he believes it to be” (Engel, 1971, p. vi).

In the essay “Form, Substance, and Difference,” Bateson distinguishes two worlds of explanation, borrowed from Jung by way of Gnostic vocabulary: the pleroma, “the world in which events are caused by forces and impacts and in which there are no ‘distinctions’”; and the creatura, in which “effects are brought about precisely by difference” (Bateson, 1972, p. 462). Bateson defines information as “this difference which makes a difference” (Bateson, 1972, p. 463); elsewhere he specifies that “a difference which makes a difference is an idea. It is a ‘bit,’ a unit of information” (Bateson, 1972, p. 272).

#### 3. Diffractive Practice

Diffractive practice names a set of methods that reject representational distance and treat inquiry as participation in the production of the phenomenon under study. Across the texts gathered here, method proceeds through situated partiality, non-objectifying relation, and accountability to the apparatus rather than through neutral observation from outside. What matters methodologically is not comparison between fixed terms but the tracing of interference patterns, the specification of cuts, and the maintenance of the intervals through which something new becomes perceptible.

#### 3.1 Trinh, Haraway, and the genealogy of diffraction

Diffraction enters feminist theory through a specific citational chain: from Trinh T. Minh-ha’s writing on difference, through Haraway’s construction of the diffraction metaphor in dialogue with that writing, to Barad’s later ontological formalization. The chain has been largely reported at second and third hand, with Trinh’s contribution appearing through Haraway’s citations and Barad’s footnotes rather than from the primary source. Restoring Trinh to a full accounting changes what the genealogy is a genealogy of.

In Woman, Native, Other (1989), in the section titled “Infinite layers: I am not i can be you and me,” Trinh writes: “A critical difference from myself” means that differences must be “grasped both between and within entities, each of these being understood as multiple presence” (Minh-ha, 1989, p. 94). Identity is composed from infinite layers: “not a unified subject, a fixed identity, or that solid mass covered with layers of superficialities one has gradually to peel off before one can see its true face” (p. 94). Identity displays a “necessary ambivalence” (p.94), always fleeing static essence, defying all attempts to contain categories that leak. Trinh’s filmmaking and writing from the 1980s onward develop this ontology in specific methodological form: speaking nearby, the interval, the refusal of the transparent observer position \[Minh-ha (1989); 1990; 1992\]. Part Three § 2 treats her practice as artistic research in depth.

Haraway introduces diffraction as a feminist optical metaphor in 1992, explicitly taking up Trinh’s formulation. In “The Promises of Monsters,” Haraway extends Trinh’s “critical difference within” into a methodological claim: “Diffraction does not produce”the same” displaced, as reflection and refraction do. Diffraction is a mapping of interference, not of replication, reflection, or reproduction. A diffraction pattern does not map where differences appear, but rather maps where the effects of difference appear” (D. Haraway, 1992, pp. 299– 300). This is the origin of the diffraction metaphor in feminist theory: generated from a reading of Trinh, not from quantum physics alone. By 1997, in Modest\_Witness, Haraway develops diffraction into what Barad later calls a “fourth semiotic category,” connecting it to her broader critique of reflexivity: “reflexivity, like reflection, only displaces the same elsewhere,” whereas diffraction produces genuinely new interference patterns (D. J. Haraway, 1997, p. 16). Haraway’s version of diffractive practice is inseparable from her commitment to figuration: representations that are “performative images that can be inhabited,” partial maps accountable to their situatedness (D. J. Haraway, 1997, p. 11). In her 2003 essay, Barad cites this chain explicitly, naming Haraway’s reading of Trinh as the source from which diffraction moves into material-discursive theory (Barad, 2003, pp. 804 n.3) (citing Haraway, 1992, p. 300).

The chain matters because it places an artist-practitioner/anthropologist at the root of a concept typically attributed to science studies. Diffraction as feminist method does not begin from physics; it begins from Trinh’s insistence on critical difference within, reworked by Haraway into an optical metaphor for situated knowledge, and is then grounded by Barad in quantum field theory. Restoring Trinh as source rather than citation repositions the genealogy: diffractive practice is not physics applied to feminist theory but feminist practice given ontological weight after the fact.

#### 3.2 Barad

For Barad, diffractive practice is inseparable from the apparatus. The apparatus is not a tool external to the researcher. It is the material-discursive arrangement: the configuration of instruments, concepts, bodies, institutions, and practices: through which phenomena are produced. The researcher is always already part of the apparatus; there is no view from outside. Diffractive practice, in Barad’s version, means making the apparatus clear: naming it, specifying the agential cuts it makes, and tracing the marks it leaves on bodies. The agential cut is not a free interpretive choice. It is the determinate event through which boundaries are enacted, relata emerge, and accountability is located. Diffractive practice is rigorous precisely because it specifies these cuts rather than naturalizing them. The question it asks is not “what does this mean” but “what does this configuration of differences produce, and what does it foreclose.” Its ethical force comes from the insistence that exclusions are constitutive: what the apparatus does not produce is as significant as what it does, and the researcher is response-able for those constitutive exclusions \[Barad (2003); 2007\].

Barad then takes Haraway’s diffraction metaphor and grounds it in quantum field theory, making the metaphor into an ontological claim: phenomena do not pre-exist the apparatuses that produce them, and diffraction is not only a way of reading but a description of how the world is constituted (Barad, 2003) (citing Haraway, 1992, p. 300).

#### 3.3 Diffractive Practice in Education

The five educators gathered here work across levels and contexts: from early years practice to teacher education to undergraduate curriculum: and each one attaches diffractive practice to specific educational acts. Taken together they show diffractive practice not as a fixed procedure but as a commitment to materially situated inquiry, multiple forms of attunement, and knowledge-making through entangled practice.

Cher Hill, writing in teacher education at Simon Fraser University, gives the clearest methodological distinction. Her aim is not to replace reflective practice with diffractive practice, but to move “beyond reflective practice” by distinguishing “diffractive reading” from “experimenting with a diffractive apparatus” (Hill, 2017, pp. 5–6). Diffractive reading usually happens after the event, while experimenting with a diffractive apparatus happens within the event as an “onto-epistemological material practice” that helps “configure the world in particular ways” (p. 6). Hill names three interrelated ways of becoming diffractive: “becoming-with the world,” “displacing and diffracting the selves who teach,” and “embracing difference and interference” (pp. 6–11). Hill illustrates becoming-with through a beach assignment in which teachers collecting materials for a weaving project encounter a wounded bird. The event is an assemblage composed of “the assignment, the wounded bird, the hot sunny day, the cell phone, and the instructor’s lack of intervention,” (p. 8) which together produced “enacting care in the face of hopelessness.” (p. 8) She illustrates displacing and diffracting the selves who teach through the “interview to the double” (p. 9) method: a teacher’s account of meeting with a struggling student materializes several teacher-identities at once: “the compassionate teacher,” “the professional teacher,” “teacher as gatekeeper,” “the litigious teacher,” and “teacher as empath.” (p.10) She illustrates embracing difference and interference through a family quarrel read through two different theories: self-regulation theory and new materiality theory: to show that diffractive practice produces “new phenomena and new subjectivities” (p.11) through interference rather than fit.

Louise Lambert, working in initial teacher education at Birmingham City University, defines diffraction as an “otherwise” practice. She writes against forms of reflection that have become “a technical task orientated towards summative assessments of professional competency” and “a predominantly cognitive and linear practice” (Lambert, 2021, p. 2). Against that model, she proposes “diffraction as an ‘otherwise’ means” for exploring teaching as “non-linear and materially and affectively entangled through time and space” (p. 1), and states that diffraction is “not an ‘either/or’ alternative but an ‘otherwise’, expanded way of thinking” (p. 3). The named concepts through which she develops that practice are affect, intra-action, assemblage, becoming-with, and spacetimemattering. Her fullest example is the participant “M,” whose collage centers on her attempt to run a Black Lives Matter club and her later move to running a choir. Lambert shows assemblage by naming the heterogeneous elements in the scene: mentor, policy, curriculum silences, local racial history, music, administration, children, affects, and M’s own body. She shows spacetimemattering when historical and contemporary racial discourses come to matter in the present encounter, including the local history of Enoch Powell’s “rivers of blood” speech and the wider resonance of Black Lives Matter. The collage itself materializes affect and overload through images Lambert describes as a “disintegrating body, eyeballs rolling across the craft paper,” and an “octopus whose legs perform multiple functions of administration and teaching tasks.” (p. 25)

Kate Dudley, writing from early childhood studies and early years professional practice, presents diffractive practice through Mosaic listening. She describes the Mosaic Approach as “multi-contextual listening” and names “photo-elicitation, digital cameras, and observations of play” as the techniques through which children are given “diverse avenues to express themselves naturally” (Dudley, 2025). Dudley names this approach “Mosaic listening (Clark & Moss, 2001)” and states that it “centres children’s voices, ensuring they are heard and respected” (Dudley, 2025). She grounds the study in “ethico-onto-epistemology”: the intertwining of ethics, knowing, and being in every intra-action: and turns immediately to “nomadic ethics,” which she defines as “an ongoing responsibility, requiring flexibility and responsiveness in practice” attentive to “a constant state of becoming” (Dudley, 2025). She specifies what this looks like in practice through examples from the study: a child using painted sticks while talking about “complex friendships and family,” children “making a home fit for a King,” and a “chatterbox session” where children bring a favorite toy. Her summary formulation is that “diffractive practice: a way of seeing through multiple lenses: is a powerful tool” for practitioners working with children’s gender expression and identity (Dudley, 2025).

Carol Taylor, is writing from the location of a Professor of Gender and Higher Education describing undergraduate BA Education Studies module, places diffraction in curriculum design. Her chapter uses Barad’s notion of diffraction to show how “curriculum-making can be done via a patterned activity of creative interference” (Taylor, 2019, p. 37) and grounds that claim in the module Educational Spaces: Theories and Perspectives (pp. 42–43). She illustrates diffraction first through the design of the module itself. The course runs for twelve weeks, but the order of the themed teaching weeks is not fixed in advance. Students are given the themes and readings, and participate in sequencing the module. They “participate in materializing the (their) curriculum through an act of collaborative co-creation” (p. 50) and must “live with” the curriculum they have helped make, which means they experience their own “cuts” as productive of matter and meaning and as entailing “responsibility and accountability” to themselves and each other (p. 45). Taylor illustrates innovative teaching and learning practices by having students inventory the classroom matter itself: “desks, carpet, lectern, rubbish, dust, lights, pens, iPads, whiteboard etc.” (p. 45). They then ask questions such as “why is that there?” and “who decided that should go there?” (p. 45). In another task, the class sits in silence for two minutes and attends to “the lights hum, the feet pass outside, the traffic in the distance, and to our own breathing,” along with “heat, atmosphere, aromas and light” (p. 46).

Sofie Sauzet, reporting through work with students in a professional bachelor’s programme in social education, names her method “thinking through picturing.” She wanted to move students from “learning about practices” to “producing situated knowledges in and through practices” (Sauzet, 2015, p. 42). She names “a diffraction apparatus” (p. 39) and describes the specific method she chose: “snaplogs,” defined as “a visual, ethnographic method that involves taking pictures (snap) in response to specific questions, and writing small corresponding texts (logs)” (p. 41). The snaplog assignment required students to conduct ethnographic fieldwork in practicum settings, take at least five pictures of something that went “across and beyond” their existing conception of professionalism, and answer for each image what is shown, why they took the picture, and how it relates to interprofessional practices (p. 42). In Carla’s snaplog, the image shows “M” working at a local DIY center. The interview expands the scene to include sweeping the parking lot, wearing yellow safety vests, moving shopping carts, using the coin mechanism, and M’s pride in saying, “Hi I work at the DIY center!” (pp. 45–46). Sauzet reads the photograph, student, activity center, DIY center, parking lot, vests, shopping carts, and feeling of usefulness as entangled in the production of interprofessionalism (pp. 46–47). In Johanne’s snaplog, the image is a bathroom with a hoist at the youth club where she is interning (p. 47). Johanne’s concern is not only what the hoist is, but what professional knowledge is needed to use it safely over time and who takes responsibility for that expertise (pp. 47–48). Sauzet says this case does not only show what interprofessionalism is, but also what it “might be” (p. 49). The snaplog slows practice down and makes different cuts possible (p. 49).

#### 3.4 Refusal of exhaustive capture as method

Diffractive practice does not aim to produce a total account of its object. It aims to produce a situated, accountable account that remains open to further intra-action. Braidotti describes cartography as an embodied, embedded, grounded account that is relational and affective (Braidotti, 2024). Trinh names the interval and speaking nearby as the twin commitments that hold the account open rather than closing it around the object \[Minh-ha (1990), p. 77; Chen (1992), p. 86; see Part Three § 2 for the fuller treatment\]. Barad names every method as boundary-making: agential cuts are material events that enact what becomes determinate and what is foreclosed, and the researcher is responsible for them (Barad, 2007). Braidotti’s cartography, Trinh’s interval, and Barad’s agential cut resonate across the three accounts as commitments to generativity rather than exhaustive capture.

#### 4.1 Braidotti

Braidotti’s transpositions provide both the method for movement and frame that mediates the move. Transposition is “a scientific term used in biology and musicology” that she adopts as “a metaphor for this specific way of working” (Braidotti, 2006, p. 5). It “indicates an intertextual, cross-boundary or transversal transfer, in the sense of a leap from one code, field or axis into another, not merely in the quantitative mode of plural multiplications, but rather in the qualitative sense of complex multiplicities” (Braidotti, 2006, p. 5). The move from genetic code to music is not analogical; it is the method itself: a “creative leap” that produces new relations by carrying content across registers that were not previously connected. Transposition “is not just a matter of mere translation, but a creative act” that produces “something new” through the passage (Braidotti, 2006, p. 6). Braidotti’s cartography extends this: the researcher is a cartographer whose accounts are embodied, embedded, and grounded in specific locations and histories (Braidotti, 2024).

#### 5. Making Strange

Making strange has a specific theoretical genealogy that runs through literary theory, theater, and into new materialist art practice. Viktor Shklovsky, 1917, (as cited in Andersen 2012) introduced ostranenie: defamiliarization: as the defining operation of art: art exists to restore the experience of life, to make us feel things rather than merely recognize them. The technique of art is to make objects unfamiliar, to make forms difficult, to increase the length and difficulty of perception. Bertolt Brecht translated this into theatrical practice as the Verfremdungseffekt, the estrangement effect, a set of techniques for preventing the audience from losing itself in the illusion of the performance and forcing critical distance rather than identification. For Brecht, estrangement is not a failure of art but its most powerful political tool: to make the familiar strange is to reveal that it could be otherwise, that what appears natural and necessary is in fact historical and contingent.

New materialist art practice inherits and extends this tradition and shifts its target. Where Shklovsky and Brecht use making strange to defamiliarize human social relations and cultural forms, new materialist making strange extends the operation to the nonhuman. Making strange clears the trained perceptual habits: Bennett’s “already-always-perceiving of objects”: that suppress the vitality, the thing-power, the ongoing becoming of matter (Bennett, 2010). Schwab’s multistable-image logic extends this further: making strange becomes an epistemic operator. Representation can be treated as a breakdown that collapses multiple potentials; artistic research operates in the intertwinedness of knowledge and non-knowledge, emphasizing the “active state,” operations, rather than result (Schwab, 2018).

Kristina Andersen writes that “Making strange or defamiliarisation is the artistic technique of forcing the audience to see common things in an unfamiliar or strange way in order to enhance perception of the familiar (Shklovsky 1917),” and identifies the technique as:

> a central concept to both Surrealism and Dada, centered on the idea that the act of experiencing something occurs in the moment of perception and that the further you confuse or otherwise prolong that moment of arriving at an understanding of an object of art, the deeper or more detailed that understanding will be (Andersen, 2012, p. 3)

In the 2016 chapter, she restates the principle as the operational core of her own practice: “Hypothetical making facilitates the creation of different kinds of objects and interpretations. The actual process of investigating these magic machines leans directly on the technique of making strange (or othering)” (Andersen, 2016, p. 46). She adds, “In a world that already feels overwhelmingly unfamiliar, making things stranger somehow allows me to inhabit that strangeness, as if it was mine” (Andersen, 2016, p. 47).

### 2.3 The field: art practice as research

*Pasted from comps final, Part Three §1, pp. 59–66*

#### 1. What This Question Asks

Part Three asks a narrower question than the sections that came before it: how is new materialist art practice research? The answer does not lie in adding theory to art practice after the fact, or in treating artworks as illustrations of claims produced elsewhere. It lies in practices that enact the ontological and methodological commitments established in Parts One and Two: material participation, distributed agency, situated apparatuses, non-representational engagement, and inquiry conducted in and through entangled practice rather than detached observation of pre-existing objects.

#### 1.1 What is research?

Taken together, the accounts gathered here define research as original inquiry directed toward knowledge and understanding, and as a practice that may take different forms across different fields. Henk Borgdorff defines research as “original investigation undertaken in order to gain knowledge and understanding” (Borgdorff, 2012, p. 87), and cites the Dublin Descriptors: the European higher education qualifications framework developed through the Bologna Process: which understand research “in an inclusive way,” not in a “limited or restricted sense” tied only to a narrow scientific model (Borgdorff, 2012, p. 87). Graeme Sullivan extends the same principle by writing that “an agreed goal of research is the creation of new knowledge” and that this goal “can be achieved by following different, yet complementary pathways” (Sullivan, 2006, pp. 19–20). Christopher Frayling distinguishes research with a small r, “the act of searching closely or carefully,” from research with a big R, “work directed towards the innovation, introduction, and improvement of products and processes” (Frayling, 1993, p. 1); he then collapses the hierarchy the capitalization implies by stating that “research is a practice, writing is a practice, doing science is a practice, doing design is a practice, making art is a practice” (Frayling, 1993, p. 4). Across these accounts, research is defined by its orientation toward new knowledge and understanding rather than by the specific pathway it takes, and making art is situated within the same definitional field as writing, science, and design.

#### 1.2 What is art practice as research?

Within this broader definition, art practice as research names a specific mode of inquiry in which practice is central rather than supplementary. Sullivan writes that “art practice is a creative and critical form of human engagement that can be conceptualized as research” (Sullivan, 2006, p. 19), that “human understanding arises from a process of inquiry that involves creative action and critical reflection” when “art practice is theorized as research” (Sullivan, 2006, p. 28), and that “art practice involves giving form to thought in a purposeful way that embodies meaning” (Sullivan, 2006, p. 29). Borgdorff converges on the same claim from the side of the research: in artistic research, “artistic practice is central to the research itself,” and “the research takes place in and through the artist’s creative and performative actions” (Borgdorff, 2012, p. 121). Art practice qualifies as research, he adds, “if its purpose is to expand our knowledge and understanding by conducting an original investigation in and through art objects and creative processes” (Borgdorff, 2012, p. 160), and seeks “in and through the production of art” to contribute to what we know and understand (Borgdorff, 2012, p. 161). Frayling’s three-part distinction: Research into, through, and for art and design: specifies what this looks like at the level of method: “Research through” includes “materials research,” “development work,” and “action research,” and “Research for” names work “where the end product is an artefact” and where “the thinking is, so to speak, embodied in the artefact” (Frayling, 1993, p. 5). Paulo de Assis and Lucia D’Errico extend the point by describing artistic research as “a particular mode of artistic practice and of knowledge production, in which scholarly research and artistic activity become inextricably intertwined,” and as “practice-based, practice-led, and practice-driven,” “primarily conducted by practitioners” (Assis & D’Errico, 2019, p. 3). Christensen-Scheel, Aure, and Bergaust confirm the heterogeneity of the field: “the field of artistic research is constituted not by one art-based or artistic research mode, but is nourished by several theoretical, scientific, and artistic approaches,” and current work moves toward “methodological crossovers” and “multimethod development that is not based on polarised dichotomies” (Christensen-Scheel et al., 2022, p. 2).

Esa Kirkkopelto names the condition under which art practice becomes research: the artistic researcher’s medium is converted into a medium of research, and the transition “remains suspended between two stages; it can only suggest the transition by repeating it endlessly but always anew” (Kirkkopelto, 2015, p. 49). That conversion transforms the artist into “the primary expert of the medium that she herself has created” (Kirkkopelto, 2015, p. 49), responsible not only for the work but for making its consequences traceable and communicable. Refusing exhaustive capture is constitutive of this suspension, because artistic research is the form of inquiry in which the medium’s conversion into a medium of research is itself ongoing, and in which what counts as completion is not the finality of a claim but the traceability of what the practice has made possible.

#### 1.3 What counts as data, as evidence, and as knowable in artistic research?

Once research is understood as inquiry directed toward new knowledge and art practice is understood as one of its legitimate modes, a further question follows: what counts as data, as evidence, and as knowable in artistic research? The accounts gathered below approach this question from different angles, but they converge on a shared answer: data in artistic research is not given in advance, and evidence is not a stable object extracted from a world that preexists the inquiry. Both are produced in and through the practice that investigates them.

Georgios Kaounas provides a framework of artistic research as an “epistemic culture,” he adopts Karin Knorr Cetina’s definition of epistemic cultures as “those… arrangements… which, in a given area… make up how we know what we know” (Kaounas, 2024, p. 9). An epistemic culture consists of three interlocking “machineries of knowing”: epistemic subjects, epistemic objects, and epistemic practices (Kaounas, 2024, p. 10). In Cetina’s original account, the machinery of epistemic subjects names the group of people producing knowledge within an epistemic culture and the social relations organizing their production; the machinery of epistemic objects names the collection of things members of the culture interact with to produce knowledge; and the machinery of epistemic practices names the set of patterns of action established within the culture (Kaounas, 2024, p. 10). Kaounas adapts Cetina with a new materialist lens where subject and object are collapsed, where practice is “constitutive of epistemic subjects and objects alike” (Kaounas, 2024, p. 10): which is to say that the practices do not presuppose the subjects and objects they engage but participate in making them what they are. Kaounas approaches artistic research “as an epistemic culture… examining how my participants produce knowledge in order to understand how they make sense of artistic creation as a form of research” (Kaounas, 2024, p. 21).

Kaounas identifies three practices through which artistic creation produces knowledge: the production of tacit knowledge, the circularity of artistic research, and the experimentation of creating art (Kaounas, 2024, p. 25). On tacit knowledge, he draws on his participants’ own accounts: there is “a form of knowledge that’s not shareable without the experience,” and “the knowledge produced in artistic research can only be understood through the artistic practice producing it” (Kaounas, 2024, pp. 25, 28). On circularity, he writes that “the knowledge production carried out by my participants not only takes place through artistic creation, but also results in artistic creation, ending up at the same point where it starts and enriching the process of creating art” (Kaounas, 2024, p. 28). On experimentation, the studio becomes “a space where the artistic researcher experiments on their artistic practice,” and “knowledge production in artistic research is an experimentational process” (Kaounas, 2024, p. 28). These three practices provide a specific metric for how creating art produces knowledge within the epistemic culture of artistic research.

Kaounas’s “epistemic objects,” plural and machinery-level, are not to be confused with Rheinberger’s singular “epistemic thing”: the underdetermined object that drives a particular experimental system and must be distinguished from the stable “technical objects” that create working conditions around it \[Rheinberger (2010); see Part Two § 6.5\]. The distinction is not a contradiction but a scale difference: Rheinberger names the unknown that each specific inquiry pursues; Kaounas names the collection of things an entire epistemic culture engages with. Both hold that the object of research is constituted through the research rather than given to it, and both resist the separation of inquiry from what inquiry produces.

David Pirrò develops the notion of data through what he calls the facta argument. Data, he writes, “is not simply ‘given’” but “might as well have a double nature of ‘facta,’ created, produced, and emerging from the interactions between all the technological and theoretical tools that scientists employ” (Pirrò, 2018, p. 145). Data has a double nature as facta because it is simultaneously the output of a material process and the expression of the theoretical and instrumental framework that produced it ((Pirrò, 2018)).

Data is transposed. “Transposing a data set means recognising and acknowledging its complexity. It means recovering its context and the net of interactions in it” (Pirrò, 2018, p. 145). On data, he writes that “data is not simply ‘given’” but “might as well have a double nature of ‘facta,’ created, produced, and emerging from the interactions between all the technological and theoretical tools that scientists employ” (Pirrò, 2018, p. 145). Under this formulation, evidence in artistic research is not a stable object extracted from the studio but the trace of the specific arrangement through which the studio’s inquiry was conducted, and the work of the researcher is to maintain the complexity of that trace rather than reduce it to a propositional claim.

From a practitioner’s location, Margaret Somerville’s account of “data that glow” grounds the preceding claims in the everyday phenomenology of the research site. Certain pieces of material, in her case, certain videos from a large corpus, “continued to stand out from the very large data-set and grab \[her\] attention” (Somerville, 2016, p. 1165). These data glow precisely because they resist sense-making and “can be seen as agentic, choosing \[their\] multiple material participants in the event of place” (Somerville, 2016, p. 1170) (citing MacLure reading Deleuze; see Part Two § 6.3). What Kaounas describes at the machinery level, what Pirrò describes at the level of data as facta, and what Bolt describes at the level of performative materialisation, Somerville describes at the level of what the researcher actually notices when the research is underway: the material that commands attention because it exceeds the existing analytic frame.

#### 1.4 What is new materialist art practice as research?

New materialist art practice as research retains Borgdorff’s and Sullivan’s claims about art practice as research and places them under conditions in which matter is active, apparatuses are constitutive, and knowledge emerges through specific material-discursive arrangements rather than through detached observation of pre-existing objects. The artist-researcher is a horizontal actant within the apparatus, sharing distributed agency with the materials, instruments, bodies, and institutions that together constitute the research phenomenon (Barad, 2007; Bennett, 2010). Through practice, the artist-researcher’s transpositional framework produces the research artifact as evidence for other perceiving entities.

For the purposes of this section, three related operations organize what new materialist APAR does. Apparatus names the material-discursive arrangement through which phenomena become knowable; transposition names the operative movement by which an arrangement is assembled, entered, and put to work, the creative leap across registers that produces new material to diffract; and diffraction names the condition of a relational reality encountered through interference rather than reflection, the reading of the patterns the apparatus produces (Barad, 2007; Braidotti, 2018, p. 31; Kirkkopelto, 2018, pp. 37–38; Schwab, 2018, pp. 7–9). Apparatus, transposition, and diffraction are not a sequence but a triad: each names something the practice does that the others do not, and each is constitutive of the research condition under which the work becomes legible as knowledge production.

A related claim running through Parts One and Two is that the researcher or practitioner is not external to the process of inquiry. What matters is not the elimination of the researcher’s position but accountability to the cut: objectivity is not secured by pretending to stand outside the phenomenon but by specifying the arrangement through which the knowledge emerged and the exclusions that arrangement enacted. Across the sections that follow, new materialist art practice functions as research through recurring commitments: refusal of exhaustive capture, knowing in the doing, expanded accounts of what counts as knowledge and what counts as evidence, non-representational engagement with the material itself, and processes that remain generative rather than closing too quickly into conclusion.

Critically, this vocabulary is not describing a new condition. It is naming what has always been operating. The Dada artists, Rauschenberg, Cage, Cézanne -- they did not anticipate posthumanism. Reality is an ongoing becoming. Matter is agentive, relational, and generative — not a passive substance awaiting inscription by an external force. The world is not composed of discrete objects standing in external relations but of entangled phenomena, intra-acting assemblages, folds. Knowledge is not produced by a detached observer standing outside what it studies: it is situated, embodied, produced through the body of the knower and through the specific material-discursive apparatus of the inquiry. The subject is not a bounded individual but a nomadic, non-unitary, multiply-constituted process of becoming — "not one but many" (Ferrando, 2014, p. 169), or as Bennett would say, "walking talking minerals" (Vibrant Matter) — "we have never been human" because "human is a 'human' concept" (Ferrando, 2014, p. 170). The ground of knowledge is relationship. The unit of analysis is phenomenon, not object. Representation is not a mirror of a pre-given world but a cut that produces what it appears merely to describe. These are the affirmations posthumanism and new materialism share. They are not new. They have been made before — in philosophy, in science, in art, in feminist practice, in Indigenous ways of knowing that long predate the Western academic traditions that have most systematically suppressed them. What is new is the moment when enough of these traditions found common vocabulary at once to name what they were doing, to make it speakable as method. That moment was the 1990s. But the conditions for it accumulated across millennia — not as a linear progression toward a predetermined destination, but as a folding, a series of intra-actions in which the same ontological commitment kept resurfacing in different registers, kept being covered over, and kept breaking through.

The problem these frameworks respond to is as old as Leucippus and Democritus: the atomist claim that reality consists of tiny, indivisible, passive particles careening through the void, their shapes and sizes fixed and eternal, their combinations determined by random collision. Ancient atomism gave Western metaphysics its picture of matter as fundamentally inert — moving only when struck, generating only what its pre-given characteristics permit, never surprising itself. Epicurus introduced the clinamen, the swerve, as a small concession to spontaneity: atoms occasionally deviate from their predetermined paths, triggering cascades of changed outcomes. But the deviation is still individual and random, not relational and generative. The full range of cosmic possibilities remains fixed. Lucretius extended Epicurus into the philosophical poem *De Rerum Natura* and came closer — Gamble, Hanan, and Nail argue that Lucretius anticipated performative new materialism, that he studied matter as something that ceaselessly constitutes novel entities and relations (Gamble et al., 2019, pp. 112–113). But this reading was suppressed for most of Western intellectual history, both by the mechanist tradition that followed and by the Christian theology that preceded it, which needed matter to be dead so that God could be its animator.

The modern consolidation of that suppression runs through Bacon, Descartes, and Hobbes. Bacon synthesized theology, naturalism, and mechanism into a single framework: God imparts force to matter, sets the laws of nature in motion, and then rests. "The force implanted by God in these first particles makes up all the variety of things" (Bacon, cited in Gamble et al., 2019, p. 115). Matter is what is moved. Force is what moves it. The observer is what understands both. Descartes sharpened this into a hard dualism: mind and matter are categorically different substances, and the mind examines the material world from a position of categorical separation from it — the detached Cartesian observer for whom Galileo's famous formulation had already named the method: nature speaks the language of mathematics, and the human mind is uniquely positioned to read it. Kant later secured this structure epistemologically: we only ever have access to the correlation between thinking and being, never to either term considered apart from the other. The human subject is the transcendental condition of knowledge. Van der Tuin and Dolphijn name the consequence precisely: "Kant started his philosophy with the human being — or even better, with human thought and its relation to nature. In other words, Kant turned (theological) metaphysics into transcendental anthropology" (van der Tuin & Dolphijn, 2012, p. 165). The anthropological turn was a humanist revolution. Against this tradition — not in direct opposition to it but as an alternative that kept being marginalized — Spinoza had already made the counter-claim in 1677: the mind is the idea of the body. Mind and body are the same thing. God equals nature. What Spinoza calls conatus — the striving of all singular things to persevere in their being — is not something added to matter from outside but immanent to it, the power of all material things to exist and act. "Bodily motions alone" is the starting point (Spinoza, \[1677\] 2001, E2P49 Schol., cited in van der Tuin & Dolphijn, 2012). Deleuze later names Spinoza's project "a philosophy of the body" and identifies him as the founding figure of the monist minor tradition — the tradition Deleuze spent his career recovering, tracing through Leibniz, Nietzsche, Bergson, Whitehead, and Lucretius (van der Tuin & Dolphijn, 2012, pp. 95–96). This minor tradition never disappeared. It was, in van der Tuin and Dolphijn's phrase, "freeing itself from the Platonist, Christian, and Modernist rule under which it suffered for so long" (van der Tuin & Dolphijn, 2012, p. 95). New materialism is what that freeing looks like when it becomes speakable as method.

Bergson extended the Spinozist commitment into a philosophy of duration. Matter, for Bergson, is not substance but "the aggregate of images" — and perception of matter is "these same images referred to the eventual action of one particular image, my body" (\[1896\] 2004, p. 8, cited in van der Tuin & Dolphijn, 2012). This is matter given in relation to a body that is itself material, not from a detached point of view. What endures is not Platonic essence but what "diverges and transforms itself with the passage of time" (Grosz, 2005, p. 110, cited in van der Tuin & Dolphijn, 2010, p. 164). Duration inserted into matter: this is the first articulation of what NM will call the ongoing iterative process, the generative indeterminacy that is not random but pedetic. Merleau-Ponty extended it again: the body is itself a fold, the lived experience of Leibnizean folded ontology — not binary opposition between two fixed terms but a reversibility that always miscarries at the last moment (Coole, 2010, pp. 110–111). His working notes explicitly planned to substitute being-in-the-world for Leibniz's God, "only ridding them of their substantialist and onto-theological elaborations in order to apply them to wild Being" (cited in Coole, 2010, p. 110). The body does not perceive the world from a detached point of view. It does not inhabit a flat, geometrical, fully determinate plane but a milieu — an Umwelt that remains ambiguous, indeterminate, resonant with expressive significance. Cézanne tried to paint this: the object "in the act of appearing, organizing itself before our eyes" (Coole, 2010, p. 104). Merleau-Ponty reads Cézanne's refusal of Renaissance perspective not as an aesthetic choice but as a phenomenological position — the attempt to suspend cultural habit long enough to see the material world as it actually materializes in perception.

The postwar moment introduced a different kind of disruption to the Cartesian observer — not from phenomenology but from physics. The Macy Conferences on cybernetics (1946–1953) gathered Bateson, McCulloch, Wiener, von Neumann, and others around a new theoretical model for biological, mechanical, and communicational processes that removed Homo sapiens from any particularly privileged position in relation to meaning, information, and cognition (Wolfe, 2010, p. xii). Bateson's reformulation was characteristically precise: information is "a difference that makes a difference" (1972, p. 459) — a relational definition that makes no reference to a subject who receives it. And Bateson followed his own logic: "the whole of logic would have to be reconstructed for recursiveness" (cited in Wolfe, 2010, p. xviii). The logic of the closed system — input, processing, output, observer standing outside — was inadequate to the phenomena systems theory was studying. In quantum physics, Bohr had arrived at the same conclusion from a different direction. Heisenberg's uncertainty principle held that measurement disturbs the particle — a limit on knowledge. Bohr argued this was an epistemic error: the issue is not uncertainty but indeterminacy. "Things are indeterminate; there are no things before the measurement, and the very act of measurement produces determinate boundaries and properties of things" (Barad, in Dolphijn & van der Tuin, 2012, p. 63). Observation is not disturbance of a pre-existing state. It is constitution of the phenomenon. This is not a claim about human psychology or perceptual limitation. It is an ontological claim about the nature of reality.

Feminist science studies arrived at the same claim through a different route. The route was the body. Adrienne Rich named the methodological principle directly in 1984: "Begin not with a continent or a country or a house, but with the geography closest in — the body" (*Blood, Bread, and Poetry*, p. 212). Braidotti names this "the cornerstone of the neo-materialist feminist project" (Braidotti, *New Materialism*, p. 22), tracing a direct line from Rich's politics of location through Haraway's situated knowledges into the standpoint debates that shaped NM's early formation. Haraway radicalized the epistemological stakes in "Situated Knowledges" (1988): the "god trick" — the view from nowhere, the claim to disembodied universal perspective — is not a neutral description of scientific method but a specific exercise of power, and the only honest alternative is not relativism but a stronger objectivity, one that is partial, embodied, and accountable to its own situatedness. "I am arguing for politics and epistemologies of location, positioning, and situating, where partiality and not universality is the condition of being heard to make rational knowledge claims" (Haraway, 1988, p. 589). This is Merleau-Ponty's Umwelt made explicitly political. It is Bergson's aggregate of images given a standpoint. It is Spinoza's body given a feminist address.

Trinh T. Minh-ha made the same commitment in film practice, working from a hybrid site of Vietnamese, African, and American crossings. In "Documentary Is/Not a Name" (1990), she names the condition that makes non-totalizing knowledge possible: "meaning should be prevented from coming to closure at either what is said or what is shown," and what persists between meaning and truth is "the interval, a break without which meaning would be fixed and truth congealed" (Trinh, 1990, p. 77). The interval is not a methodological deficiency. It is the condition of possibility for a knowledge that does not replicate the epistemic violence of the view from nowhere. Two years later, in "The Promises of Monsters" (1992), Haraway introduced diffraction as a critical method in direct engagement with Trinh's formulation of "critical difference within" (Haraway, 1992, pp. 299–300). The genealogy of diffraction begins with a filmmaker, not a physicist.

Barad's *Meeting the Universe Halfway* (2007) is where these threads — the ontological, the epistemological, the feminist, the phenomenological, the quantum — become explicitly and systematically braided into method. Agential realism names what this entire genealogy was already doing: the refusal to accept that entities exist prior to the intra-actions that constitute them, the insistence that knowing and being are not separable, the recognition that each cut that produces determinate phenomena simultaneously enacts an exclusion that carries ethical weight. Timeto (2011) traces this as a single continuous argument with three stations: Hayles's constrained constructivism establishes that representations are consistent or inconsistent with the flux; Haraway's situated knowledges extend this by insisting that partiality names where the perspective sees from; Barad takes the final step by insisting that the phenomenon itself is produced through the intra-action of apparatus and matter. But this is only three stations of a much longer argument. Spinoza is a station. Bergson is a station. Merleau-Ponty is a station. Bateson and Bohr are stations. Rich and Trinh are stations.

The term "new materialism" may have been coined in the 1990s, but the theorists who built it consistently locate its epistemological foundations upstream. What the 1990s coinage names is not a new condition but a consolidation — the moment when these traditions found common vocabulary across disciplines simultaneously (Ibrisim, 2020, p. 11; van der Tuin & Dolphijn, 2012, p. 13). Not a convergence at a point. A fold in which what had always been folded together became legible as such.

Posthumanism's position in the entanglement is best illustrated -- as a Braidottian nomadic subject on the cartography of human thought -- by Cary Wolfe's Introduction to the edited collection *What is Posthumanism?* Wolfe provides two origin stories and progenitors of the thread of connection: Foucault in the 1960s and the circa World War II 1946-1953 Macy Conferences on cybernetics and the subsequent invention of systems theory. Quoting Wolfe quoting Foucault (Wolfe, 2010, xii), "the historical appearance of this thing called 'man' was not":

> the transition into luminous consciousness of an age-old concern, the entry into objectivity of something that had long remained trapped within beliefs and philosophies: it was the effect of a change in the fundamental arrangements of knowledge. As the archaeology of our thought easily shows, man is an invention of recent date. And one perhaps nearing its end.
>
> If those arrangements were to disappear as they appeared, if some event of which we can at the moment do no more than sense the possibility -- without knowing either what its form will be or what it promises -- were to cause them to crumble, as the ground of Classical thought did, at the end of the eighteenth century, then one can certainly wager that man would be erased, like a face drawn in sand at the edge of the sea.

Posthumanism hinges on varying intensities of belief that man is an invention/transient/ephemeral -- "there is no there there" (Bennett admonishing Lucretius, 2010, 18; see also Gertrude Stein, *Everybody's Autobiography*, 1937) -- in constant peril of erasure.

It is not only new materialism that is not new in any absolute sense. Systems of thought that refuse the human/nonhuman divide and insist on the agency and vitality of the material world have long informed Indigenous ways of knowing, being, and valuing (Pennycook, 2024; Ros Sole, 2020), as well as Spinoza's monism and the idea of multitude as the agent of potentia (Torosyan, 2021, p. 55). What the 1990s coinage names is not a new condition but a consolidation -- the moment when these traditions found common vocabulary across disciplines simultaneously.

Braidotti names Adrienne Rich's "politics of location" -- the methodological claim that knowledge production must begin from the specific, situated, material body of the knower -- as "the cornerstone" of the neo-materialist feminist project, tracing a direct line from Rich's 1984 essay through Haraway's situated knowledges (1988) into the standpoint debates that shaped new materialism's early formation (Braidotti, *New Materialism*, p. 22; *Posthuman Knowledge*, p. 59). Bennett, tracing the intellectual lineage of *Vibrant Matter*, identifies "feminist studies of the body" as the larger trend within political theory that put "desire and bodily practices back on the ethical radar screen" -- the precondition for her own move toward nonhuman vitality (*Vibrant Matter*, p. xi). She then names the eco- and ecofeminist philosophers -- Plumwood, Mathews, Coles among them -- as the teachers from whom she learned the "cultivated, patient, sensory attentiveness to nonhuman forces" that vital materialism requires (p. xiv). Rich's formulation was characteristically direct: "Begin not with a continent or a country or a house, but with the geography closest in -- the body" (*Blood, Bread, and Poetry*, p. 212). That sentence is not a political slogan. It is an onto-epistemological claim -- that situatedness is not contamination but the only honest starting place -- and Braidotti and Bennett are both, in their different registers, still standing on that ground. Mąkowska (2022) names what this means structurally: Rich's poetics constitutes an anticipatory new materialism in which "the politics of location is manifested in her medical history and how the history of her life is registered in her flesh" (p. 50), a formulation indistinguishable from what Barad will later call the material-discursive intra-action through which bodies come to matter.

> Patriarchal thought has limited female biology to its own narrow specifications. The feminist vision has recoiled from female biology for these reasons; it will, I believe, come to view our physicality as a resource, rather than a destiny. In order to live a fully human life we require not only *control* of our bodies (though control is a prerequisite); we must touch the unity and resonance of our physicality, our bond with the natural order, the corporeal ground of our intelligence.
>
> -- Adrienne Rich, *Of Woman Born* (1976, p. 40)

The epistemological break this represents was named most sharply by Haraway. In "Situated Knowledges" (1988), she identifies the "god trick" -- the view from nowhere, the claim to a disembodied, universal perspective -- as the dominant fantasy of western science, and refuses it not in the name of relativism but in the name of a stronger objectivity: one that is partial, embodied, and accountable to its own situatedness. "I am arguing for politics and epistemologies of location, positioning, and situating, where partiality and not universality is the condition of being heard to make rational knowledge claims" (Haraway, 1988, p. 589). Kuhn had earlier shown that scientific paradigms are not neutral accumulations of truth but historically conditioned frameworks that determine what counts as a problem, what counts as a solution, and what gets suppressed as anomaly (*The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*, 1962). Haraway radicalizes this: the issue is not only that paradigms shift but that the fantasy of the view from nowhere is itself an exercise of power. **Posthumanism and new materialism inherit both moves -- Kuhn's paradigm critique and Haraway's situated epistemology -- and extend them into ontology: it is not only knowledge that is situated and partial, but the world itself that is constituted through specific, accountable, intra-active arrangements.**

Timeto (2011) traces this as a single continuous argument with three stations that build on each other rather than simply accumulating. Hayles's constrained constructivism establishes that representations are neither mirrors of reality nor arbitrary constructions -- they are consistent or inconsistent with the flux, where consistency means local, interactive, instrumentally efficacious engagement. Haraway's situated knowledges and figuration extend this by insisting that the partiality of the perspective is not a weakness but the condition of accountability -- the partial perspective names where it sees from, and that naming is what makes it answerable. Barad's agential realism takes the final step: it is not only the knowing subject that is situated but the phenomenon itself that is produced through the intra-action of apparatus and matter. Representation is not removed from an originary source -- it is the cut that produces the things it appears merely to describe. The diffraction metaphor itself originates earlier: Haraway first introduces it in "The Promises of Monsters" (1992, pp. 299-300) in direct engagement with Trinh T. Minh-ha's formulation of "critical difference within." By 1997 Haraway develops it further; Barad (2003) then grounds it in quantum physics and makes it an ontological claim. Diffraction did not begin when Barad named it. It is the name Barad gives to what this entire philosophical lineage was already doing -- and that lineage begins with a filmmaker, not a physicist.

**Adrienne Rich named the project in 1973. In "Diving into the Wreck," she describes descending not to recover the myth of the wreck but the wreck itself -- "the thing I came for: / the wreck and not the story of the wreck / the thing itself and not the myth" (*Adrienne Rich's Poetry and Prose*, ed. Gelpi and Gelpi, p. 67). This is the epistemological stance the entire genealogy traced above requires: the refusal to accept the inherited story -- the dualist framework, the representation standing in for the thing, the concept exhausting the object -- and the insistence on returning to the material itself, to what was there before the mythology overlaid it.** Rich was practicing this in poetry two decades before new materialism named it as theory. The origin story does not begin with DeLanda and Braidotti in the 1990s. It begins wherever someone refused the wreck-as-story and dove toward the thing itself.

Understanding what new materialism is positioning itself against requires a brief sketch of what it replaces. The dominant modern account of matter -- Cartesian-Newtonian in its inheritance -- treats matter as passive, inert stuff to be acted upon by an external force, whether that force is the human will, divine providence, or the laws of physics imposed from without. As Coole and Frost describe it, this framework "yields a conceptual and practical domination of nature as well as a specifically modern attitude or ethos of subjectivist potency" (Coole & Frost, 2010, p. 9): the observer classifies matter from a distance, aided by theories that enable the manipulation and reconfiguration of matter on an unprecedented scale. The human subject is the agent; matter is the object of that agency. New materialism defines itself precisely as an alternative to this picture -- not by reversing its terms, but by refusing the distinction that makes it possible.

Coole & Frost position the posthumanist ontological reorientation similarly as a response to contemporary conditions, where we must "reorient ourselves profoundly in relation to the world, to one another, and to ourselves," naming what is at stake as "nothing less than a challenge to some of the most basic assumptions that have underpinned the modern world including its normative sense of the human and its beliefs about human agency" (Coole & Frost, 2010, p. 6). The challenge extends to "material objects and the natural environment" -- to how we labor on, exploit, and relate to the nonhuman world. Crucially, Coole and Frost insist that calling this turn toward new materialisms does not deny "their rich materialist heritage": many contributors to the project draw inspiration from materialist traditions that long predate the 1990s coinage. The newness is not in the recognition of material entanglement, but in the convergence of tools and vocabularies that finally make it speakable across disciplines simultaneously.

This convergence marked a turn away from the persistent dualisms in humanist traditions (Ibrisim, 2020). New materialist theories largely emerged from scholars trained as natural scientists with interests in science theory, technoscience, and social, feminist, indigenous, environmental, and animal-rights issues (Taguchi & Eriksson, 2021).

Bennett (2010) provides an exhaustive, yet not exclusive, accounting of vitalist and materialist movements that predated her own vital materialism. In philosophy and metaphysics, materialism is a form of monism holding that matter is the fundamental substance of nature, so that all things, including mind and consciousness, arise from material interactions and depend on physical processes, including those of the human brain and nervous system (Wikipedia, 2026).

Bennett elaborates on the long view of time and provides a proof from the first single cell organisms to the evolution of homosapiens: we are all made of the same thing, we are boundaryless, and thus humans are walking, talking minerals. If we are all made of the same things, and humans have agency, and humans are things, then things have agency. And vice versa, by inscribing things with thing-power we also elevate the agency of humans as things with more power in the ethical configurations and reconfigurations of reality than humans believe they have. Because Barad grounds herself at the atomic level, we can imagine that we are all permeable and things moving freely through each other at the level of electrons, protons, quarks, and other members of the particle zoo.

The ontological commitments of new materialism have roots in a vitalist tradition whose scope extended well beyond biology. As Coole and Frost trace in their introduction to *New Materialisms*, critical vitalism posited a generative principle responsible not only for morphological development in organisms but for "the progressive development of personality and history" -- an isomorphism between physical and psychological orders, matter and meaning, that the dominant mechanist tradition systematically suppressed (Coole & Frost, 2010, p. 49). Matter as intelligent, self-organizing, and generative was repeatedly displaced by matter as passive, inert, and awaiting inscription by an external force. The ethico-onto-epistemological insistence of Barad, Bennett, and Braidotti is, among other things, a refusal of that displacement -- an insistence that matter is "always already an ongoing historicity" (Barad, 2003, p. 822) and that any ethics adequate to this reality must take the vitality of matter seriously, not as metaphor, but as condition.

The entanglement described above is not a recent insight. It was present in the earliest strata of western thought -- in Heraclitus, in the pre-Socratics, in thinkers who were willing to sit with the complexity and flux of the world before a more convenient framework was institutionalized over them. The pattern that follows is consistent across centuries: wherever a thinker pointed toward interrelatedness, indeterminacy, or the porousness of boundaries, the Platonic inheritance arrived to domesticate the insight -- extracting the useful notation, stripping the relational ontology, and handing back a binary. This is not incidental intellectual history. Posthumanism and new materialism resubstantiate entanglement, bringing to the fore what kept getting suppressed -- grounding theory, method, and practice in the reality that everything is interconnected and always becoming, a configuration that prior counter-movements, however vigorous, had not yet assembled in the specific register of the post-human and vital materialism together (Ferrando, 2019, p. 170; see also van der Tuin and Dolphijn, 2010, p. 95; Haraway, 2016 \[page TK\]). Ferrando traces these proclivities back to Aristotle, Plato, and the Old Testament, where the world was separated into hierarchies of the ideal and the profane.

The retreat did not end with Plato. It became a repeating pattern, and the examples that follow are not exhaustive -- they are chosen because they trace the binary's dominance through domains directly relevant to the concerns of this project: computation, information theory, digital logic, and the ontological assumptions embedded in the tools that now mediate most knowledge work. Thinkers whose work pointed toward entanglement, relation, and indeterminacy get misread or domesticated into the dualist framework that followed them. Leibniz's binary arithmetic -- originally a cosmological meditation on generativity, creation from nothing, the infinite combinations arising from two starting conditions -- was extracted from its relational context and formalized by Boole into a logic of true/false, yes/no, on/off. The mystical generativity got stripped; the binary stayed. That stripping became the foundation of digital computing, as though Leibniz had meant to say: reality is made of two things. He meant almost the opposite. Shannon's information theory compounds the irony: his core insight was that noise -- disorder, uncertainty, the unpredictable -- carries the most information. A completely predictable signal carries none. But the computer science built on his foundations embraced boolean either/or and suppressed the noise. And underneath every boolean bit is an analog signal -- a continuous voltage -- interpreted as on or off by applying an arbitrary threshold. The digital is a reading convention imposed on an analog reality. The entanglement was always there; it kept getting bracketed out. (The connection is not accidental: Leibniz sent his binary system to the Jesuit missionary Joachim Bouvet in 1703, reading it through the I Ching -- the relational logic was always in dialogue with non-Western thought before Boole stripped it to boolean arithmetic.)

What Boole's formalization stripped from Leibniz was not incidental surplus but the ontological core of his thought. Coole, following Merleau-Ponty and Deleuze, traces a Leibnizean inheritance that runs directly counter to the binary logic his notation was misread to authorize. For Leibniz, the world is constituted not by discrete units standing in external relations but by folds: matter as endlessly folding and unfolding, generating provisional forms as styles of existing rather than fixed substances. There are no voids in Leibnizean matter: "folds replace holes," and Leibnizean matter offers, as Deleuze glosses him, "an infinitely porous, spongy, or cavernous texture without emptiness," with each body elastic, "pierced with irregular passages," dependent for its coherence on the pressure of external forces (Coole, 2010, p. 111). Deleuze, writing on Leibniz in *The Fold*, notes that the exemplary science of the fold is not Euclidean geometry but origami -- and finds anticipations of Leibnizean folding among the pre-Socratics, as well as within postclassical physics: in Merleau-Ponty's Nature lectures, in Whitehead, and -- most strikingly -- in the folded proteins of microbiology (Coole, 2010, p. 110). For Merleau-Ponty -- who in his working notes explicitly planned to substitute being-in-the-world for Leibniz's God while maintaining "entirely" certain of Leibniz's descriptions, "only ridding them of their substantialist and onto-theological elaborations in order to apply them to wild Being" -- the body is itself a fold: the lived experience of precisely this Leibnizean folded ontology, not binary opposition between two fixed terms, but a reversibility that "always miscarries at the last moment," producing the productive dehiscence, the difference between identicals, that makes emergence possible rather than only inert repetition (Coole, 2010, p. 107). What the Baroque artists understood, and what Boole stripped out, is that this fold is not a theoretical position but a perceptual and material fact -- present in how the body inhabits the world, in how matter coils and hollows and wraps, in how Baroque chiaroscuro renders "clarity endlessly plunging into obscurity" rather than separating light from dark as in Cartesian optics (Coole, 2010, p. 111). Deleuze observes that Descartes tried to move through the labyrinth of matter along rectilinear tracks and failed to grasp its curvature; the Baroque art of Caravaggio and Tintoretto -- where contours are effaced, where light vibrates in the folds of matter, where draperies and tessellated fabrics and complex harmonics with multiple rhythms make philosophies that "resolve Cartesian distinctions of mind and matter through physical means" -- these are not decorative flourishes but ontological propositions enacted in form (Coole, 2010, pp. 111-112). The binary was always a misreading of a thinker who meant something closer to the opposite.

Quantum physics follows the same pattern. Bohr's core claim -- that observation and observed are inseparable, that there is no view from outside the phenomenon -- was both philosophically and politically threatening. The entanglement that quantum theory places at the foundation of physical reality was domesticated: the equations were kept, the ontological implications were bracketed. The Copenhagen interpretation became the acceptable face of quantum mechanics precisely because it permitted physicists to use the formalism without confronting what it said about the nature of reality. As Barad demonstrates at length in *Meeting the Universe Halfway*, Bohr was pointing toward something that the dominant framework had no room for: an entangled world in which the observer is always already part of what is being observed, in which there is no prior independently determinate reality waiting to be measured. The retreat from this recognition is the same retreat Plato made. Posthumanism and new materialism are not the first traditions to contest this retreat -- Spinoza's monism, Whitehead's process philosophy, and phenomenology each pushed back in their own registers -- but they are the tradition that refuses it in the specific configuration of the now: holding the post-human and vital materialism together in a way that earlier counter-movements did not.

The suppression did not go uncontested. While Bohr's entanglement was being domesticated by the Copenhagen interpretation, artists were enacting the same ontology in practice -- not as philosophical argument but as perceptual fact. Merleau-Ponty identifies Cézanne as a phenomenologist avant la lettre, and the connection is not incidental. Cézanne was working at precisely the historical moment when both quantum physics and phenomenology were beginning to articulate what classical science and Cartesian painting had systematically suppressed: that the body does not perceive the world from a detached point of view, and that matter does not consist of solid objects with fixed contours awaiting neutral description. When the body moves around in space, it does not perceive things with the relative sizes objective calculation would predict. It does not inhabit a flat, geometrical, fully determinate plane but a milieu, an Umwelt that remains ambiguous, indeterminate, and resonant with an expressive significance that affects the body's perception of spatial relations. Merleau-Ponty discerns Cézanne as trying to capture these perspectival distortions as they occur in perception, in order to convey "an emerging order," where the object is "in the act of appearing, organizing itself before our eyes" (Coole, 2010, p. 104). Cézanne's painting brings the material world alive; it does not measure or represent reality but emulates the way it materializes in perception (Coole, 2010, p. 106). Coole notes that Merleau-Ponty explicitly contrasts this with the Cartesian tradition of classical perspective -- what he calls "Renaissance perspective as a cultural fact" and "impoverished" as artistic style precisely because its Euclidean geometry presents nature in fixed, objective coordinates rather than in the ambiguous, indeterminate, resonant field in which it actually appears. Cézanne's refusal of that convention is therefore not an aesthetic choice but a phenomenological position: the attempt to suspend culturally fashioned perception and uncover the "vertical" world of "brute" or "wild" perception as it emerges (Coole, 2010, p. 106). Dada introduces chance, noise, and indeterminacy into artistic practice as ontological statement, not provocation. The Bauhaus and Black Mountain College bring together artists, scientists, musicians, and designers in configurations that refuse disciplinary separation -- themselves a kind of institutional enactment of the entangled world. John Cage, working at Black Mountain and drawing directly on the I Ching -- the relational cosmology that Leibniz had encountered and stripped to notation -- recovers what got bracketed out: indeterminacy, chance, noise as information rather than interruption. His 4'33" is not silence; it is the insistence that the world is always already sounding, that the frame of attention is the apparatus. Robert Rauschenberg's combines -- street debris, newspaper, paint, radio -- are assemblages in Bennett's sense before Bennett named them: matter with thing-power, distributed agency, the refusal of a sovereign authoring subject. Shannon's suppressed insight that noise carries the most information finds its artistic enactment in all of this: the Dadaist cut-up, the found sound, the accidental score. The counter-tradition is not incidental to the theoretical project of posthumanism and new materialism. It is its genealogy.

The suppression of matter by representation was not only contested in music and physics. Visual art theory ran the same argument, and it ran it with quantum physics as its resource. Barbara Bolt, working from painting practice (in Coleman and van der Tuin, eds., *Methods and Genealogies of New Materialism*, 2024), identifies the gap that Western visual theory has installed since Plato as its founding premise: representation is second-order data, a substitute that can never be its object, a sign that circulates within social meaning but cannot be in dynamic relation to the materiality of its referent (Bolt, 2024 \[orig. 2003\], p. 269). The influence of literary theory and cultural studies on visual art deepened the gap -- reducing visual representation to a sign-system, a complex of discursive meanings. Bolt traces the limit of even Butler's sophisticated performativity account: Butler can explain how bodies materialize through repetition and discourse, but cannot account for "the materialisation that occurs in the interplay between the matter of bodies, cultural knowledges or discourse, and the materials of production" (Bolt, 2024 \[orig. 2003\], p. 271). Matter slips away back into signification. The return Bolt calls for -- "to the energetic potential of matter" -- runs via Vicki Kirby's (1997) reading of Bohr: for quantum physics, representation is not removed from an originary source, particles are inseparable identities in/of spacetime, and there is no enclosed local context within which causality might be contained. Kontturi and Tiainen (2021) confirm that this made Bolt one of the first art scholars to address quantum theory in relation to visual practice -- a move made in parallel with, not downstream from, Barad's development of agential realism from the same quantum sources.

The art history lineage that supports this claim runs deeper than Bolt's individual contribution. Alongside the philosophical counter-tradition traced above -- Baroque folding, Cézanne's phenomenological perception, the Black Mountain assemblage -- there is a parallel track in which artists and art educators were explicitly drawing on particle physics as a resource for rethinking what art does and how it works. Scholars in art education have traced a lineage in which the quantum overthrow of classical determinism -- the insistence that observation is always participatory, that phenomena do not pre-exist the apparatus that produces them -- found its cultural articulation in the dematerialization of the art object and the turn toward process, event, and relation (Sullivan, \[year and citation TK\]). The trajectory runs from quantum indeterminacy (Bohr, Heisenberg) through pedagogical translation at Bauhaus and Black Mountain (process over product, experimentation over mastery) through Cage's chance operations as explicit quantum analogue through conceptual art's refusal of the fixed object through systems art and early computation (Burnham's *Systems Esthetics*, 1968) to generative and posthuman practice now. Bolt's intervention, originally published in 2003 and republished in Coleman and van der Tuin's *Methods and Genealogies of New Materialism* (2024), names the same quantum resources from within painting practice rather than conceptual art -- arriving at the same conclusion from a different studio position. The two lines -- conceptual art via Sullivan and painting theory via Bolt -- converge on the same claim that Barad will later formalize philosophically: representation is not a second-order picture of a pre-given world but a co-constitutive intra-action with it.

#### 3.1 The Critique of Man

Posthumanism is a critique of a specific notion of what it means to be human: the autonomous, sovereign, rational subject installed by Enlightenment humanism: and of the exclusions that produced that figure (Ferrando, 2014). Foucault could already see, from within the humanist revolution, that it was contingent: “man is an invention of recent date. And one perhaps nearing its end” (Wolfe, 2010, p. xii) (citing Foucault). The “man” at stake is not all humans. It is a particular configuration that van der Tuin and Dolphijn trace to Kant: “Kant started his philosophy with the human being, or even better, with human thought and its relation to nature. In other words, Kant turned (theological) metaphysics into transcendental anthropology” (Dolphijn & Tuin, 2012, p. 165). After Kant, the human subject becomes the condition of possibility for knowledge itself, the measure against which everything else is placed.

Ferrando (Ferrando, 2014) specifies what was installed in the figure of that universal human: a particular type of embodiment: male, white, Western, heterosexual, physically able, propertied: treated as neutral and fit-for-all. The critique of Man is therefore inseparable from the feminist, critical race, queer, disability, and postcolonial traditions that refused to accept the universal as universal. Braidotti (Braidotti, 2024) proposes the framework of two discrete but intersecting critiques: the critique of the humanistic “‘man of reason’” and the critique of exceptionalist anthropocentrism, or “‘anthropolatry’” (p. xii). Together, these critiques expose how Eurocentric universalism and belligerent “‘species-ism’” produced exclusionary norms of the human, casting sexualized, racialized, and naturalized others as dehumanized or outside the category of the human (p. xii). Braidotti argues that the notions currently circulating around the posthuman are affirmative because they “break free from the sedimented habits of thought that confirm institutionalised power relations” (p. xii).

Posthumanism is not anti humanism, and should not be confused with transhumanism. Transhumanism is antihumanism’s mirror: it claims to transcend the human while intensifying the very figure posthumanism rejects. Transhumanism, associated with figures such as Nick Bostrom and Hans Moravec, seeks the enhancement of human intellectual, physical, and emotional capacities, the elimination of disease and suffering, and the extension of human life span, all in service of a figure of the human whose “basic capacities so radically exceed those of present humans as to no longer be unambiguously human by our current standards” (Bostrom, cited in Wolfe, 2010, p. xiii). Wolfe describes transhumanism as “an intensification of humanism” because it extends the prerogatives of the autonomous liberal subject rather than questioning that subject as the basic unit of analysis (Wolfe, 2010, p. xiv). Moravec’s fantasy of downloading consciousness into a computer in pursuit of technological immortality continues the humanist investment in disembodied rationality. Posthumanism instead opposes “the fantasies of disembodiment and autonomy, inherited from humanism itself” and insists on embodiment, embeddedness, and materiality (Wolfe, 2010, p. xiv).

#### 3.2 The Critique of Inert Matter

Western metaphysics inherited from the atomists (Leucippus, Democritus, Epicurus), a model of matter as inert: tiny, indivisible, passive particles moving through the void, their shapes fixed, their combinations determined by collision. Matter moved when struck. It produced only what its given characteristics permitted (Bennett, 2010). Gamble, Hanan, and Nail (2019) argue that Lucretius, in his De Rerum Natura, moves beyond earlier atomism and anticipates performative new materialism by treating matter as something that ceaselessly constitutes novel entities and relations (pp. 112–113), but the line that endured was the one that served mechanism.

The multiple theorists cited here trace the evolution of matter through the proponents of matter as inert: French philosopher and scientist René Descartes (1596–1650), English Scientific Revolution philosopher Sir Francis Bacon (1561–1626), German Enlightenment philosopher Immanuel Kant (1724–1804). Then they trace the movement through the proponents of matter as lively: French philosopher Henri Bergson (1859–1941) and French philosopher Maurice Merleau-Ponty (1908–1961).

Bacon synthesized theology, naturalism, and mechanism into a single framework in which, as Gamble et al. summarize him, “the force implanted by God in these first particles makes up all the variety of things” (2019, p. 115). God imparts force to matter, sets the laws of nature in motion, and rests. Descartes, in a hard line account of dualism, states mind and matter are categorically different substances, and the mind examines the material world from a position of separation. Kant, as already noted, secures this epistemologically, through his conception of a world in which matter can be inscribed, extracted, and instrumentalized without resistance: because matter, by definition, offers none (Gamble et al., 2019) . Coole and Frost (2010) name the consequence: a framework that “yields a conceptual and practical domination of nature as well as a specifically modern attitude or ethos of subjectivist potency” (p. 9). The critique of the sovereign subject (§ 3.1) and the critique of inert matter meet across this cut.

Barad’s matter is “not a fixed substance; rather, matter is substance in its intra-active becoming, not a thing but a doing, a congealing of agency” and “always already an ongoing historicity” (Barad, 2003, pp. 821, 822). Bennett opposes the parsing of the world into “dull matter (it, things)” and “vibrant life (us, beings)” and asks how political responses would change if we took seriously “the vitality of (nonhuman) bodies” (Bennett, 2010, pp. vii–viii). Braidotti describes philosophical neo-materialism as a line of immanence concerned with “the vital immanence of non-anthropomorphic life-systems,” where differences are not constituted oppositionally or dialectically but as “internal modulations within a common matter that is intelligent and self-organised” (Braidotti, 2024, p. xvii). Barad’s intra-active becoming, Bennett’s vibrant matter, and Braidotti’s line of immanence continue to resonate throughout this text.

Coole and Frost (2010) also stress that new materialism is not new and does not deny a “rich materialist heritage” (p. 7). Matter as active, generative, and self-organizing has a longer philosophical history than the recent turn. Spinoza had already made the counter-claim in 1677: the mind is the idea of the body. Mind and body are the same thing. What Spinoza calls conatus, the striving of singular things to persevere in their being, is not something added to matter from outside but immanent to it (Bennett, 2010; Dolphijn & Tuin, 2012). Henri Bergson expands Spinoza’s notions into a philosophy of duration in which life works to “insert some indetermination into matter,” and the forms it creates are therefore “indeterminate, i.e. unforeseeable” (Bennett, 2010, p. 79). Merleau-Ponty extended the emphasis on immanence, process, and embodiment through a phenomenology of perception in which the body is itself a fold, a reversibility that “always miscarries at the last moment” (Coole, 2010, pp. 110–111).

Postmodernism began the dismantling of the nature/culture divide, but Ferrando (Ferrando, 2013) argues that postmodernism’s dismantling of the nature/culture dualism often privileged its nurtural side, producing a constructivist and representationalist radicalization that “somehow lost track of the material” (p. 114). Barad laments: “language matters, discourse matters, culture matters,” but “the only thing that does not seem to matter anymore is matter” (Barad, 2003, p. 801, cited in Ferrando, 2013, p. 114). New materialism responded not by restoring nature against culture, but by refusing the dualism altogether and treating matter as an ongoing process of materialization that is “dynamic, shifting, inherently entangled, diffractional and performative” (Ferrando, 2013, p. 114). Posthumanism inherits postmodernism’s critique of the sovereign subject; new materialism reasserts the material dimension that constructivist accounts subordinated (Ferrando, 2013, pp. 25, 114).

#### 7. The Violence of Traditional Methods

Trinh T. Minh-ha’s critique of anthropology in Woman, Native, Other (1989) names the violence of traditional methods in its most sustained form in the corpus gathered here. The problem is not that particular disciplinary practices are badly executed but that the traditional methodological apparatus operates as a form of compulsion. “Speaking, writing, and discoursing are not mere acts of communication; they are above all acts of compulsion” (Minh-ha, 1989, p. 52). Language itself carries the operation: “language is one of the most complex forms of subjugation, being at the same time the locus of power and unconscious servility” (Minh-ha, 1989, p. 52). The methods that purport to let the subject speak for itself are the same methods that determine what can be heard as speaking in the first place.

The specific shape of this compulsion in traditional research is what Trinh calls “the positivist dream of a neutralized language that strips off all its singularity to become nature’s exact, unmisted reflection” (Minh-ha, 1989, p. 53). The dream is not of clarity in the ordinary sense but of a language that effaces itself in favor of what it names: “The claim to objectivity subjects words to a willed meaning-intention.” The words themselves become disposable: “Words are solicited only for their effacement from the page. Their materiality, their glaring bodies must somehow sink and disappear from the field of visibility, to yield ground to the ‘pure presence’ or that which he attempts to capture and retain, which, however, always lies outside of words” (Minh-ha, 1989, p. 53). Artistic research cannot operate under this regime without ceasing to be research in the sense Parts One and Two have established: the materiality of the apparatus is constitutive of what the research can produce, and a method that requires the apparatus to disappear has already foreclosed the knowledge the apparatus makes available.

The positional move that enforces this regime is the designation of the “native.” Trinh names the double move by which anthropological method operates: “Terming us the ‘natives’ focuses on our innate qualities and our belonging to a particular place by birth; terming them the ‘natives,’ on their being born inferior and ‘non-Europeans.’ As homonyms, these two ‘natives’ sometimes claim to merge and other times hear nothing of each other” (Minh-ha, 1989, p. 52). Traditional methods operate through the ambiguity of this double naming: they claim to let the native speak while retaining the authority to determine what the native’s speech is about, what it means, and what counts as a legitimate articulation of it. Any artistic research methodology that does not actively refuse this operation risks reproducing it under a new name.

The literature gathered below reports several linked forms in which traditional methods of research operate as constraint: forced verbalization, premature closure, overwritten voice, and the reduction of material or performative inquiry to something acceptable only once translated into a prior academic grammar. The forms are not separate problems but specific operations of the compulsion Trinh names.

Van der Tuin and Dolphijn identify “dualist thinking that still dominates academia today” (2015, p. 26). Bolt places artistic research within that academic terrain when she writes that “the development of artistic research has proceeded in the shadow of the research ‘model’ par excellence, that is, science-as-research” (2016, p. 137). Schwab identifies “reflection” as a prescribed demand in art education and artistic research contexts, especially when it is assumed that knowledge must be verbalized after the fact in order to count (Schwab, 2018, p. 9). Luc Döbereiner states: “Epistemologically, artistic research tends to challenge propositional semantics,” and it must go “beyond the confines of standardised forms of academic publishing in the humanities” (2019, p. 48). De Assis and D’Errico write that artistic research “makes of the resistance to definitions, closures, and disciplinary constraints one of its strongest points” (2019, p. 2). Bolt adds that artistic research “continues to be seen as lacking credibility because the methods cannot be replicated exactly” (2016, p. 137).

Draude and Klumbytė warn against treating hybrid or “third space” methods as neutral zones. Their argument is that such spaces must retain their feminist, decolonial, and postcolonial commitments, including attention to power, positionality, and the uneven conditions under which methods are produced and received (Draude & Klumbytė, 2022). They insist that hybrid methodologies may need to be created rather than imported (Draude & Klumbytė, 2022).

Fox and Alldred (2021) warn that diffractive methodology can become “researcher-centric and context-dependent,” questioning “how or to what extent” particular methods influenced findings. Fox and Alldred work within posthumanist methodology, not against it. The question of transferability remains a tension within the methodology (Fox & Alldred, 2023).

Barad rejects the demand that diffractive methodology prove its objectivity by removing the researcher from the apparatus. Representationalism, she argues, is “a prisoner of the problematic metaphysics it postulates” (Barad, 2003, p. 803). Accountability comes from naming the apparatus, specifying the cuts, and tracing the marks left on bodies (Barad, 2007).

> What is an assemblage? It is a multiplicity which is made up of many heterogeneous terms and which establishes liaisons, relations between them, across ages, sexes and reigns – different natures. Thus, the assemblage's only unity is that of co-functioning: it is a symbiosis, a "sympathy". It is never filiations which are important, but alliances, alloys; these are not successions, lines of descent, but contagions, epidemics, the wind. (Deleuze & Parnet, 2007, p. 69)

- **The translation.** Paul Foss and Paul Patton first rendered *agencement* as "assemblage" in their 1981 translation of "Rhizome." Later translators, including Brian Massumi in *A Thousand Plateaus* (1987), kept it (Bogic, 2017, p. 140).
- **The critique of the translation.**
  - Phillips (2006) argues that Deleuze and Guattari rarely used the French word *assemblage* in a philosophical sense, and that the English rendering can "give rise to connotations based on analogical impressions" (quoted on Wikipedia; confirm in Phillips).
  - Bogic: in French the word "implies both a process and a state of being: both the act of fitting/arranging and the arrangement itself," while "the English word assemblage tends to imply only the state of being, therefore eliminating the active aspect" (2017, p. 140).
  - Puar: "'Assemblage' is actually an awkward translation of the French term agencement," and the question is "the generative effects of this 'mistranslation'" (2012, p. 57).
  - Frohmann: "Agencement suggests design, a sense missing from the French assemblage" (2012, cited in Goehring, 2019, p. 13).
- **Downstream.** DeLanda builds a large-scale social ontology on the English term (2006; 2016). Prokić (2021) and Buchanan (2015) argue that it departs from Deleuze and Guattari. Bennett's "agency of assemblages" (2010, ch. 2) is the line that reaches your comps. Pennycook's *Language Assemblages* (2024), already in your references, carries the term into applied linguistics, and Siffrinn and Coda (2025, p. 195), in your comps, take the assemblage as the basic unit of inquiry there: your LLSS bridge. Your comps keep the units distinct (Bennett's thing or actant, Barad's phenomenon), so assemblage is not the universal unit.

#### Strand 2: assemblage, the art term

**\[Note\]** What the sources say so far:

- Jean Dubuffet used "assemblage" from 1953 for his *assemblages d'empreintes*, relief collages that included butterfly wings. He is credited as the first to use the word for art practice (National Galleries Scotland, n.d.; Seitz, as summarized in Cotz, 2023).
- William C. Seitz's exhibition *The Art of Assemblage* opened at the Museum of Modern Art in fall 1961 (October 2–November 12 per Koregos; Wikipedia gives October 4), then traveled to Dallas and San Francisco in 1962. #### Diffraction: assemblage and the found object

**\[Note\]** Both practices take up matter "not intended as art materials" (Seitz's phrase, Strand 2). Read through each other, they place agency differently. What the sources say, then interference points to test.

1. **One chooser or many relations.** The readymade puts the act in a single choice and the frame that receives it ("He CHOSE it"). ## Chapter 3: Methodology and Design

### 3.1 Design rationale

*Pasted from comps v35, Relational Worlds Need Relational Methods, pp. 31–33. Its last paragraph is a drafting note to yourself.*

If the world is constituted through relation and intra-action — if phenomena do not pre-exist the apparatuses that produce them, if agency is distributed across assemblages of human and nonhuman actants, if matter is vibratory and generative rather than inert — then methods that treat phenomena as discrete, stable, and pre-given do not merely produce incomplete findings. They produce a systematically distorted account of what the world is. The mismatch between a relational ontology and a representationalist method is not a technical problem to be corrected through rigor. It is an ethical problem: to impose a subject-object framework on a relational world is to enact the very suppression of vitality, agency, and entanglement that posthumanism and new materialism were built to refuse.

Bennett names the perceptual root of the problem directly. Without a cultivated, countercultural practice of attending to what exceeds the subject-object split, "the world appears as if it consists only of active human subjects who confront passive objects and their law-governed mechanisms" (*Vibrant Matter*, p. xiv). This appearance is not a neutral description. It is the product of trained perceptual habits — habits that are, as Bennett insists, politically consequential. A world that appears to consist only of active subjects and passive objects is a world that authorizes domination, extraction, and the suppression of the distributed agency that runs through every assemblage. Relational methods are not a preference or a philosophical style. They are the attempt to correct a trained perceptual distortion that has been doing political damage for as long as the subject-object split has been the default frame.

Developing the capacity for relational perception requires what Bennett calls foundational comportments: an open-minded, open-ended willingness to suspend the pre-conceived; an anticipatory readiness to encounter thing-power before it has been named or categorized; and "a perceptual style open to the appearance of thing-power" (*Vibrant Matter*, p. 5). This last phrase matters because it frames the researcher's orientation not as neutrality but as a trained receptivity — a readiness cultivated in advance of any specific encounter. Bennett traces this orientation to Thoreau's instruction to practice "the discipline of looking always at what is to be seen" (p. 5): not looking for what you expect, not looking to confirm what you already know, but sustaining the open discipline of looking itself as a practice.

This perceptual discipline connects directly to van der Tuin and Dolphijn's (2010) argument that new materialism requires cartography rather than classification as its methodological posture. Classification is a territorializing gesture: it assigns new phenomena to pre-existing categories, confirms what is already known, and forecloses what the encounter might otherwise disclose. Cartography is generative: it maps forces, movements, and encounters as they unfold, producing a "living map" — Braidotti's phrase — rather than a fixed taxonomy. The shift from classification to cartography is the methodological consequence of the ontological shift from substance to relation. Relational worlds cannot be classified into stability. They can only be mapped in motion.

Fox and Alldred's (2021) work in applied research domains — education, health, sustainability — demonstrates what this looks like in practice. Diffractive method is particularly generative precisely in research situations that are already assemblages of institutions, bodies, technologies, and affects, because it refuses to quarantine any of those elements as mere context. The research situation itself — the classroom, the clinic, the policy environment — is treated as an active participant in what gets known, not as a neutral container for findings that then travel elsewhere. The question of how findings travel into policy and practice contexts is itself a question about assemblage: what configurations make certain knowledge mobile, and which suppress it. Fox & Alldred name typical applied-research domains (education, health, sustainability, etc.) and give concrete topical examples like research on "sexual harassment and sexual violence in the workplace or educational settings," or research to address "vaccine hesitancy."

(Fox & Alldred, 2021)

This is a clean "practice case" because it lets you show: diffractive method is attractive precisely when the research situation is already an assemblage of institutions, bodies, technologies, and affects---and because it foregrounds the problem of how findings travel into policy/practice contexts.

*Pasted from comps v35, Speaking Nearby and the Refusal of Closure, p. 83 (cut from the final)*

The institutional stakes of this position are not abstract. The traditional research evaluation framework demands a specific form of accountability: the research question is posed in advance, the methods are specified, the findings are reported, the conclusions are stated. This sequence assumes that knowledge is extractable from the encounter — that the researcher enters the field, gathers data, and produces conclusions that travel out of the context of their production. For new materialist art practice as research, every one of these assumptions enacts the suppression the framework is built to resist. The question that drives the research often cannot be fully articulated before the making begins. The methods are inseparable from the encounter. The findings are not propositional statements separable from the practice that generated them. The conclusion, in the traditional sense, is precisely what the practice withholds — because the interval that makes meaning possible cannot be closed without destroying what it was producing.

The thinker-by-thinker account above describes how each major theorist has conceptualized diffractive practice at the level of orientation and commitment. What the practice looks like when operationalized as a protocol — when a researcher actually tries to work diffractively — has been developed by a range of practitioners working across fields, each arriving at similar structures through different routes.

Lambert (submitted) develops five conceptual entry points for diffractive practice in teacher education: affect, intra-action, becoming-with, assemblage, and spacetimemattering. Each is a conceptual tool derived from posthumanist inquiry that reorients the practitioner away from the humanist, linear, product-oriented logic of reflective practice and toward an "otherwise" practice adequate to the complex, relational, non-linear nature of teaching encounters. Crucially, Lambert does not offer these as a protocol to replace reflection and explicitly avoids a recipe for implementation. They are entry points — ways of beginning to think differently, not steps to follow. Diffraction in Lambert's framing is an orientation, not a procedure: "not an 'either/or' alternative but an 'otherwise', expanded way of thinking" (Lambert, submitted, p. 3). This is consistent with the broader literature: every serious account of diffractive practice warns against treating it as a recipe precisely because a recipe reinstalls the linear, product-oriented, closure-seeking logic the practice is designed to resist.

Hill (2017) makes the most useful structural distinction in the literature for practitioners: the difference between **diffractive reading** and **experimenting with a diffractive apparatus**. Diffractive reading occurs outside the event — it is a way of analyzing or interpreting material after the fact, reading texts, data, or experiences through one another to trace the interference patterns they produce. It is, Hill argues, analogous to Schön's reflection-on-action: retrospective, outside the immediate encounter, producing new understandings that may then inform practice. Experimenting with a diffractive apparatus occurs in situ — it is the practitioner's intra-action with other bodies during an educative or research event, contributing to particular configurations of reality as they unfold. This is analogous to reflection-in-action: embedded in the encounter, unfolding in real time, attending to what intra-action is producing rather than representing what was produced. Both are material-discursive practices that configure the world in particular ways; neither is merely interpretive. The distinction matters because it maps onto different modes of being a researcher: one retrospective and analytical, one embedded and generative. Hill identifies three modes of becoming a diffractive practitioner: becoming-with the world, displacing and diffracting the selves who teach, and embracing difference and interference (Hill, 2017, pp. 6–14). These three are not sequential steps but orientations that interpenetrate — each requires the others, and all three together constitute what it means to shift from a reflective to a diffractive stance. Martin, Sayers, and Carroll (2025), working in organizational studies, operationalize Braidotti's transpositions as six practices: embracing alternative ways of seeing and understanding the world, assessing and critiquing imaginary forms, preparing to make the creative leap, accounting for locations and positioning, storying the in-between space, and developing new frames of resonance. As noted in the thinker-by-thinker account above, these six practices are the authors' own derivation from Braidotti's oeuvre — they are not steps Braidotti herself prescribes. This is an important methodological point: scholars build protocols from theoretical frameworks all the time, and the test of such a protocol is not whether the theorist endorsed it but whether it remains accountable to the theoretical commitments of the framework it derives from. Martin et al.'s practices do: each is grounded in Braidotti's relational ontology of becoming, her critique of imaginary formations, her nomadic subject position, and her affirmative ethical orientation. The protocol is an operationalization, not a transcription.

The Mosaic Approach (Clark and Moss, 2001), developed for listening research with young children, represents a different kind of case: a protocol that satisfies the umbrella criteria without using NM vocabulary and without having been derived from NM theory. It predates Barad's *Meeting the Universe Halfway* by six years and developed independently through three roots — participatory rural development, Reggio Emilia's pedagogy of listening and relationships, and a rights-based view of young children as experts in their own lives. Yet it enacts the same ontological commitments: knowledge is co-constructed between researcher and participant through multiple simultaneous modes; no single method or perspective has priority; the non-verbal, embodied, and material are data alongside the verbal; the research is ongoing and open-ended rather than closure-seeking; and the researcher is a visible part of the process rather than a neutral observer. Dudley (2024) brings the Mosaic Approach explicitly into contact with Braidotti's nomadic ethics and Barad's ethico-onto-epistemology, using the Mosaic tiles as a material-discursive apparatus and nomadic ethics as the accountability framework. That convergence is possible precisely because the Mosaic Approach was already doing diffractive practice before the label existed. This is the same pattern the document has traced elsewhere: practice arrives at the ground that theory then names. The Mosaic Approach is another instance of the argument that what posthumanism and new materialism discovered was not new — what they did was finally refuse to suppress it.

The convergence of these protocols under the umbrella — Barad's apparatus work, Haraway's figuration, Braidotti's transpositions, Bennett's perceptual retraining, Lambert's five entry points, Hill's two modes, Martin et al.'s six practices, Clark and Moss's mosaic listening — is not accidental. It is the signature of a shared ontological ground. Each arrived at similar structures from different domains, different vocabularies, and different institutional contexts. What they share is the refusal to treat knowledge as extraction from a pre-given world, and the insistence that accountability is located in the specific, specifiable apparatus of the inquiry rather than in correspondence to an external canon of truth. Research does not need to be checked against some outside verifiable standard. It needs internal consistency — coherence with its own ontological commitments and accountability to the apparatus, the cuts, and the marks it leaves on bodies (Militsi, 2021; Timeto, 2011).

*Pasted from comps v35, What is Research?, pp. 49–51 (mostly cut from the final)*

Fox and Alldred (2021) define research as an assemblage made of tools, recording and analysis technologies, software and hardware, theoretical frameworks, literatures, researchers, editors, ethics committees, end users, and the data outputs — where outputs "derive from the multiple affects between these human and non-human materialities." Under an agential realist account, this definition of research gains additional precision. Phenomena --- the basic units of reality for Barad --- "are not the mere result of laboratory exercises engineered by human subjects. Nor can the apparatuses that produce phenomena be understood as observational devices or mere laboratory instruments" (Barad, 2003, p. 815). Research is not the neutral observation of pre-existing objects but the co-constitution of phenomena through specific material-discursive arrangements. The apparatus is not separate from what it produces; it is part of the phenomenon itself. This means that the decisions a researcher makes --- what apparatus to build, what cut to enact, what to attend to and what to exclude --- are not methodological conveniences but ontological acts. Research, on this account, is world-making.

Agency, correspondingly, is not something the researcher possesses and applies. "Agency is a matter of intra-acting; it is an enactment, not something that someone or something has" (Barad, 2003, p. 826). It is "about the possibilities and accountability entailed in reconfiguring material-discursive apparatuses of bodily production including the boundary articulations and exclusions that are marked by those practices in the enactment of a causal structure." Research as assemblage --- the Fox & Alldred formulation --- and research as agential intra-action --- the Baradian formulation --- are naming the same condition from different angles: the researcher is inside the phenomenon, not above it.

Rheinberger frames research as an "epistemic encounter" within experimental systems where the object of inquiry is not fully known in advance --- what he calls "epistemic things" --- and where research trajectories are non-teleological: driven from behind rather than oriented toward a predetermined omega (Rheinberger, Raw Flows, p. 17). Studios and laboratories are, in this account, comparable working environments --- not identical, but analogous in their capacity to allow the fruitful interaction between the known and the not-yet-known that characterises research proper. What becomes knowable is not independent of the material arrangements that make inquiry possible; it depends entirely on how instruments transform matter to render phenomena accessible. In X-ray crystallography, fluid biological material must be made crystalline to enter the research regime. In electron microscopy, vacuum conditions force liquids to evaporate and require elaborate preparation that produces replicas rather than originals. The boundary-work --- wet/dry, hard/soft, living/nonliving --- is not incidental to the research; it is where the research happens. For new materialist art practice as research, the implication is immediate: the studio is not a private expressive room waiting to be supplemented by scholarly commentary. It is an experimental system that materially conditions what can appear, be handled, and be known.

Moñivas Mayor pushes this apparatus logic into explicitly studio-based terrain by arguing that when the object of inquiry is fluid processes and fluid imaginations, method must itself become fluid. The studio/lab, in her account, is an ecology: a permeable environment of maps, notes, vessels, instruments, and experiments always in readiness, where the methodological inventory includes testing observation points, researching theoretical sources, experimenting with documentation formats (field notebook, sketch, photo, video), and long durations of waiting, meals, and walks that contextualize what gets produced (Moñivas Mayor, Raw Flows, p. 112). Her key methodological claim is that fluid rhythms and unpredictability do not merely complicate research --- they reshape how practice must be conceptualized and how it must be analyzed. She draws on the Deborah number to show why conventional observation regimes produce "solids" out of processes: normal timeframes make a solid of any substance, which forces a rethinking of object-centered epistemologies (Moñivas Mayor, Raw Flows, pp. 112--114). The studio/lab becomes the place where this crisis is lived as a research condition --- learning to look and listen from the blurry margins of rationality rather than resolving it through abstraction. Crucially, she treats accidents as epistemic events: a spill produces an unanticipated form that becomes a new priority and redirects the inquiry. Rather than eliminating such noise, she argues for methodological distance from exactness to admit surprise, play, and reflection, insisting that analysis must combine documentation with tool and material knowledge and imagination (Moñivas Mayor, Raw Flows, p. 122). Her culminating formulation names the whole laboratory as the epistemic medium itself: a "condenser of flows and mutations" and a "catalyst of imagination, experimentation and knowledge." The studio/lab is not where research happens and is then written up. It is the apparatus through which research happens at all.

Andersen introduces the practice of marking from the medium of dance. Marking makes a record of the movement of the dancer's body through time/space/matter so that the movement may be studied, collected, shared, and recreated later. The studio and workshop space that houses this kind of research is what Moñivas Mayor (2016) calls a flux condenser — a site that gathers drifting phenomena, sounds, material behaviors, and atmospheric conditions, and converts them into creative-research movements. It is not documentation of practice but practice itself as a condensing apparatus for knowing-with materials. The condenser does not contain the flow; it intensifies it until something becomes perceptible that was not perceptible before.

Andersen names the performative moment of making as a fog chamber: the material-discursive arrangement where agential cuts are made through the intra-action of apparatus, subject, object, and time. The fog chamber creates what she calls a possibility space — the conditions under which something new might become thinkable. Making these conditions requires a mentally and emotionally fluid environment, where the researcher risks coming undone through the process of making strange (Adorno's courage of the clown), and where a "chance of becoming human" is created rather than assumed (Andersen, citing Butler, 2005). Minh-ha's break without which meaning would be fixed and truth congealed (Trinh, 1990, p. 77) — between the concept and the material, between what was known before the encounter and what becomes thinkable through it.

The boundaries of the experiment are set not by the researcher's prior plan but through the agency and liveness of the material pushing back on the limitations of skill, imagination, and time. The boundaries are redrawn through the presentation of the object to others and through subsequent play, where the participants define the play space through improvisation, listening, and the creation of the rules of the game as the game is played. Britton, Klumbyte, and Draude name this directly in *Doing thinking*: artistic research and new materialism together reposition research away from object-production toward making situations, sites, and worldings — the elements do not pre-exist the situation but emerge within it (explicitly Baradian). Research here is not the production of a stable object that then gets analyzed. It is the creation of a situation in which phenomena can appear that could not have been predicted in advance.

### 3.6 Reading the traces

*Pasted from comps v35, Differences That Make a Difference, pp. 45–46 (cut from the final)*

Diffraction is the mapping of effects of difference rather than the locating of difference in things (Barad, 2003). This distinction — between difference as a property of objects and difference as the pattern produced by their intra-action — is what separates diffractive practice from comparative analysis. Comparison asks: how do these things differ? Diffraction asks: what interference pattern is produced when these things are read through one another, and what does that pattern make visible that neither could produce alone?

Bateson's formulation is the methodological anchor: information is "a difference that makes a difference" (1972, p. 459). Information is not content extracted from a source; it is the relational event of one difference registering as significant within another system. This is the same claim Barad makes about phenomena: the boundaries and properties of things are not given in advance but enacted through specific material-discursive arrangements. The diffraction pattern does not show what the things are. It shows what the encounter produces — which includes what it forecloses.

For research practice this means the unit of analysis is never a single text, dataset, or object in isolation. It is always the encounter: what happens when this theoretical framework is read through this practice, when this body of work is read through that ontological commitment, when this site is held against that history. The pattern that emerges from the encounter is where the knowledge lives. Bennett's actants, Barad's phenomena, Haraway's situated knowledges, Braidotti's transpositions — each names a version of the same methodological ground: the differences that matter are the ones the encounter produces, not the ones the researcher brings to it already formed.

*Pasted from comps v35, Bennett, Braidotti, Barad: Ontology, p. 35: the diffractive practice notebook (cut from the final)*

For Barad, phenomena -- unique material discursive arrangements -- are the primary building block of reality. Agential realism is her term for how this reality works: phenomena are the basic unity formed through the frame of an apparatus to create intra-actions of things through material discursive arrangements. Barad created the term "intra-action" as a response to the inadequate and misleading term interaction, which implies a pre-given body with inherent qualities. Intra-action defines agency as a mutually constituted, distributed, emergent force (Barad, 2007, p. 141). Apparatuses are phenomena, not unique stable things, constituted as "specific agential practices... through which specific exclusionary boundaries are enacted," and as "dynamic (re)configurings of the world." As each apparatus is a unique material-discursive arrangement, so are the cuts they make, drawing unique distinctions between subject/object/instrument. Different marks on bodies indicate configurations of apparatus.

Objectivity can only be obtained through accountability to the boundaries we made through the agential cut and the marks we left on bodies. Barad rejects the "God's trick" highlighted by Haraway (1988, p. 581), that outcomes or measurements are ever neutral. These commitments generate a set of orienting questions that function as a diffractive practice notebook — not a checklist but a recursive accountability structure the researcher returns to throughout the inquiry: What is the apparatus — the specific material-discursive arrangement through which this phenomenon is being produced? What agential cut does it enact — what boundaries does it draw, what relata does it constitute? What/who does the cut exclude — what is rendered invisible or inaudible by this specific configuration? What marks are left on bodies — what material traces does the encounter produce in the researcher, the participants, the site, the objects? These questions are answerable. They do not demand a generalizable finding. They demand specification of the apparatus and accountability for its cuts — which is a more rigorous standard than the demand for a transferable conclusion, because it names what the specific encounter actually produced rather than what a decontextualized claim extracted from it might assert.

### 3.7 Ethics

*Pasted from comps v35, A Shared Commitment to an Affirmative Ethics, pp. 33–34 (reduced in the final)*

Bennett, Braidotti, and Barad share an ethical orientation that follows directly from their ontology — which is to say, ethics in these frameworks is not a set of principles applied to research from outside but the practical consequence of taking seriously what the ontology says about the world. If agency is distributed, if matter is vibrant, if phenomena are constituted through intra-action — then the ethical question is not "what are the rules" but "what does this specific configuration produce and who and what does it include or foreclose." The three share a name for this stance: affirmative ethics. Not optimism, and not the suspension of critique, but the refusal to let critique be the final word — the insistence that diagnosis must be followed by construction, that naming what is wrong must open toward imagining what could be otherwise.

Bennett's ethics are grounded in the understanding that if humans are also things, then what we do to things, we do to ourselves. This is not a sentimental claim. It is the ethical consequence of the ontology: if agency is distributed across the assemblage, if matter is vibrant and generative rather than inert, then the human is not the only ethical agent in the configuration and cannot be the only ethical addressee. Bennett hopes through this realization that humans will decenter themselves and take more care in their situatedness in the world and ecology. What this produces methodologically is the practice of attentiveness -- lingering long enough in the encounter with matter that the thing's own vitality becomes perceptible, and allowing that perception to reorient the ethical imagination toward nonhuman agency and distributed consequence.

Barad's ethics are inseparable from her epistemology and ontology — the hyphenation ethico-onto-epistemology is not decorative. The agential cut is simultaneously a cognitive act (it produces determinate phenomena from indeterminate fields), a material act (it leaves marks on bodies), and an ethical act (it forecloses what it does not produce, and the researcher is response-able for those constitutive exclusions). Accountability, in this framework, is located precisely in the cut: naming the apparatus, specifying what it enacts, tracing the marks it leaves. The researcher who does this is doing ethics in the Baradian sense — not as a separate activity from research but as the same activity seen from the angle of responsibility. For Braidotti, the raison d'etre is affirmative ethics. The ontological grounding for Braidotti's affirmative ethics is zoe -- life as a cosmic force that exceeds and precedes any individual subject. The subject is "an autopoietic machine fuelled by targeted perceptions" functioning as "the echoing chamber of zoe." This nonanthropocentric view "expresses both a profound love for Life as a cosmic force and the desire to depersonalize subjective life-and-death. This is just one life my life. The life in 'me' does not answer to my name: 'I' is just passing" (Braidotti, 2010, p. 211). Affirmative ethics is not optimism. It emerges from this radical depersonalization: because the individual "I" is not the center of life but a temporary passing of it, the logic of retribution, rights-claims, and compensation -- all premised on a stable individual subject -- dissolves. What replaces it is an "ethics of affirmation" that "suspends the quest for both claims and compensation and resists the logic of retribution or rights" (Braidotti, 2010, p. 214). The wound is real. The response is to depersonalize it, move through it, and affirm the generative force of life continuing beyond it. Bennett names the same insistence from a different angle: critique without construction is the explicit failure she positions vibrant materialism against. The political ecology of things is not a theory of what is wrong with the world. It is a practice of attending to what is generative in it — enchantment as the perceptual ground for a politics that can imagine differently rather than only diagnose more precisely.

The three ethics converge on the same refusal: the refusal of a sovereign subject at the center of the ethical universe, and the insistence that accountability is distributed across the assemblage, enacted through the apparatus, and oriented toward the generative force of life rather than the stable claims of the individual. This is what it means for ethics to follow from ontology rather than being imposed on it from outside.

*Pasted from comps v35, Bennett, Braidotti, Barad: Ontology, p. 36: the relativism objection (reduced in the final)*

The move from representationalism to relational ontology consistently generates one objection: that in rejecting absolutes, posthumanism and new materialism embrace relativism. If there is no view from nowhere, if knowledge is always situated, if the apparatus is always part of what it produces -- then how is any account better than any other? This is the charge that sank postmodernism's ethical project, and both Barad and Braidotti are aware of it. The answer runs through three moves made in sequence.

The first is Hayles's constrained constructivism (1997): representations are neither mirrors of an independent reality nor purely arbitrary constructions. They are consistent or inconsistent with what Hayles calls "the flux" -- the real but unreachable ground of things. Consistency means local, interactive, instrumentally efficacious engagement; it replaces the true/false binary with a not-true/not-false relation that "rides the cusp" between the world as it is and the world as we can construct it (Hayles, 1997, cited in Timeto, 2011, p. 157). This is not relativism -- it is accountability to the constraints the world imposes on representation, even when those constraints cannot be specified in advance.

The second is Haraway's situated knowledge: the partial perspective is not a weaker objectivity but a stronger one, because it is accountable for the position from which it sees. The god-trick -- the view from nowhere -- is not neutral; it is a specific and interested position that refuses accountability for the cuts it makes. Situated knowledge names the cut and owns it.

The third is Barad's agential cut as the locus of ethical accountability. The cut is not arbitrary -- it is specified by the apparatus, produces determinate marks on bodies, and can be described, compared, and held accountable. Braidotti's nomadic cartography enacts the same move at the level of subject formation: the nomadic account is grounded not in a universal standard but in the specific political and material coordinates of the subject's location. Neither framework produces relativism. Both produce accountability without universalism -- which is precisely what the ethico-onto-epistemological framework names: responsibility is located in the intra-action, not outsourced to an external canon.

The three questions resolve into a single arrangement. Posthumanism and new materialism are twinned frames whose combined force dismantles the Cartesian-Newtonian settlement that made inert matter and the sovereign rational subject the default ground of knowledge. Diffractive practice, transpositions, and making strange are the methodologies that follow when that ground is refused: methods that read through rather than against, that produce new material rather than reflecting pre-given content, and that suspend perceptual habit long enough for the nonhuman to become perceptible. New materialist art practice as research is what happens when these ontological and methodological commitments are enacted by practitioners whose medium is the apparatus through which the research is conducted.

The figure that emerges is not the detached researcher but the artist-researcher as horizontal actant within the apparatus, sharing distributed agency with the materials, instruments, bodies, and institutions that constitute the research phenomenon. Her transposition framework, mediated through her practice, produces the research artifact as evidence for other perceiving entities. What the artifact evidences is not a prior world that the apparatus revealed but the specific arrangement through which the encounter was produced and the specific exclusions that arrangement enacted. Accountability to the cut replaces the claim to stand outside the phenomenon.

What this changes for artistic research is the location of its authority. Artistic research does not need theoretical permission from philosophy to count as knowledge production. The Trinh-Haraway-Barad genealogy traced in Part Two restores an artist-practitioner to the root of a concept typically attributed to science studies, and the practitioner accounts in Parts Two and Three show artists already producing the ontological claims the philosophy of matter would later formalize. Art practice is not a later application of new materialist thought. It is one of the milieus in which the call to rethink matter arose.

The commitments the document has traced are not positions to defend but conditions to inhabit. Each of the artist-researchers gathered in Part Three inhabits them differently; each refuses a different separation that traditional method would impose. What they share is the orientation the document has named throughout: generativity as refusal of exhaustive capture. The work is research when it keeps the object open to further intra-action. The work is done when someone else picks it up and makes something of it that the apparatus could not predict.

Fox & Alldred also provide an essential caution: diffractive methodology can become highly researcher-centric and context-dependent if analytic cuts are treated as interpretive license rather than accountable apparatus-work. They note that DM may be "researcher-centric and context-dependent" and that this raises concerns about transferability and applicability in policy/practice contexts.

Their abstract similarly points to the challenge of elucidating how analytic diffractions influence findings.

This caution is not a reason to abandon diffractive practice. It is a reason to insist on the strictest version of diffractive accountability: specify the apparatus, specify the cuts, and trace the effects, so that situatedness does not become a free pass and context sensitivity does not become methodological opacity.

Van der Tuin and Dolphijn warn against a second risk: that bringing new materialism into a discipline as if both are pre-existing tends to undo transversality, producing "inter- rather than intra-acting" relations and reinstalling a transcendental schism.

The methodological demand this raises is not abstract. If diffractive practice must be genuinely transversal rather than merely interdisciplinary, then applying it to the specific domain of art practice as research requires rethinking not just what art does but what research is. What counts as evidence, what counts as an apparatus, what counts as knowledge, and who counts as a researcher when the researcher is also a maker --- these are the questions Part Three takes up.

Fox & Alldred (2021, applied research) warn that diffractive methodology can become "researcher-centric and context-dependent" because the analytical cuts are driven by the analyst's decisions, raising issues of bias and transferability. They also note that ethico-onto-epistemology alone may not supply insight into "how or to what extent" specific methods influenced findings.

(Fox & Alldred, 2021)

This push back elucidates the central problem of art practice as research within traditional institutions. Critique of APAR methods misunderstand that the essential nature and foundational tenants do not comport with the structure and desires of traditional research and reporting of research findings.

Critique of diffractive practice because of the potential for bias and researcher centeredness, missed the necessity of the researcher experience investigated horizontally alongside the other participants in the assemblage/apparatus. The goal is not to erase the human, but to flatten the field and make space for things/matter/material.

The charge of researcher-centrism is itself a representationalist criticism. It presupposes that a properly objective method would be one from which the researcher has been removed --- a view from outside the phenomenon, a neutral apparatus that doesn't contaminate what it measures. But as Barad makes clear, representationalism is "a prisoner of the problematic metaphysics it postulates. Like the frustrated would-be runner in Zeno's paradox, it never seems to be able to get any closer to solving the problem it poses because it is caught in the impossibility of stepping outward from its metaphysical starting place" (Barad, 2003, p. 803). The demand that diffractive methodology demonstrate its objectivity by removing the researcher from the apparatus is not a stricter standard of rigor --- it is a return to the very representationalist fantasy that diffractive practice is designed to refuse. Accountability in diffractive practice does not come from removing the researcher. It comes from naming the apparatus, specifying the cuts, and tracing the marks left on bodies. That is what objectivity means when the observer is understood to be always already part of the phenomenon.

Draude and Klumbytė add a further dimension to this pushback by warning against idealizing collaborative or hybrid research spaces as neutral zones. "Third spaces" and hybrid methodologies must retain their postcolonial, decolonial, and feminist stakes, including sustained attention to power relations and positionality (Draude & Klumbytė, 2019). Their insistence that genuinely hybrid methodologies may need to be created --- rather than simply importing methods from one domain into another --- is both methodological and political: it rejects extractive method transfer and demands the accountable co-constitution of methods inside hybrid research spaces. This applies directly to the critique of diffractive methodology as researcher-centric: the researcher's situatedness, positionality, and power are not contaminants to be removed from the apparatus, but constitutive conditions to be named, examined, and held accountable. Removing the researcher does not produce objectivity; it produces the illusion of objectivity while concealing the specific human-nonhuman assemblage that generated the findings.

The necessity of explicitly tying specific methods to research insights is ignoring the emergent causality of the field and that the entanglement of becoming/knowing/doing is a feature, not a bug.
