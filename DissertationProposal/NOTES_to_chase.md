## 1. The Deleuze Seminars, Purdue

**https://deleuze.cla.purdue.edu/lecture/lecture-01-7/**

The Deleuze Seminars project at Purdue (Daniel W. Smith and Charles J. Stivale, with Timothy S. Murphy and others): transcripts and English translations of Deleuze's Vincennes and Saint-Denis lectures, 1971-1987, free and searchable by seminar, date and topic.

**Why it is worth searching rather than reading.** Deleuze says things in the lectures that he does not say in the books, and says them in the plain register of a man teaching a room. For terms that the published work leaves implicit, the lectures are often where the working definition is.

Search it for:

| Term | Why |
|---|---|
| ***agencement*** | **Section 2.5.** This is the one. Deleuze defines and redefines it in the seminars, in French, repeatedly, without Massumi's translation in the way. If the "assemblage" translation lost something, the lectures are where the loss is visible |
| **the fold / *le pli*** | The Leibniz seminars, 1986-87, run directly into *The Fold*. For the gut-mannerism material and the Baroque |
| **individuation, Simondon** | Underneath Manning's whole vocabulary |
| **the diagram** | Manning's chapter 6 and the Deligny tracings |
| **haecceity, the event, the virtual** | 1.5 and 2.1 |
| **painting, Bacon, the figure** | The 1981 painting seminars, for 2.3 |

Note the seminar you linked is numbered `lecture-01-7`, so check which seminar series it belongs to before citing; the site organises by seminar, and lecture numbering repeats across series.

**Citation caution.** These are transcripts of speech, translated by volunteers, and the site says so. Cite them as lecture transcripts with the seminar name, date and translator, not as Deleuze's published prose, and do not let a transcript carry an argument on its own.

### The Hayles book, now held

> **N. Katherine Hayles, *The Cosmic Web: Scientific Field Models and Literary Strategies in the Twentieth Century*. Ithaca: Cornell University Press, 1984.**
> ISBN 0-8014-1742-2. LC 84-45141.

`K:\OneDrive\UNM\Dissertation\todo October 26\Hayles_N_Katherine_The_Cosmic_Web_Scientific_Field_Models_and_Literary_Strategies_in_the_Twentieth_Century_1984.pdf`

209 PDF pages, 78,802 words, a full text layer already present, so no OCR was needed. Written when she was at Rolla, Missouri, before *Chaos Bound* (1990) and *How We Became Posthuman* (1999). *How We Became Posthuman* is already in Comps, and it names this book on its own author page.

**Structure.** Preface p. 9, Introduction p. 15, then Part I Mathematical and Scientific Models, one chapter: 1. "Spinning the Web: Representative Field Theories and Their Implications" p. 31. Then Part II Literary Strategies, five chapters: 2. Pirsig, *Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance* p. 63; 3. "Evasion: The Field of the Unconscious in D. H. Lawrence" p. 85; 4. Nabokov, *Ada* p. 111; 5. Borges p. 138; 6. Pynchon, *Gravity's Rainbow* p. 168. References p. 199, Index p. 205. Hayles says the literary chapters are ordered by **ascending resistance** to the field concept, and that "none of these writers adopts the field concept simplemindedly or wholeheartedly."

**What the field concept is, in her words.**

> "In this book I have singled out the 'field concept' as the theme that is at the heart of this revolution, and have examined its various manifestations in the models of physics and mathematics, the theories of the philosophy of science and linguistics, and the structure and strategies of literary texts." (Preface, p. 9)

> "The field concept, as I use the term, is not identical with any single field formulation in science... The term 'field concept,' by contrast, draws from many different models those features that are isomorphic, and hence that are characteristic of twentieth-century thought in general." (p. 9)

> "Perhaps most essential to the field concept is the notion that things are interconnected... In marked contrast to the atomistic Newtonian idea of reality, in which physical objects are discrete and events are capable of being isolated, [the field view] pictures objects, events, and observer as belonging inextricably to the same field; the disposition of each, in this view, is influenced... by the disposition of the others." (pp. 9-10)

The second feature she names is **the self-referentiality of language**: because everything in the field view is connected by the medium of language, and language is itself part of the field, "every statement potentially refers to every other statement, including itself."

**The web is chosen over the dance, and it is made, not found.** This is the passage that matters most for the ontology layer:

> "The stickiness of this situation, our inability to extricate the object of our description from the description itself, suggests that a more appropriate image for the field concept than the 'cosmic dance' is the 'cosmic web.'... Readers who desire a visual image to go along with the metaphor may imagine it as a network of strands coextensive with space. Note that the web is not space itself, nor does it 'contain' space. Rather **it is an artifact, a created object whose artificiality corresponds to the conceptualization of the field models it signifies**; what we are concerned with in these models is not reality..." (Introduction, p. 20)

She is explicit that she calls it a **metaphor** (44 uses of metaphor in the book), and that it is an artifact rather than a description of what is there. That is the opposite move from Braidotti, who insists her figurations are not metaphors.

The epigraphs to the Introduction are Saussure ("Tout se tient"), Heisenberg ("What we observe is not nature in itself but nature exposed to our method of questioning"), and Whorf.

**She also refuses the one-way causal claim about her own argument:** "Rather, the literature is an imaginative response to complexities and ambiguities that are implicit in the models but that are often not explicitly recognized... In this sense the literature is as much an influence on the scientific models as the models are on the literature."

Their materiality, their glaring bodies must somehow sink and disappear from the field of visibility.**"

The close read sets the two side by side in a table and leaves the difference standing: one account is about whether a language can be made exact, the other about what a demand for exactness does to the people who do not speak it. **Where the two readings of Borges diverge, recorded and not settled.** Hayles runs on Cantor's transfinites, where the part is as copious as the whole because the sets can be matched element for element. Deleuze cites Abraham Robinson for the monad as "**an infinite number quite different from transfinites**, as a unit surrounded by a zone of infinitely small numbers." **The Pynchon reading, in one move.** Bateson: "**Information, in the technical sense, is that which excludes certain alternatives**," and a unit of information is a difference selected out of an infinitude, selectable because "the pathways are ready to be triggered. **We may even say that the question is already implicit in them.**" Hayles's Pynchon at p. 174 says nearly the same thing ("**Meaning is made possible, indeed, defined, by its distinction from non-meaning**") and at pp. 174-175 withdraws it, because the principle of equal distance admits every difference and excludes nothing, so meaning becomes "a function not of difference but of similarity." ## 17. Hayles's "field theory of culture": who picked it up (30 Oct, Amy)

> Can you see if anyone ever picked up the Whitehead culture theory mentioned in Hayles?
> Whitehead
> Alfred whitehead

**The passage is *The Cosmic Web* (1984) p. 22**, in the Introduction, and the book's own index confirms the folio at "Whitehead, Alfred North, 22". Hayles proposes "a field notion of culture, a societal matrix which consists (in Whitehead's phrase) of a 'climate of opinion'", then writes that "**such a field theory of culture has yet to be definitively articulated, and is beyond the scope of this study**". She does not return to it.

**The answer: she picked it up herself, in *Chaos Bound* (1990), and renamed it on the way. Nobody else has.**

| When | What |
|---|---|

| 1989 | Hayles in interview: "In *The Cosmic Web* I hypothesized a cultural matrix without really looking at what it meant or how it works. In my second book, *Chaos Bound*, I've tried to go more deeply into the questions" |
