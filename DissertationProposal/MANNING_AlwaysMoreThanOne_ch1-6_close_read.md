## 2. Chapter 1 sharpens the Ivinson and Renold finding considerably

I wrote yesterday that chapter 8 attacks Tustin for the move Ivinson and Renold's choreographer makes. **Chapter 1 does the same to Bick, and it is the first thing in the book.**

It opens by laying out Bick:

> "In Esther Bick's psychoanalytic theory, the infant's relation to the world is mediated by the skin's capacity to serve as a container for experience... the infant must become 'able to hold himself together in his own "skin" in the absence of the external holding object, without spilling out and falling to bits'... myriad symptoms associated with the necessity of creating **'second skins.'**"

And then, immediately:

> "**What if the skin were not a container? What if the skin were not a limit at which self begins and ends?** What if the skin were a porous, topological surfacing of myriad potential strata that field the relation between different milieus?"

The rest of the chapter replaces Bick with Daniel Stern (72 mentions), for whom "relation is always the first principle of worlding," and then with Simondon. Manning's verdict on the psychoanalytic model:

> "While Bick's and later Ogden's psychoanalytic theories make interaction a necessity, their matrix is not relational: it always presupposes a constituted, bounded self and other."

### The citation

Ivinson and Renold 2021, on the girls folding their arms across their stomachs, write that it

> "might have signalled a need to feel a physical reassurance as a 'second skin' **(Esther Bick, 1987, cited in Manning, 2013: 2)**"

**Page 2 is the setup paragraph.** They took the concept from the page where Manning states it in order to spend the chapter refusing it, and they used it affirmatively. Not a misquotation, and not worth framing as an error, but it is exact, checkable, and it means the critique is sitting unclaimed in a book they cite.

So the lineage now reads: Bick and Tustin → Walkerdine 2010 takes them straight → Ivinson and Renold take "second skin" **through Manning** → Manning's chapters 1 and 8 are an argument against both. Three texts in your folder, one lineage, and the dissent is inside the citation.

## 3. Chapter 2, and the problem that runs through the book

Chapter 2 is Simondon: individuation (129 mentions), the preindividual, dephasing, transduction. "A body is the how of its emergence, not the what of its form."

Then this:

> "A body is black, gendered, sexed, you might say, adding that these are irrefutable givens that situate the body within the realm of fixed form. Irrefutable, yes, but only as the limit of a constellation of processes that collude to foreground one measure of how the body expresses... **The question is not 'how is the body not black or gendered or sexed?' but 'how is the body more-than the classification this singular constellation foregrounds?'**"

This is the same move she makes on autistic perception in chapter 7, now applied to race and gender: the category is granted, then dissolved into process, and the interesting question is declared to be the one about excess.

**That is the structural problem of the book for your purposes, and it is worth naming once rather than meeting it three times.** Manning's consistent gesture is to refuse the category as a ground. It is a principled refusal, aimed at pathologisation. It also means that a project built on *situated* subjectivities, where being Appalachian-born, autistic, a mother, a programmer are the ground rather than the thing to be exceeded, is doing something Manning's framework does not underwrite.

Your section 1.5 already argues that the standard new materialist genealogy omits certain positions. Manning is a clean instance of what that omission looks like in practice: Barad 0, Haraway 0, Braidotti 0, Trinh 0, and no decolonial engagement anywhere in 125,909 words. **She is the case, not the counter-case.** That is usable and does not require hostility: the point is that the field's most developed account of neurodivergent perception was built without the feminist and decolonial apparatus, and what it can and cannot do follows from that.

## 4. Chapter 3 is a film chapter, and nobody would guess it from the title

"Waltzing the Limit" is a sustained reading of **Ari Folman's *Waltz with Bashir*** (2008), the animated documentary about the Sabra and Shatila massacre. 76 mentions of the film, 31 of Folman.

It opens on a scene description and stays with the repeated image of three naked soldiers rising out of the sea, "a face that will haunt us with its detached familiarity," streets "full of mute, anguished women, women whose voices we won't hear until the very last scene."

Two reasons this matters to you. It is the chapter where Manning does **politics** rather than process philosophy, and it is a worked example of reading an artwork at length without turning it into an illustration of a theory. If 2.2 needs a model for what close attention to a work looks like in this register, this is it, and it is nothing like the diffractive readings you have been collecting.

## 5. Chapters 4 to 6: the method half

**Chapter 4** reframes the choreographic through Massumi's activist philosophy, and opens with eight competing definitions of "choreograph" and "choreography" as verb and noun, including "a way of seeing the world" and "act of interfering."

**Chapter 5** is Forsythe's *One Flat Thing, reproduced* and Arakawa and Gins's "architectural body." The concept to take is **cueing**:

> "The cue is an enabling constraint for structured improvisation opening onto the collective individuation not of an individual movement but of the body of the dance as a whole."

> "'When you stop, create silence. **Do not stop individually**,' Forsythe warns."

And the everyday example, which is the clearest sentence in the chapter: two people cooking, reaching past each other for the grater and the fork without colliding, "dancing the interval of the decisions as they realign your cooking bodies."

**Chapter 6** opens, with no preamble, on a numbered procedure:

> *Step 1: Lie down on the floor. Close your eyes.*
> *Step 2: Begin to create a diagram of the space. Allow the diagram to settle.*
> *Step 3: Find an open space in the diagram and move into it, virtually.*
> ...
> *Step 11: When your movement slows to a standstill and the diagram has solidified, slowly open your eyes.*

Then it analyses what the procedure does rather than what it means. **There are 15 such numbered procedures in the book.**

This is the formal thing worth stealing. A method written as a score to be enacted, printed in the text, with the analysis following the doing. For a chapter 3 that has to show rather than describe a practice, that is a usable form, and it comes from a Duke University Press monograph rather than from an arts-based-research manual.

## 6. The interlude on participatory art, which is a caution for your own method

"Fiery, Luminous, Scary" is about the invitation to touch in participatory art, and it is the most directly self-critical thing in the first half:

> "despite calls made toward open-endedness and process (understood as positive effects of a generative event) **the call to touch is never straightforward**. For it is a call, and like all calls it asks that it be listened to in certain ways, that it elicit behavior considered manageable under the conditions of the work."

> "**Touch, but touch this way!** No matter how carefully crafted, no participatory art event is ever completely exempt from this imbrication of touch with the expectations that accompany it."

> "This is the risk any artist takes: that the call to touch will expose itself as an all-or-nothing proposition, that the participant will feel burdened by the event rather than excited by it, that instead of setting new conditions for experimentation, a failure will be experienced from the outset, **a failure to have known in advance what it means to touch**."

Set this beside the nine-item test in `Posthuman_FirstPerson_Anthropocentrism_Notes.md`. It is the same suspicion aimed at openness itself: a method that announces it is not capturing data is still issuing instructions. Worth holding against the Ivinson and Renold claim that theirs is "not about deploying pre-conceived, arts-based methods for 'data' that is waiting to be captured," and against your own method when it makes a similar promise.

## 7. The other interlude worth having

"Dancing the Virtual" gives the cleanest statement of **technique** in the book, and technique is its most-used term (138):

> "Think technique not as an add-on to a preexisting body-form but as **a process of bodying**. Think technique as an in-forming of a mutating body."

And the example, which is doing real work and not decoration:

> "you are in the garden, your knees covered in mud, hands deep in the earth for an early spring planting. Instead of seeing the earth as a quality apart from the knee attached to a preexisting human form, see the **knee-hand-earth** as a worlding, a force of form, an operative ecology."

## 9. Cautions

**Chapters 3, 4 and 5 are specialist.** *Waltz with Bashir*, Forsythe's choreography and Arakawa and Gins's architecture are each treated at length and on their own terms. If none of those is going into the dissertation, the chapters are background rather than source.

**Simondon is assumed, not taught.** 38 mentions, and the vocabulary of dephasing and transduction is used from chapter 2 onward as though settled. Reading chapter 2 without Simondon in hand will be slow.

**Still no Barad, Haraway, Braidotti, diffraction, intra-action or *agencement*, anywhere in the first half either.** The zeros reported yesterday hold across the whole book.

**Research-creation appears twice in 125,909 words.** Despite the practice-heavy content and Manning's own institutional position, this is not a book about research-creation or practice as research. It is philosophy that takes practices as its material.
