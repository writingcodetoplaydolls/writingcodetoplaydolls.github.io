# The proposed alternatives to critique, looked at properly

29 Oct 2026. Follows the one-paragraph list in note 2 of Ashley Barnwell's "Method Matters: The Ethics of Exclusion", in Kirby (ed.), *What if Culture was Nature all Along?* (2017), pp. 26-47. Nine proposals, all offered as replacements for critique. Full citations below are transcribed from Barnwell's own reference list, so they are her bibliography rather than mine, and none of the items is held.

**Mess as method first, since it is the only one that is not a reading method.** The other eight are proposals about how to read a text. Law's is about how to do research, which makes it the one that bears on 2.2 rather than on a literature review.

## 1. Mess as method. John Law (2004)

**Law, J. (2004), *After Method: Mess in Social Science Research*, New York: Routledge.** International Library of Sociology. Law was Professor of Sociology and Science and Technology Studies at Lancaster, later at the Open University ([oro.open.ac.uk/22040](https://oro.open.ac.uk/22040/)); borrowable copy at the [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/aftermethodmessi0000lawj). The book itself is **not held**.

> **UPDATED 29 Oct. The argument is readable without the book.** Law put the chapter-length version online himself: **"Making a Mess with Method"**, the paper that became part of *After Method*, drafted as "After Method 3", 17 March 2003, and published by the Centre for Science Studies, Lancaster, on 20 December 2003: [lancaster.ac.uk PDF](https://www.lancaster.ac.uk/fass/resources/sociology-online-papers/papers/law-making-a-mess-with-method.pdf). A later draft, "After Method 4", 19 January 2006, is on his own site at heterogeneities.net, and he asks that citations of it give the version date and download date. The published version is in **William Outhwaite and Stephen P. Turner (eds), *The Sage Handbook of Social Science Methodology* (Sage, 2007), pp. 595-606.** The 2003 PDF is in hand and read; what follows is from it, not from the publisher's blurb.

### What it argues, from the paper

He opens on a collage with a caption that is the whole argument as a question: **"If this is an awful mess ... then would something less messy make a mess of describing it?"**

> "My intuition, to say it quickly, is that the world is largely messy. It is also that contemporary social science methods are hopelessly bad at knowing that mess. Indeed it is that dominant approaches to method work with some success to repress the very possibility of mess."

His target is **method as hygiene**: "Do your methods properly. Eat your epistemological greens. Wash your hands after mixing with the real world. Then you will lead the good research life. Your data will be clean." And his aim is to imagine method "if it were not caught in an obsession with clarity, with specificity, and with the definite."

**The sentence that answers the sloppiness worry**: "Clarity doesn't help. **Disciplined lack of clarity**, that may be what we need."

### The empirical case, which is a research failure told honestly

With Vicky Singleton he was commissioned by a hospital trust to map the "typical trajectories" of patients with alcoholic liver disease through the health system. Two things went wrong.

The trajectories would not map onto each other. His example: an alcohol advice centre that could only be attended by appointment and only by people who were sober, which many in the hospital believed was a drop-in centre. "There was, so to speak, no 'system'."

And the object would not hold still. Commissioned to study ALD, they found themselves talking about liver disease in general, then alcoholic cirrhosis, then alcohol abuse, then alcoholism, then quality of life. "What on earth, we wondered, was it that we were actually studying? Why couldn't we hold it still? Why did it keep on going out of focus?"

**Then the part worth having.** The first response was self-blame: "Why were we such shoddy researchers? . . . We certainly quite often felt that we were failing and weren't up to scratch. As time went on, however, we started to be kinder to ourselves." The second was the conclusion that the object was "a fluid object. Or even one that was ephemeral in any given form, flipping from one configuration to another, **dancing like a flame**."

So the two available responses to a vague finding: "**methodological moralising**. That things should be clear, either because they needed to be put right, or because they really were clear all along and our methods weren't understanding them." Or: "things are at least sometimes vague and can only be known vaguely."

### The five commitments, which are a checklist

The most operational thing in the paper. Law unpacks common-sense realism into five separable commitments, and insists they are usually taken as a package.

| | |
|---|---|
| 1. **Primitive out-thereness** | There is a reality out there, and nothing more specific than that. **Law holds this himself** |
| 2. **Independence** | What is out there is substantially independent of our actions and perceptions |
| 3. **Anteriority** | What is real "in general precedes any attempt to know it" |
| 4. **Definiteness** | What is out there is definite. "If findings are vague then it isn't reality that is vague, but those doing the research. They've failed" |
| 5. **Singularity** | The world is a single reality held in common. **Not** a claim about perspectives: "it is about ontology, about what is real, what is out there." Cites Annemarie Mol, *The Body Multiple* |

> "most of what we think of as research methods in social science are committed to the full package. In practice research methods don't buy into realism à la carte. It is the full set menu, or nothing. And as you can tell, I think this ought to change."

His own answers after the poststructuralist detour: independent, no; prior, no; definite, not necessarily; singular, no. "Realities can be made independent, prior, definite and singular, but that is because they are being made that way. It could be otherwise." And he is explicit that this is not relativism: "No doubt different out-therenesses overlap and interfere with one another . . . making them is extremely hard work."

**A method can be audited against these five.** That is far more usable than the word "mess", and it is a page of a free PDF.

### Two kinds of absence, and four styles of Othering

The second operational part, and it bears directly on this folder's absence findings.

Making something present necessarily makes something else absent, and there are two kinds:

- **Manifest absence**: "what presence acknowledges. Makes manifest." Absent but explicit
- **Otherness**: "absence that is not acknowledged". And, logically, "Otherness cannot be brought to presence and listed. But we can hint, or we can look at other practices and notice out-therenesses that they don't acknowledge"

Four styles of Othering, which read as a checklist of what a corpus study drops:

1. **the invisible work** that helps to make a research report
2. **the uninteresting**, "everything that seems to be not worth telling"
3. **the obvious**, "things that everyone is taken to know"
4. **the repressed**, and what is repressed is "everything that doesn't fit the standard package of common-sense realism"

His logical core: "Independence depends on lack of independence. Anteriority depends on simultaneity. The definite depends on the vague. And the singular depends on the multiple. Both are there. Both are always there. **The only question is this: how do we choose to handle them?**"

He also names the target directly: "the all-knowing subject. The all-seeing eye. **The all-representing database.**" And he does not exempt himself: "What I am doing does not, of course, escape this logic."

Other things to keep: **Helen Verran's "disconcertment"**, which he borrows for "imagining methods that live . . . with disconcertment"; **"an ontological politics"**, since "Realities are not fixed in concrete . . . they might be otherwise"; and the diagnosis of the realist sleight of hand, that "Good method creates a reliable representational conduit from reality to depiction. It is a one-way street. Nature is made to speak for itself. End of story." The book he was writing when he gave this paper was provisionally called *Method Assemblage*.

### The precursor, found 29 Oct: Pickering's mangle, 1995

Law is not the first. **Andrew Pickering, *The Mangle of Practice* (1995)**, quoted at length in Hekman's chapter of *Material Feminisms* (which is held), makes the same argument nine years earlier and about **instruments** rather than about social science:

- "Scientists are human agents in a field of material agency which they struggle to capture in machines. Further, human and material agency are reciprocally and emergently intertwined in this struggle . . . the reciprocal tuning of human and material agency, tuning that can itself reconfigure human intentions" (1995: 21)
- The **mangle** as both noun and verb, for the transformative process and for what it does to the contours of agency (23)
- "the world makes us in the same process by which we make the world" (26)
- Nature's **resistances** occur "in the boundary of the two realms", neither in the instruments nor in the concepts (92)
- "mangles mix everything up and produce unpredictable results"
- **"Mangle realism"**: "how the material world is leaks into and infects our representations of it in a nontrivial and consequential fashion" (183)

**For a practice-based dissertation Pickering may be the better of the two**, because his mess is about what happens between a maker and a machine, and his "resistances" sit exactly where the comps grating sits. Tuana's chapter uses his **"dance of agency"** as well, so he is in two chapters of a held book.

### Why this is the one to read

**Mess as method is not a licence for untidiness.** It is the claim that insisting on clarity misrepresents a reality that is not clear, and that choosing a method is choosing which reality to bring into being. That is the same argument as three things already in this folder:

| | |
|---|---|
| **The skin prick test** | Jamieson (2017): the grid printed on skin "manufactures a set of circumstances" that can cause the sensitisation it measures |
| **The 3 Standard Stoppages** | Molderings: chance-made standard metres, measurement producing its object |
| **Plotnitsky's preference** | "Experiment" in place of "measurement", because measurement presumes properties that precede it |

So Law supplies the **social-science methods statement** of an argument the folder already holds in art-historical, clinical and physics versions. That is a four-way join, and it is the strongest available defence of a maximalist method: not that holding more at once is more thorough, but that a method which demands less mess would produce a different and thinner object.

**One caution.** Law is an STS sociologist, not an arts researcher, and "mess" in his usage is an argument about the ontology of the research object, not about the texture of the writing. It would be easy to cite him as permission for a loose chapter. He is not that. The claim has to be that the object is diffuse, with evidence that it is.

## 3. Sedgwick's other method statement, which is better and is not in the list

Barnwell cites Sedgwick 1997 for reparative reading. But **Bost's chapter in *Material Feminisms*** quotes a different Sedgwick, from *Tendencies* (1993), writing about her own cancer:

> "As a general principle, I don't like the idea of 'applying' theoretical models to particular situations or texts, it's always more interesting when the pressure of application goes in both directions."

And in the same passage she calls the experience of cancer, surgery, chemotherapy and hair loss "an adventure in applied deconstruction", asking "How could I have arrived at a more efficient demonstration of the supposed oppositions that structure an experience of the 'self'?" She describes cancer leaving the body in a "free-fall interpretative panic" (1993: 12-13).

**Pressure in both directions is a methods proposal, and it is the one the proposal's own structure already enacts**: the comps texts read through the practice and the practice read through the texts. It is also closer to what diffraction is for than reparative reading is. Two statements by one author, eighteen years apart, pulling different ways, and the earlier one is the more useful. Per the standing rule, that divergence is the material.

## 4. Fictocriticism, which needs its own note

The one lane here that is a **writing** practice rather than a reading protocol, and therefore the one that could do work in chapter 3.

### Origin, which is disputed

- Scott Brook (2003: 10) states that the term was first used in Australia in **Muecke, S. & King, N., "On Ficto-Criticism", *Australian Book Review* 135 (Oct 1991), pp. 13-14**
- Helen Flavell, in the UWA journal *Outskirts*, notes that the term was already in use in **Canada**, from the cultural theorist **Jeanne Randolph's** work in the 1980s, and that the Jameson interview Muecke and King cite was published there. A separate article is titled "Who Killed Jeanne Randolph?"
- **The priority is unsettled.** Record it as contested rather than giving either date

### The Australian feminist line

- **Kerr, H. & Nettelbeck, A. (eds) (1998), *The Space Between: Australian Women Writing Fictocriticism*, UWA Press.** The anthology
- **Gibbs, A. (1997), "Bodies of Words: Feminism and Fictocriticism", *TEXT***. Ties the form to feminism, and also to Canada
- **Muecke, S. (2002), "The Fall: Fictocritical Writing", *Parallax***. Works from Deleuze's distinction between concepts and percepts

### Stewart, who is the practitioner to read

**Kathleen Stewart, *Ordinary Affects* (Duke, 2007).** Written, in one account, in and as a "ficto-critical" force that aims to provoke rather than represent, and to perform an intensity of nervous attunements rather than get at the truth of a system. Built from fragments of fieldnotes, not a single argument, and it says on its first page that it is an experiment, not a judgment. Her earlier **"Cultural Poesis" (1996)** started the fragmentary style, arguing for writing that mimics the creativity of living, "as if the writing were itself a form of life". Stewart was Taussig's student.

### Why this matters here

`READING_PLAN_2026-10-27.md` records Jonker building echolalia into the form of his writing and naming it. Fictocriticism is the named genre for doing that, with a thirty-year history, an anthology, a feminist line and a disputed origin. **Nothing in this folder has named it until now.**

### Muecke 2010, read 29 Oct, and a correction to how Barnwell files it

**Muecke, S. (2010), "Public thinking, public feeling: research tools for Creative Writing", *TEXT* 14(1).** Free at textjournal.com.au/april10/muecke.htm, and as a PDF at textjournal.scholasticahq.com/article/31432.pdf, which is what was read.

**It is not primarily about fictocriticism.** Barnwell cites it in her fictocriticism cluster, and the word barely appears. It is about creative writing as research, publics, and affect. Worth knowing before citing it for the genre.

What it actually gives, and it is more useful than the genre label:

| | |
|---|---|
| **The funding problem, stated candidly** | He recounts helping a former student write an Australian Research Council application for a project whose "major outcome" would be a novel, and neither of them knew how. His solution was to put **ethnography** under the Methodology heading, having already said "fieldwork" out loud, and to argue the National Benefit through the value to readers of a book about corporate work. **A first-hand account of translating practice into fundable method language**, which is the same problem as making a making-based dissertation legible to a committee |
| **The disciplinary shift** | The rise of creative writing as a tertiary discipline as "an instance of a kind of paradigm shift: from reflection on literary works to their production". And the teaching problem: "the technical vocabulary used for the critique and appreciation of literary works does not effectively speak to creative practice pedagogy" |
| **His own terms** | Public thoughts and public feelings, made specific as **"knowledge acquisition pathways and affect acquisitions pathways"**. "the domain of literature, the pages of novels and poems, are the places where we can both learn and feel at the same time, and that is the nature of the technology of writing well" |
| **The theory** | A version of the social "organised around flows and coagulations of thoughts and feelings", following Latour and Kathleen Stewart, applied in a reading of Helen Garner. His claim about fiction: that fictional texts "sustain their existence . . . by being suspended in an ecology of heterogeneous allies" |
| **On publics** | Via Michael Warner (2002) and Gay Hawkins's gloss (2006: 64): public formation does not work by persuasion, because that "presupposes that a public already exists", and the assumption of pre-existing publics "excludes the poetic or textual qualities of discourse. Sense and reason are privileged rather than the play of meaning, emotion, and the expressive possibilities of public speech" |
| **His example** | Indigenous Australian literature as a literature from outside the public space, with oral traditions "thousands of years older than Homer", and the repression of traumatic events that "haunt the public sphere" |

## 4b. Three methods that are not in the nine, found 29 Oct

All three came out of reading *Material Feminisms*, and two of them are Latour's.

### The other half of Latour, which Barnwell leaves out

Barnwell quotes Latour for the destruction: critique has run out of steam, the hammer cannot stitch. **Hekman's chapter quotes the constructive half**, from the same 2004 essay:

> "The question was never to get away from facts but closer to them, not fighting empiricism but, on the contrary, **renewing empiricism**." (2004: 231)

Plus "a second empiricism", a "stubbornly realist attitude", and the critic redefined "not as one who 'debunks,' but as one who '**assembles**'" (2004: 246).

**This matters for a dissertation built on a 21-million-word concordance.** The turn against critique is not a turn against evidence. Read only through Barnwell, Latour licenses abandoning method; read with Hekman, he asks for more empiricism and a different job description for the critic. Full citation: **Latour, B. (2004), "Why Has Critique Run out of Steam? From Matters of Fact to Matters of Concern", *Critical Inquiry* 30(2), 225-48.**

### Critical by proximity

Also Latour, quoted by Kirby in her *Material Feminisms* chapter (Latour 2004b): "Critique as a repertoire is over. It has run out of steam entirely, and now the whole question is, '**how can we be critical, not by distance but by proximity?**'"

Kirby then defines and performs it: "making yourself at home in the very logic of your opponent's argument and showing how the direction of that argument can comprehend a very different set of implications." Her whole chapter does it, first to Butler and then to Latour himself, ending by asking whether she and Latour "share an uncanny alliance".

**This is closer to diffractive reading than any of the nine.** It is reading two texts through each other to find what each makes possible in the other, rather than adjudicating between them. She also states its ethics: "the practice of doing critique involves close encounters with another person's way of thinking, with their intellectual commitments and even the temperament and personal idiosyncrasies that animate their writing style."

### Disclosure rather than constitution

Hekman's own proposal, in the same volume, and the one that most directly formalises the standing rule about divergent readings. Language "structures how we apprehend the ontological but it does not constitute it". She specifies the sense of disclosure carefully: not "to uncover, to expose to view", which is too modernist, and not Heidegger's mystical sense, but **the intransitive OED definition, "to show itself, to come to light"**.

> "Disclosure does not entail uncovering, getting reality right, but bringing to light. Different aspects of reality can be disclosed from different perspectives. One of these aspects is not right and the others wrong, but rather they represent different aspects of the same reality. But, importantly, **it is possible to compare the consequences of the different disclosures** of the same reality."

Readings need not agree, none is the correct one, and you can still compare them by what each discloses and at what cost. "We cannot appeal to an objective reality to trump the argument. But we have something to argue about." Her illustration is **Naomi Scheman on photography** (1993: 161): "a photo is a visual trace of an act of seeing", different photos depict the same object differently, and yet "the photo is of something."

**Note how well this sits with Law.** Law gives five commitments to audit a method against, and a vocabulary for what a method makes absent. Hekman gives a way to hold several disclosures at once and still compare them. Together they are a method statement that neither abandons evidence nor demands a single answer.

## 5. What the nine have in common, and the objection that holds

They share a structure: each identifies an ethical or explanatory failure in critical method and offers a novel form of inquiry in its place. Barnwell's own objection, and it is the one to keep:

**Lauren Berlant, *Cruel Optimism* (Duke, 2011), p. 124.** She is sympathetic to Sedgwick's aims and still "resist[s] idealising, even implicitly, any program of better thought or reading", because "the overvaluation of reparative thought is both an occupational hazard and part of a larger overvaluation of a certain mode of virtuously intentional, self-reflective personhood". Her question: "How would we know when the 'repair' we intend is not another form of narcissism or smothering will? Just because we sense it to be so?"

**Claire Hemmings, "Invoking Affect: Cultural Theory and the Ontological Turn", *Cultural Studies* 19(5), 2005, pp. 548-67.** On what the turn leaves social scientists: affect as "something scientists can detect the loss of (in the anomaly), [and] social scientists and cultural critics cannot interpret, but philosophers can imagine" (563).

**Andrew Cole, "The Call of Things: A Critique of Object-Oriented Ontologies", *The Minnesota Review* 80 (2013), pp. 106-18.** Not about reading methods, but the same shape of objection: the new philosophies exclude the premodern thought they are actually kin to.

So the honest position is that adopting one of the nine does not escape method, it chooses one. Which is Law's point, arriving from the other end.

Two of the three are now in hand and read, so this is no longer a shopping list.

| | |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Law, "Making a Mess with Method"** (2003 Lancaster PDF, or the 2007 Sage Handbook chapter at pp. 595-606). **In hand and read.** The five commitments and the two kinds of absence are the operational parts. The book can wait |
| 2 | **Hekman, "Constructing the Ballast"**, in *Material Feminisms*. **Held and read.** Disclosure, and Pickering's mangle, and Barad's sonogram, all in one chapter |
