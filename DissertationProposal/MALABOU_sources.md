## 1. Malabou is already in the comps draft

This is the thing worth knowing first. Malabou is **already cited in
`Comps_Draft_v48`** and in `Draft_v37_with_quantum_indigenous_added`, in the
transposition section, by way of Michael Schwab. The draft sentence:

> "The key claim is about the point of origin. Rheinberger and Malabou need
> historically established concepts to work from, foregrounding deconstructive
> approaches while keeping a certain degree of distance; artistic researchers
> seem to have the luxury to 'jump right in simply appropriating or inventing
> concepts whose plasticity can be supplied by artistically staging them as
> epistemic things. If they work, new understanding will be gained; if they
> fail to work, we may encounter forced acting, incoherent scripts, and
> pointless monologues. In the absence of deeper historical grounding, there is
> no other choice but to take this risk' (Schwab, 2018, pp. 8-9)."

So plasticity is already doing work in the methodology, as a property of
**concepts** staged as epistemic things, and Malabou is already named as one of
the two figures that artistic research is being distinguished from.

## 2. Schwab's own Malabou passage (the source behind that draft sentence)

Michael Schwab, Introduction to *Transpositions: Aesthetico-Epistemic Operators
in Artistic Research* (Leuven University Press, 2018), pp. 7-9. Full text in
`K:\OneDrive\UNM\Comps`. Malabou appears on pp. 8, 9 and 20 (the index), that
is, in the Introduction only.

> "The same is true, to introduce a second example, of Catherine Malabou's work
> on the brain, where with 'plasticity' she comes across a concept central to
> neuroscience: 'we run into this word in every neurology department of every
> medical school and of every university hospital' (Malabou 2008, 4). Yet,
> despite the ubiquity of the concept, she argues that through a confusion with
> notions of 'flexibility' it had not been realised that 'the brain is not
> already made' (ibid., 7) and that not only neuroscience but all aspects of
> culture are part of a process of formation outside which nothing is given.
> Like 'experimental system,' 'plasticity' may be a concept readily used to
> describe the crucial, productive part of a system that awaits complication.
> As such concepts are taken on, new potentials are realised not determined by
> what we believe them to mean."

Schwab pairs Malabou with **Rheinberger** (experimental system, epistemic
things, technical objects) and **Cecile Malaspina** (epistemic noise). The pair
Rheinberger / Malabou is his illustration that these operations are not
exclusive to artistic research, only that artistic research changes their point
of origin.

Two Malabou page references are supplied here: **p. 4** (the word is everywhere
in neurology) and **p. 7** (the brain is not already made).

**Side note, since it has been an open item:** Schwab's reference list in the
same volume gives Kirkkopelto as "Kirkkopelto, Esa. 2015. 'Artistic Research as
Institutional Practice.' In *Artistic Research Yearbook 2015: From Arts College
to University*, edited by Torbjörn Lind, **48-53**. Stockholm: Swedish Research
Council." Kirkkopelto also has his own chapter in *Transpositions* at pp.
33-40.

## 3. The best substitute for the missing book

*Posthuman Glossary*, ed. Braidotti and Hlavajova (Bloomsbury, 2018), has a
dedicated **"Plasticity" entry by Tom Giesbers, pp. 320-323**, entirely on
Malabou. The volume is in the folders twice, in `todo October 26` and in
`K:\OneDrive\UNM\Comps`.

The entry opens with the etymology and the three senses:

> "As used in the work of the French philosopher Catherine Malabou, the word
> 'plasticity' derives from the Greek *plassein*, which has a dual meaning: 1)
> to receive form (like clay is plastic); and 2) to give form (like surgery can
> be plastic). Malabou adds a third meaning, which is especially suited to
> philosophical use: 3) plastic in reference to the annihilation of form (as in
> plastic explosives)."

What the entry supplies that the Yergeau quotations do not:

- **The Hegel ground.** Plasticity comes from Malabou's dissertation *The
  Future of Hegel*, where she reads a minor element of Hegel's argument as
  able to answer Heideggerian concerns about time. The third sense, explosive
  plasticity, is read back into Hegel, who "anticipated it in some respects."
- **Where she parts from Derrida.** Her supervisor. She uses the concept to
  vindicate dialectics as a method, rather than to destabilise it.
- **Flexibility is the enemy term, not rigidity.** The popular neurobiology is
  "articulated solely in terms of flexibility," which lets the ideology of
  flexible labour present itself as scientific fact. Those not deemed flexible,
  "or rather 'immobile'," are relegated to poverty; mental illness is treated
  "solely with the aim of the restoration of flexibility, in the terms dictated
  by flexible labour."
- **"Neuronal liberation."** The entry attributes this phrase to Malabou's own
  project, a culture of neuronal liberation rather than biological determinism.
  Yergeau reaches the same phrase through Pitts-Taylor, who uses it critically.
- **"Our brain is what we do with it."**
- **Brain damage.** The capacity of injury "to completely destroy someone's
  personality, while retaining the option for a new personality to emerge."
  This sits radically outside psychoanalytic explanation. She then draws
  parallels between brain trauma and social trauma: terrorist attacks, sexual
  abuse, oppression, slavery, "in both cases, a violence cuts the subject off
  from its accumulated memories."
- **"If we didn't destroy ourselves a bit, we could not live."**
- **A longer genealogy than Malabou gives.** Giesbers argues Hegel likely took
  the term from Schelling, who used it for nature; Goethe on the plastic nature
  of man; the alchemist F. M. van Helmont describing Leibniz's monads as *vis
  plastica*; William Harvey using *vis plastica* for epigenesis, which was then
  used to explain fossils as "the spontaneous results of the plastic latency of
  the earth."

That last bullet is the one that runs toward an art and materials lineage
rather than a neuroscience one.

## 4. The Deleuze route

Louise Mazet, "Gilles Deleuze's 'the Brain is the Screen' in Digital Culture"
(MA, Erasmus University Rotterdam, supervisor Sjoerd van Tuinen, 31 July 2018,
12,286 words). In `todo October 26`. 34 occurrences of Malabou. Its cover image
is a **diffraction grating** and it has a chapter headed **Diffraction**.

Its opening paragraph is a summary of the missing book:

> "In *What Should We Do with Our Brain?* Catherine Malabou discusses different
> models that are used to understand the brain and evaluates them in terms of
> plasticity, the ability to mold. Rejecting the metaphors that depict the
> brain as if it is a machine, central telephone exchange or computer, she
> finds that Deleuze offers a take on the brain which allows for and is even
> determined by the plasticity it actually possesses. For him, the brain is an
> acentered system characterized by its cuts and gaps. Furthermore, according
> to Malabou the plasticity of the brain is also the 'real image of the world'.
> This means that although we might not see it, we experience (brain)plasticity
> here and now. The world itself is thus actually plastic, it can be shaped and
> created in ways we have not yet imagined, but as we are not aware of it we
> miss out on great potential for change."

Malabou's own examples in that book, per this thesis: Deleuze's cinematic
brain, plus films by Alain Resnais and Stanley Kubrick. So the book itself runs
through artworks.
