## 1. Who is speaking, and from where

The article states its two positions in its first sentence: "As a fully sighted mother with young children and a male lecturer with a vision impairment, we both thought with George Lakoff and Mark Johnson's *Metaphors We Live By* (1980) during our recent doctoral studies." The piece is written out of two just-finished doctorates and the dissatisfaction that followed them, and it names what each of them is up against in working life: "Ben, for example, frequently struggles to gain access to the materials he seeks to read. Lucinda struggles to get to meetings inevitably planned at school pick-up time."

They say what they are not doing. "We do not solely position ourselves outside language, as scientists operating on it, but as coming into being through its material-discursive entanglements even as we enact performances of 'knowing'." And they refuse the instrument even for their own work: "we do not consider ourselves to be applying a feminist or disability studies 'lens' but creating new readings in a performative fashion that is both personal and political."

They state the limit of their own position twice. They are "open to critique of our own metaphors, in particular those that reveal our White, middle-class backgrounds and biases". And on method they say plainly that there is no survey behind this: "It would be impossible to do a formal review of the use of the word 'lens' in the literature of qualitative educational research; this figure is invoked by countless academics, in countless papers." What they offer instead is "an ethnographically inflected discourse analysis of key examples of language that we have collected", with the problematic "emerging from our accounts of our reading, rather than a positivist predetermined research question."

## 2. The argument

Three moves, in order.

**One. The lens is everywhere, including where it should not be.** They collect it from their own favourite writers. Lani Florian's framework is "a lens to guide research on developing the inclusive practices of primary and secondary classroom teachers". UNESCO's 2009 inclusion guidelines advise that "looking at education through an inclusive lens implies a shift from seeing the child as the problem to seeing the education system as the problem". Cooper, He and Levin say "it is important to first examine the individual, personal lens through which we view today's diverse world". Lisa Mazzei reads data "through a new materialist lens". Rorty, whose *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature* is the field's classic critique of visual metaphor, "relies, without irony, on 'seeing' the future in the epigraph opening his critique of visual metaphor."

**Two. Blindness as the figure for ignorance is the second half of the same habit.** Bateson: "At the centre of any tradition it is easy to become blind to alternatives." Wagner: materials that "illuminate blind spots". Bartolomé, in an article called "Beyond the methods Fetish", warns against "blind replication of instructional programs". Their response to the last one: "by invoking the metaphor of the visionless, repetitious educator, Bartolome perpetuates mistreatment and miseducation by conceptualising blindness as both deficient and ignorant." And Altman, who in 1990 had already made their own argument against Lakoff and Johnson, "describes Lakoff and Johnson's failing as a 'blind spot'".

**Three. The reason it persists is fetish, in Stuart Hall's sense.** They take the definition from Hall 1997: "fetishism as looking but not looking, as using a decoy to cover illicit desire, as ambivalence, as using and not using, indulging and denying." Then the step: "Metaphor is substitution of one thing for another ... It is a small shift to query how this substitution might facilitate adulation of the forbidden with vehicle (substitute word) allowing us to countenance tenor (subject) without shame. In the examples given above, the lens stands in for a lust for power and for the authority to speak."

Hence the title's claim, and the one sentence that carries the whole paper: "The 'feminist lens', for example, gives work apparent legitimacy while simultaneously undermining any claims to have moved beyond positivist certainties and epistemologies."

They note that Hall's own illustration of disavowal is itself a lens: "a caricature of a portly middle-aged man in breeches, with fob watch, peering through a Priapic telescope at the naked buttocks of a Black woman (1997, p. 269)."

## 3. The re-reading of Lakoff and Johnson

This is the section with the most evidence in it, and the counting is theirs.

They quote the book's own definition, that "the essence of metaphor is understanding and experiencing one thing in terms of another" (p. 5), and the claim that we can learn our conceptual systems by "looking at language" to gain "insight" (p. 3), and say the authors "fail to interrogate the politics of their own choice of terms". On the visual metaphor specifically: in one paragraph on p. 10 the book says we "may lose sight" of aspects of argument, that an opponent can be "viewed as giving you his time", and that we "often lose sight of cooperation". "This visual metaphor, of perception as vision, is ironically never explored in their own work."

Then the pronouns. "'He', 'his' and 'him' reign until Chapter Four, where, under the heading, HAVING CONTROL OR FORCE IS UP; BEING SUBJECT TO CONTROL OR FORCE IS DOWN, we read 'I have no control over her' (1980, p. 15)." The chain they draw from the book's own small capitals: CONTROL IS UP gives MAN IS UP gives RATIONAL IS UP (pp. 15-17). Their comment: "Power seems irresistibly conceived through metaphors of phallic verticality."

Their list of quoted examples from the book, under the heading "Multiple winces follow", with the book's page numbers: "She saw getting married as the solution to her problems" (p. 27); "She's just a pretty face" (p. 37); "He is the father of modern biology" (p. 47); "He has a barren mind" (p. 47); "He invited a sexy blonde to our dinner party" (p. 163). Plus the inventory of what the book's metaphors are drawn from: "automobiles, wheels, ships, windshield wipers, races, inflation, war, butts, buttresses, Fords, guns, buses, Nixon, Napoleon, the White House and the Kremlin."

The three places they find the book turning its own argument on others and not itself: its call to critique the ways metaphors are imposed by people in power (p. 160); the warning that "the blind acceptance of metaphor can hide degrading realities" (p. 237); and the afterword's self-congratulation on "seeing beyond the 'truths' of our culture" (p. 239).

The citation count they give for the book, from Google Scholar on 8 September 2016, is over 42,000. The date and source are theirs, and the figure is nine years old as of this read.

Their closing judgement on it, in their words: "Lakoff and Johnson can perform brute patriarchy under the guise of linguistic analysis."

## 4. The section where they turn it on themselves

"Distorting the lens" is the part of this article that does the most work, because the examples are Ben Whitburn's own thesis.

The quotations are from his own writing. In the thesis he has to "turn the analytical lens inward and upward" (2015a, p. 147), which they call "a contortionist feat that smacks of both pleasure and pain". The paper after it makes the case for "the analysis of inclusive schooling through the lens of students 'included' subjectivities" (2015b, p. 1). Their reading of why: "Evidently, Ben has also bought into empowerment through normalised linguistic practices and has used language about blindness, visuality or the ability to stare through the looking glass, portrayals that are patently distant from his own experiences and dis/abled subjecthood."

And the general form they give it, which is not about either of them: "This is an uncomfortable position, common among people with disabilities shifting uneasily from renunciation of embodied difference to its embrace in attempting to be recognised as legitimately corporeal."

They also record that they have not solved it: "Yet researchers, ourselves included, have failed to find alternatives, even when we have struggled with the lens as a metaphor for conducting research." And that the figure keeps coming back inside this very article: "The male figure we describe here seeks to dominate our language, and this paper, even as we try to knock the lens out of his hands."

## 5. What they actually ask for

The ask is graduated, and it is worth keeping the gradations because the abstract's "we call for caution" is the whole of it.

The minimum: "We offer these ideas as provocation and enticement to other researchers to first of all become aware of what their language forecloses."

The next step: "We hope readers will subsequently pause and think before using visual metaphor, and if they are not prepared to reject it entirely, to employ it with conscious and deliberate intent, acknowledging all its entailments, or risk perpetuating profoundly discriminatory practices."

What they have done themselves: "We now deliberately avoid the use of the lens."

The strongest version, which they state and then withdraw from: "Can we ban metaphor, impatient with its binary logic (Deleuze & Guattari, 1987, p. 5) of this means that? This is a task beyond us as authors; we seek instead to perform better metaphors."

And the test they offer in place of a rule, which is affective rather than propositional: "So we would like to think that readers of this article may begin to notice affect, to attend to the wince as their fingers start to type the word 'lens' and to share our discomfort at the metaphors we live 'by'."

The last line of the paper keeps the figure rather than dropping it: "Until we can confront our illicit desires for power and throw out the dominant cultural tropes of blindness as ignorance and the feminine as weak, we are stuck not only with the lens, but fixed in its sights."

They also object to the preposition in the title they are critiquing: "Even the title of *Metaphors We Live By* suggests our separation from language, positioning these metaphors beside us, or as a code." Their own section heading is "Metaphors [that we might] live by/through/as".

## 6. One entailment they name that the others do not

On literature teaching, where the lens is now curriculum: "The lens posits literature as not just corpus, but as corpse, as cadaver awaiting dissection and microscopic analysis. The lens fixes particular ontologies and epistemologies antithetical to the creative and the personal." The occasion was a talk Lucinda McKnight was asked to give to senior secondary Literature teachers on "critical theory lenses including feminism and post colonialism, as dictated by new State curriculum requirements".

On the lens and responsibility, which is their inclusive-education argument proper: "Insulating the observer from the observed, the lens affords detachment from the community or culture in which inclusion might occur. The observer can opt out of the discomfort of educational segregation, and watch comfortably from afar. The master of the lens is not implicated in inclusive education change, and is instead absolved of responsibility for it, and indeed, of shame. This is a safe kind of looking." And then the sentence that is the hardest in the article: "Taking up a lens to effect change in education is a cowardly act."

## 7. The alternatives they float, and the one they concede is taken

Their closing proposal is a list of questions, not a programme. Glass "as more fascinating, desirable and useful when fractured, curved, bubbly, milky (blurry), bulging, aged and distorted"; glass "as liquid, molten, searing, shapely and blown"; the lens re-imagined "as part of a DeleuzoGuattarian machinic assemblage with multiple lenses, broken and intact, and ears and nerve endings and tongues, all resisting organisation into a sensible human body (Braidotti, 2013)"; "canes and crutches and computer cords, all at wild, wonderful angles, enacting Lather's 'multidirectionalities' (2013, p. 641)".

Then the concession, in the same paragraph: "Yet we are aware that other theorists have already gone there, with crystallisation (Richardson & St Pierre, 2008) or diffraction (Barad, 2007) to allow for complexity and change, and still the sharp-eyed man-scientist's lens endures."

That sentence is the only place diffraction appears in the article. Barad appears twice in all: once there, once in the reference list.

## 8. Where this sits beside the other reads in this folder, without settling anything

**Shildrick.** Shildrick is cited four times here, all to the 2012 "Critical disability studies" handbook chapter, for embodiment, emotion and affect and for the "spectacle of the aberration, the disabled 'other'". The article just read in this folder is her 2015 *Hypatia* piece. The two positions do not meet in these texts: Shildrick's argument is that the prosthesis is constitutive and the body does not end at the skin, and her example is the wheelchair as part of the assemblage; McKnight and Whitburn's argument is about the figurative use of optical prostheses in research writing, and their complaint is that the instrument buys the writer distance. One treats the device as what the body is made of, the other treats the device as what the writer hides behind. Both of them quote Hall's looking-but-not-looking structure of asymmetry about disability. Neither text adjudicates the other and nothing here does either.

**Butler.** Butler is not in these references. The overlap is structural rather than cited: Butler's catachresis is an argument that the figure can be the only way to say the thing, and that misuse of a term is where political work happens; McKnight and Whitburn's argument is that a particular figure forecloses, and they stop short of banning figure as such ("This is a task beyond us as authors"). The two positions on what a figure does are not reconciled in either text.

**Kotzé, and the post-qualitative thread.** St Pierre is the most cited name in this article after the authors' own: 16 occurrences, including St Pierre 2012, 2013, 2014 and St Pierre and Jackson 2014, with the load-bearing quotation "one must rethink the assumptions of a representational logic that puts language to work in a certain way in the service of the real and the material if one is to rethink the ontology that logic makes possible" (2013, p. 650). They name their debt to the *IJQSE* post-qualitative special issue, "particularly Lather (2013) and St Pierre (2013), to Alecia Jackson writing with St Pierre (2014) on qualitative research and also to Braidotti (2013)". This is the same literature Kotzé's method chapter runs on, and the Jackson and Mazzei primary articles are now in the November 2 folder.

**A precise note on the Mazzei citation, because it is easy to get wrong.** The Mazzei article criticised here by name for "a new materialist lens" is Mazzei, L. (2013), "Materialist mappings of knowing in being: Researchers constituted in the production of knowledge", *Gender and Education* 25, pp. 776-785. That is **not** the Mazzei piece in the November 2 folder, which is "A voice without organs" (2013). Same author, same year, different article. Nothing in this read tells us whether the voice-without-organs piece uses the figure.

**Fries and Fahey.** No contact in citation. The thread to note is that both of those dissertations repeatedly refuse "illustration" and "description" as the relation between work and idea (Fries's standing formula is that the work is "manifested as an encounter rather than as an illustration or description", and Fahey names a failed body of work as "illustrating an idea, rather than embodying an exploration of the question"). McKnight and Whitburn are objecting to a figure that does the opposite thing: puts the researcher at a distance from what is examined. Whether those refusals are the same refusal is not something either text decides.

**Romero.** No contact. The one point of contact with that reading is the single-sentence appearance of Barad's diffraction here as an already-tried alternative, where Romero's chapter addresses the physics-side question about such borrowings. Neither settles it.

## 9. The live check this article is for

The reason this was second in the November 2 read order, from `K:\OneDrive\UNM\Dissertation\November 2\NOV2_inventory_2026-11-02.md`, verbatim: "It is about metaphor (90 uses) and specifically about sexist and ableist metaphor in research writing, which is both the live metaphor question in `todo October 26` and a direct check on the figures any of us put into prose. It should be read before the next time a metaphor is proposed for anything."

Read for that purpose, the article yields three checks, which are theirs and not mine.

1. Does the figure put the writer at a distance from what the writing is about, and does that distance do work the writer would not defend in plain words.
2. Does the figure make a bodily capacity the measure of knowing, and some other bodily state the measure of not knowing. Their two cases are sight for knowledge and blindness for ignorance.
3. Is the figure being used because it confers authority the writer has otherwise disclaimed. Their test case is the phrase "feminist lens".

What the article does not supply is a replacement vocabulary. They say so: they have "failed to find alternatives", the ban is "beyond us as authors", and crystallisation and diffraction were already tried and did not displace the lens.

One divergence inside this folder to leave standing, not to resolve. Neimanis's position, in the chapters read on 2 November, is that her water figures are "always more than metaphorical" and work by "contiguity or continuity (rather than analogy)", that "concepts are always also material", and that "if water is an idea, it is a material, embodied idea". McKnight and Whitburn's position is that the figure's entailments come along whether or not the writer intends them, and that the way to test one is to notice the wince. Those are two different accounts of what a figure is and of what makes one accountable. Both are in this folder. Nothing in either text decides between them, and this file does not.
