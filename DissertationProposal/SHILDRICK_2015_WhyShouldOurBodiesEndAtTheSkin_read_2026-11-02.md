## 1. What the article is

The title is **Donna Haraway's question, taken as the article's own**: "Why should our bodies end at the skin, or include at best other beings encapsulated by skin?" (Haraway 1990, 220). Shildrick's move is to say the question is now answerable, and that **Critical Disability Studies is where it gets answered**, because disabled people's prostheses are where bodies visibly do not end at the skin.

**Her argument in her own summary:** the disabled body is to be understood "**not as a less than perfect form of the normative standard, but as figuring difference in a nonbinary sense**", and the route to that is a move feminist theory and disability theory have both declined to make: **from Merleau-Ponty's phenomenology to Deleuze's assemblage.**

> "Phenomenology has become a powerful tool of analysis in CDS, but **Deleuze is still treated with extreme caution. It is, I think, a missed opportunity** based on a reluctance to take more seriously the shift in thinking between Merleau-Ponty's early work . . . and his posthumously published work."

**She does not ask anyone to give up phenomenology:** "**There is no need to supersede phenomenological accounts** . . . but rather a gain can be made in approaching the problematic with a determination to use multiple resources."

## 2. The Merleau-Ponty she uses, and the caution she attaches to it

**The flesh of the world**, from *The Visible and the Invisible* (1968), is the part of Merleau-Ponty she wants:

> "What Merleau-Ponty points to is a dimension in excess of the interhuman connections that channel the co-construction of embodiment: **the 'flesh of the world' in which we are all immersed, and through which we are constituted**. It throws into doubt the very sense of self and other as distinct entities and speaks to **a folding over of flesh that creates the possibility of difference within a unified but undifferentiated medium**."

> He "seeks to instantiate '**other landscapes besides my own**' . . . that are nonetheless mutually interwoven", and reversibility between self and other "implies **not a merging of subjectivities, but more a coming-together in difference, a matter of both convergence and divergence**."

**And the caveat, which is a textual fact and not a judgment:** "it is important to remember, however, that **Merleau-Ponty's account was recovered posthumously in draft form** and as such is open to materialist feminist reinterpretations that, as well as outlining its limitations, might usefully extend it."

**The one prosthesis he does give her** is the blind man's cane (1962, 165) "becoming an extension of his self-embodiment", which "shows that **the lived body is not identical with the material entity bounded by the skin**."

## 3. The equalising claim, and the exception she makes to it

**The claim**, stated as a claim about theory rather than about experience:

> "on the level of theory **there is no distinction to be made between one body and another** in terms of the manifold interconnections and transformatory entanglements, to use Karen Barad's coinage (Barad 2003, 818), that mark all forms of embodiment . . . **At the very least, the interface with technology is the common experience of us all.**"

With two quotations doing the work: Don Ihde, "**We are bodies in technologies**" (2002, 137), and Mitchell and Snyder, "**the prostheticized body is the rule, not the exception**" (2000, 7). Her own formulation: "The point is not that we are all alike, but precisely that we are all different and that conventional categories are simply a convenient way of simplifying the irreducible complexity of corporeal forms. **To a greater or lesser extent, none of us is entitled to claim a singular body.**"

**The exception**, which she makes carefully and only as a matter of degree: "there are good reasons for seeing **the disabled body in its material specificities as a privileged exemplar of hybridity**", because anomalous bodies have historically been "more readily, though by no means exclusively, associated with various prosthetic devices", whether external (limb replacements, sensory supports, behaviour-controlling drugs) or incorporated (neuro-implants, transplanted organs). And in all cases: "**such prostheses contest our faith in corporeal integrity even as they are intended to restore the clean and proper body.**"

Later she puts the degree claim in a sentence: "**the elements of the assemblage are perhaps relatively easy to trace** [in a Paralympian's life], **though never to exhaustively map, but that is only a difference in degree, and hardly even that, rather than a difference in kind.**"

## 4. Derrida's supplement, on prostheses

The short Derrida section is the article's cleanest piece of argument, and it is reusable on anything that is said to be added to a finished thing:

> "**the very possibility of (prosthetic) augmentation indicates an absence of self-sufficiency or originary wholeness** (Derrida 1987). In Derrida's analysis, the supplement is necessary in constituting the object as such . . . In other words, **prostheses exceed their instrumentality, and construct the very thing that they purport to merely enhance.**"

> Derrida, quoted: "'**technology has not simply added itself, from the outside . . . this foreign or dangerous supplement is "originarily" at work and in place in the supposedly ideal interiority of the "body and soul"**'" (Derrida 1995, 244).

> Her bridge to Deleuze: "his claim that **the supplement is always already 'at work and in place' mirrors Deleuze's implication that the assemblage, in all its dis-organization, is the inevitable condition of living**."

**And the ethical consequence she draws from it**, which is the article's main political point: "**As Derrida so clearly shows, the very possibility of supplementation exposes the illusion of any originary integrity and autonomy that might ground hierarchies of value** with respect to the embodiment of people with and without so-called anomalies."

## 5. Technics and somatechnics, defined

She gives the word a working definition rather than leaving it to float, and says why she needs it:

> "**The term technics is difficult to pin down and is used inconsistently across the literature, but I shall take it to denote the entangled and co-constitutive interrelations between technologies, 'users,' and the sociocultural context.**"

> "it is not simply material entities that come together in technics, but **a heterogeneous array of discursive elements and practices that encompass, without privileging any one modality, the affective, the political, the institutional, and the biological** . . . **The term somatechnics, now established with an eponymous journal, seems especially apt in its grasp of what is at stake.**"

Elizabeth Grosz supplies the long view: "'**from the moment in which the human appears as such, it appears alongside of both artifacts and technologies, poesis and techne**'" (2005, 137), with embodiment understood "in the modality of practice, a series of actions that entangle the ontological with an irreducible materiality in which both undergo '**mutual metamorphosis**'".

**Her own one-line version:** "**There are no solid bodies as such, only becoming-bodies.**"

**And Deleuze and Guattari's stirrup, quoted whole**, which is the clearest statement of the principle:

> "'tools only exist in relation to the interminglings they make possible or that make them possible. **The stirrup entails a new man-horse symbiosis** that at the same time entails new weapons and new instruments. Tools are inseparable from symbioses or amalgamations defining a Nature-Society machinic assemblage . . . **a society is defined by its amalgamations, not by its tools.**'" (1987, 90)

## 6. The wheelchair, and the asymmetry test

**The wheelchair passage is a precise piece of work on a terminological correction and what it still conceals.** The correct term is "wheelchair user", not "wheelchair-bound", and she grants the reason fully: "In terms of modernist paradigms of the sovereign subject as intentional agent, this is entirely justified, for no-one should be reduced to the dimensions of an inanimate object nor figured as passive." Then:

> "Yet what the conventional model of the wheelchair user speaks to is **the illusion of a naturally bounded and self-contained body that is somehow diminished, even as mobility is undoubtedly enhanced, by the reliance on an external technology.** To counter such an image, **the person with the disability must be represented as effectively in control and thus unified in mind if not in body.**"

**Her test for this is one sentence long and it is the best thing in the article:**

> "Consider the very clear difference in **calling a gold-medal winner 'a machine' in relation to first the nondisabled sprinter Usain Bolt and then the equally successful wheelchair racer David Weir.** Whereas for the former it might be seen as a compliment to his singular teleological focus and delivery, with the latter it could be construed as undermining and insulting, even dehumanizing. **Disabled people are expected to transcend their very visible reliance on technologies.**"

On the 2012 Paralympics more generally: the athletes were "consistently represented as superheroes, **supercrips** in critical perspectives, **not because they were highly skilled and hard-working sportspeople, but because their achievements were read within a narrative of exceptional inspiration and overcoming**", and the cost of that narrative is that "**any critical appreciation of the significance of prostheses is obscured.**"

**The question she takes from Deleuze and Guattari to replace the identity question:** they are "interested not so much in what a body is . . . but in what that body is capable of", quoting: "'**we know nothing about a body until we know what it can do, in other words, what its affects are, and how they can or cannot enter into composition with other affects, with the affects of another body**'" (1987, 284). And she is careful to say this is *not* the social model: "**this is not what Deleuze and Guattari mean, at least not insofar as the argument centers on an intentional subject.**"

## 7. The athlete-as-event: a worked list

The article's method, where it stops being abstract, is to list what an assemblage actually contains. She does it twice.

**The medal ceremony**, written as what the athlete herself knows:

> "The woman who stands tearfully smiling on the rostrum with her medal in hand has every reason to bask in the approbation given by the crowd, but **she will know too what they can barely acknowledge: that her moment of triumph is just a freeze-frame in an assemblage that defies the imposition of parameters.**"

Then the inventory, which runs from the personal to the bureaucratic without a break: "her trainers; the utility of her prosthesis, if she uses one; the carefully controlled diet . . . the gym sessions . . . the loving family . . . **the schoolmates who mocked or praised her achievements against their own**; and the life she must return to once her Olympic moment has passed", and then, "equally constitutive of the athlete-as-event": "**the rules and regulations governing Paralympian categories; the specific national benefits system that impedes or supports her embodied difference; the negotiation of transport between accommodation and the stadium; the health and safety inspections; the drug tests; the paid assistants who facilitate personal care; the beliefs and expectations of the spectators; finally, but by no means exclusively, the question: Who am I?** There is no closure or completion."

**And the endnote is better still** (n. 7), on sight-impaired alpine skiing, where a social convention is made to show the mechanism:

> "The sport relies on pairings, with each skier having a guide who skis in close proximity to the competitor, calling out instructions . . . amplified by the use of radio mikes . . . and the assemblage then widens its scope to include the spectators. **Instead of cheering as the skiers approach the finish line, the crowd typically falls silent in order not to interfere with the audio communication between the pair. This is the somatechnics of the assemblage.**"

## 8. Molar and molecular, with the empirical studies attached

The vocabulary, given plainly:

| | |
|---|---|
| **molar** | "the prevailing macro-orders of ideological, social, and psychic power that mandate particular forms of identity and subjectivity, **without, however, ever finally cementing them into place**" |
| **molecular** | "flows and intensities"; "unanticipated and unpredictable connections will emerge that allow data to flow in new directions that signal **a micropolitics of molecular becoming**" |
| **territorialized / deterritorialized** | "when assemblages are what they call '**overcoded**' with meaning, they fall into molar forms; they are territorialized. Nonetheless, in a process that is continually opened up and shut down, the possibility remains for assemblages to be deterritorialized" |
| **line of flight** | after Youdell and Armstrong: lines that "'flee from and potentially scatter assemblages of meanings, representations, practices and subjectivations, **offering new "becomings"**'" |

**Her worked example of molar is borrowed, and it is Alecia Jackson's cheerleader study**, quoted directly:

> "'Molar is something that is well-defined, massive, and governing, such as large structures or identity categories. For example, **cheerleading can be considered a molar form in the southern USA**; cheerleading exists within discursive and material fields saturated with patriarchy, masculinity, and historically laden ideals of dainty, feminine southern girlhood.'" (Jackson 2010, 581)

Shildrick's parallel: "**Like the young cheerleader, who casually and intermittently de-stabilizes the program by resisting the strictures of the clean, proper, and disciplined body, the disabled athlete too can take pride in her molar achievements but need not be defined by them.**" She also notes that Jackson's data "could very well lend themselves to a Foucauldian analysis, but Jackson chose a Deleuzian approach."

**And a real political instance, not a hypothetical.** Against a UK and international "concerted agenda to reposition people with disabilities as effectively undeserving of their various specific state benefits", Paralympians "**dislocated the molar capture of their identities to disrupt the narrative of an inspirational selfhood emerging against the odds**". Her named case is **Tanni Grey-Thompson**, a former gold medallist and a life peer, "both unmistakable molar identities", who "**repositioned herself as a critic of state policy by pointing out just how far she would be rendered unable to function were it not for the many supporting networks**". Her reading of that act: "Her line of flight away from the authorized narrative **ruptures public acceptance of the austerity measures that disproportionately affect disabled people**, and introduces new dimensions of her own becoming that nevertheless remain connected back to her influence as a putative spokesperson."

## 9. Where she sets her own limits

Worth noting as a set, because they are all stated rather than implied.

- **On her own distance from her material** (n. 10): "**Even as I write, I am aware of maintaining a critical distance not yet overcome in relation to a theoretically based endeavor**, but we will learn eventually from those who have already embraced the materiality of the research assemblage and moved on. **The doctoral thesis work of Michael Feely has particularly challenged me.**"
- **On the terminology dispute, by refusing it** (n. 2): "I deliberately deploy both forms. **There are good arguments for and against both of them, but none that is compelling.**"
- **On Barad's borrowing from physics** (n. 3), in one line: "**Barad is explicit that her use of 'entanglements' goes beyond its disciplinary origins in physics.**"
- **On using Deleuze at all** (n. 4): "Certainly he would not recognize some developments, **including my own interpretation perhaps**, but that is no reason to limit exploration."
- **On outcomes**: "**I am not implying that all connections are equally positive, and some may stall, some assemblages will be reterritorialized in destructive ways, some bodies will not flourish**, but none of these outcomes is fixed; it remains the case that things could be otherwise."
- **On the risks of her own optimism**, three of them named: "**eschewing equally the determinist hype of technological singularity, the desire for a nostalgic return to a lost Eden, and the depredations of neo-capitalist global markets that would commodify the very processes of assemblage itself.** The way for forward is never clear, but **the ethical task does not reside in evaluating a projected scenario in advance, but in taking the risk**."
- **And one place where she disagrees with Braidotti and says so** (n. 12): Braidotti (2013) "takes a different view of shared vulnerability as the reactive bond between humans and other species. **I understand the dangers she sees in anthropomorphizing those others, but would maintain that any recognition that contests our privilege as a species should be welcomed.**"

Her own vulnerability claim, carried over from her 2002 book: "**if . . . vulnerability is the shared condition of becoming, then we must question the stability of human being itself**" (Shildrick 2002, 133). And Braidotti quoted approvingly on ethics: "'ethics requires adequate assemblages or interaction: **one has to pursue or actively create the kind of encounters that are likely to favour an increase in active becomings**'" (2006, 217).

**The closing sentence** names the whole project: "a turn to a kind of post-anthropocentrism that can celebrate **an erotics and ethics of connection with an array of organic and inorganic others**." ("Erotics of connection" is her own phrase, offered earlier as her name for what Haraway's later affirmation of desire amounts to.)
