## Part 3. 2012 Statement: "Towards A Posthumanist Methodology. A Statement"

### 3.1 The file

*Frame* 25.1 (2012), pp. 9-18, two-column magazine layout with marginal notes; the PDF carries two printed pages per sheet in the order 9 | 11, 10 | 13, 12 | 15, 14 | 17, 16 | 18, so a reader following the PDF order reads pp. 9, 11, 10, 13, 12, 15, 14, 17, 16, 18. All page numbers below are printed folios. Denominator 3,409 words including notes, works cited, summary and author note.

### 3.2 What it argues

The statement opens on the claim that debate in Posthuman Studies has been about content and not about method, and that the two "are not separated" because the overcoming of dualisms "includes the traditional divide between theory and practice. Posthumanism is praxis" (p. 9). Section 1 defines Posthumanism against Transhumanism and Antihumanism: it "criticizes anthropocentric humanism and opens its inquiry to non-human life: from animals to artificial intelligence, from aliens to other forms of hypothetical entities related to the physics notion of a multiverse" (p. 10), while "simultaneously emphasizing its own human-centrism on the grounds that Posthumanism is still theorized by and for human beings" (p. 10). Its metaphysics "are located in agnostic perseverance" and it offers "a revisitation of the being as transcendent immanence" (p. 11).

Section 2, "A Posthumanist Methodology", is the passage the proposal's method chapter is most likely to want. Posthumanism "seems to resist the notion of 'method,' and it actually does"; its methodology "finds its rhizomatic outlines in the postmodern critique of objective knowledge and absolute truth" and "resonates with Feyerabend's Against Method (1975), in the sense that its value is merely transitional and contingent" (p. 11). It "does not recognize any primacy to the written text; it is aware that Posthumanism can be performed in many ways", drawing on multi-sited ethnography (Marcus 1995) and "auto-ethnographic performance (Spry 2001) as a vehicle for relocating the 'I' and the body in scholarly reflection", and on "oral history, proverbs and songs" (pp. 11-12). It "sympathizes with the legal system of creative commons and open source" (p. 12).

Section 3 names the risks: "flattening difference (Luft 2009)" and "the difficulty of including non-human voices", which "at present" can only be approached "in an 'as if' mode" (p. 13). The main target is "hegemonic essentialism", "the widespread habit of only referring to thinkers, artists or theorists who belong to the cultural hegemony", whose typical form is "a text which only quotes white male intellectuals" and which "does not situate its standpoints, but presents them as neutral and fit-for-all" (pp. 13-14). The remedy is not "political correctness or affirmative action, but an invitation to fully embrace the posthuman in the way we, as academics, do research" (p. 14). The personal example is the visual-arts genealogy: having found "difficulty finding black artists", a friend's suggestion to change her keywords led her into Afrofuturism (pp. 14-15). "Resistant essentialism" (women-only or black-only accounts) is treated as the lesser risk, with Lonzi's "Let's spit on Hegel" (1970) as the example and Harding's "strong objectivity" and the "bicultural" standpoint as its justification (pp. 15-16). The conclusion: Posthumanism "should not position itself in the hegemony nor in the resistance, but it should promote a dynamic openness" (p. 16), and "may ultimately become a mode of existential inquiry to be applied in everyday life" (p. 17).

### 4.2 The three questions and the argument

The thesis announces itself as "a recent development of Critical and Cultural Posthumanism, which arose within the field of Literary Criticism, from the coining of the term (Hassan 1987), until the Nineties and the publication of the key text 'How We Became Posthuman' (1999) by Katherine Hayles" (p. 13), and its own genealogy runs "from the 'Letter on Humanism' (1947) by Martin Heidegger" through Postmodernism, the Studies of the Difference and Cyborg Theory (p. 13). Posthumanism is "a praxis, as well as a philosophy of mediation, which manifests post-dualistic, post-centralizing, inclusive and comprehensive types of approach" (p. 14), and "a second generation of Postmodernism" (p. 14).

**Question 1, "What is Philosophical Posthumanism?" (sections 1-7, pp. 22-54).** The "post" is set in the politics of the "post-s" (p. 22) with three sources: "the integral deconstruction of fixed categories invested by postmodernity; the epistemological impact of quantum physics; the increased role of technology in the formation of human identity" (p. 22). Posthumanism "suits the informal geological time of anthropocene" (p. 23). Shiva is read beside it (pp. 23-24) and Arendt's "jumping over our own shadows" sets the epistemic limit: Posthumanism "accesses such an epistemological standpoint through the feminist policies of situating the self" (p. 24). Transhumanism is "an ultra-humanism" (p. 34), rooted in the Enlightenment (p. 33), marked by "techno-reductionism" (p. 35) and by a body that "doesn't seem to be situated, nor to belong to a genealogy" (p. 35). Technology is read through Heidegger's revealing (pp. 39-42) and Foucault's technologies of the self (pp. 43-44), and "arrives to the posthuman debate through the mediation of feminism, in particular ... Donna Haraway's cyborg" (p. 42). Antihumanism is answered with "if God or Man (note the masculine form) are dead, who killed them?" (p. 47); the posthuman "is in tune with Derrida's deconstructive approach" rather than Foucault's death of Man (p. 48). Section 7 adds the New Materialisms as the feminist line that "engaged in a sophisticated inquiry into matter, directly investigating scientific fields such as Theoretical Physics, Quantum Physics and Cosmology" (p. 49), and names the fault of those who "embrace the 'exotic' difference, such as the robot, the alien, the biotechnological chimeras, without having to deal with the differences embedded within the human realm" (p. 52). Posthumanism "does not mark a separation between theory, poiesis and praxis" (p. 54).

**Question 2, "Of which 'human' is the posthuman a 'post'?" (sections 8-15, pp. 55-83).** The hyphen is "the term of mediation" (p. 56). The human is "a verb: to humanize" (p. 56), read through de Beauvoir, Irigaray's concave mirror and Butler's performativity: "as gender is gendering, human is humanizing" (p. 60). Agamben's anthropological machine is criticized on three counts and for "the uncritical use of a sexist language" (pp. 60-64). Four cases of the human "switched on and off": chattel slavery, genocide, freak shows, witch trials (pp. 65-70). Technologies of the self of the excluded: oral history, masquerade, carnival, spirituals, spirituality (pp. 70-74). Humanitas in Terence and Cicero (pp. 76-79); Linnaeus's *Homo sapiens* and *Mammalia* with Schiebinger (pp. 79-83).

**Question 3, "Have we always been posthuman?" (sections 16-23, pp. 84-136).** Life as "a human notion" (p. 86); bios and zoe (pp. 86-88); artificial life and Kember's critique of "life as information" (p. 89); Hayles's critique of disembodiment "at the core" (p. 90); autopoiesis read as perspectivism rather than relativism (pp. 91-103), with the frog experiment examined for speciesism and sexist grammar (pp. 93-98); evolution as diversification, not progress (p. 107); posthumanities and space migration (pp. 108-113), with Roden's "Speculative Posthumanism" (p. 113). Then the ontological level: New Materialisms (pp. 114-120), Vibrating Matter (pp. 120-124), the multiverse (pp. 124-136).

### 4.3 The New Materialism pages, read closely (pp. 114-120)

These five pages are the ones the proposal already draws on. New Materialisms are "another specific movement, within the posthuman theoretical scenario" (p. 114), traceable to corporeal feminism (Grosz 1994; Braidotti 1994; Kirby 1997) and arising "as a reaction to the representationalist and constructivist radicalizations of late Postmodernity, which somehow lost track of the material" (p. 114). The postmodern annihilation of nature/culture "resulted in a clear preference for the nurtural aspects of it" (p. 114), in "a wave of radical constructivist feminist literature related to the success and major influence of Judith Butler's works" (p. 115). Barad's "the only thing that does not seem to matter anymore is matter" is quoted on p. 115 (Barad 2003: 801). Matter is "a process of materialization" that is "dynamic, shifting, inherently entangled, diffractional and performative" (p. 115, the phrase "a process of materialization" is Butler 1993: 9). Agential realism is "an extension of agency to the non-human realm" based on "a relational ontology" (p. 116). Ferrando stresses that Barad "locates her agential realism within a posthuman frame" while keeping "a critical stand from Posthumanism as well" (p. 116, Barad 2007: 136).

The turn against vitalism follows. Coole and Frost "are reintroducing a hierarchy" between materiality and "mere" matter (p. 117). Bennett's "strategic anthropomorphization" risks "a humanistic assimilation, which dissolves the original encounter with alterity" (p. 119); her "Nicene Creed" "sounds like a reformulation of the Abrahamic God" (p. 119); "Bennett's subject is the human, more specifically, the good willing human" (p. 119); "What is missing is the reciprocal constitution of agents, the intra-acting of material forces" (p. 119). Against this, "Barad's theoretical investigation on matter is beyond good and evil" (p. 119). Vitalism is refused because it "relies upon the notion of life (vita in Latin), which ... carries a series of pre-scientific assumptions" (p. 120).

### 4.4 The physics pages (pp. 120-136)

Bohr appears only in two notes, for the 1913 atom (p. 120 n. 171) and for the 1935 reply to Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen, "The procedure of measurement has an essential influence on the conditions on which the very definition of the physical quantities in question rests" (p. 122 n. 175). Complementarity is not named. The physics she builds on is string theory, with the caveat that it "is a mathematical model not supported by experimental evidence yet" (p. 120 n. 173), and the multiverse, "not a theory, but a prediction of certain theories" (Tegmark, pp. 123 n. 176, 124). The observer effect "has demonstrated the inextricable relation between the subject and the object" (p. 122) and "Quantum physics leads to a relational ontology" (p. 122). Her own proposal, the "posthuman multiverse", is "both as a thought experiment ... as well as a material hypothesis" (p. 131): the same matter "would also be actualizing an indefinite number of other universes", each held together by "a specific type of vibration", "like a radio" (p. 131). It rejects "many versions of 'you'" (p. 132) and ends in "a pluralistic monism, or a monistic pluralism" (p. 132). The rhizome is borrowed but "the two notions cannot be assimilated" because a posthuman multiverse "does not necessarily exclude the notion of a structure" (p. 134). Becomings "operate as technologies of the multiverse, as modes of revealing, to go back to Heidegger" (p. 135). Nietzsche's "subject as multiplicity" is recalled in n. 200 (p. 133).

## Part 6. 2016 Genealogy: "A feminist genealogy of posthuman aesthetics in the visual arts"

### 6.1 The file

### 6.2 The method stated (p. 2)

The article "aims to reassemble a feminist genealogy of the posthuman in the arts", of visual works by "(self-identified) female artists" after first-wave feminism (p. 1). The methodological premises (p. 2) acknowledge that the focus "could be criticized as essentialist" and answer that "such a move is currently needed for strategic reasons, to re-establish an inclusive genealogy of the posthuman itself", because "By going mainstream, the hierarchical schemata that (Critical, Cultural and Philosophical) Posthumanism (Wolfe, 2010; Braidotti, 2013) wishes to deconstruct are reappearing, affected by what may be defined as hegemonic essentialism" (p. 2, self-quoting 2012: 13). "I will adopt a strategic essentialist standpoint (Spivak, 1987)" (p. 2). The visual arts are chosen because "Our posthuman present is visual, interactive and linkable; the power of representation in knowledge production is becoming less and less innocent, if it has ever been" (p. 2), with Halberstam and Livingston, "The posthuman body is a technology, a screen, a projected image" (p. 2). ### 6.3 The genealogy, by section

- **Futurist, dada and surrealist grandmothers (pp. 2-5).** Futurism's break from the past is "one of the crucial differences with Posthumanism, which, to fully embrace the future, does not disregard the past" (p. 3); Marinetti's "mechanical man" as "the fatherhood" of the cyborg (p. 3); Goncharova's Rayonism as "a pre-intuition of the central role of light in virtual reality" (p. 3); Olga Biglieri Scurto, "Barbara", from Futurism to Irigaray to the peace movement (p. 3). Dada: Höch's collages and Biro's "motherhood of the cyborg" (p. 3); collage and assemblage as dada techniques "the dada roots of the posthuman" (p. 4); "Posthumanism also shares with Dadaism the acceptance of the nonsense" (p. 4). Surrealism: Fini's masquerade (pp. 4-5); Kahlo's pain against "enthusiastic transhuman accounts of techno-bodies" (p. 5), "I never painted dreams.
