## 1. What this text is

Not a research article. It is a retrospective on her own writing practice,
written for a comparative literature readership, twenty years after *Nomadic
Subjects* (1994) and three years after she rewrote it (2nd edition, Columbia,
2011). She describes the project as a trilogy:

| Book | Year | What it does, in her words |
|---|---|---|
| *Nomadic Subjects* | 1994 (2nd ed. 2011) | the original. "spun off two other volumes" |
| *Metamorphoses: Towards a Materialist Theory of Becoming* | 2002 | "explores the cultural politics of the nomadic condition" |
| ***Transpositions: On Nomadic Ethics*** | 2006 | "the ethical implications" |

The third is the one already load-bearing elsewhere in this project: Schwab's
2018 *Transpositions* volume takes its title and its Braidotti chapter from it,
and that volume is where Malabou enters the comps draft. See
`MALABOU_sources.md` section 2 and `NOTES_to_chase.md` section 2.

Her own account of the shape: "The project of nomadic subjectivity is quite
rhizomatic in itself and it grew organically from a cluster of central and
inter-related ideas. It is as if there is no possible conclusion, but only more
productive proliferations and successive unfolding."

### Seven sections

| Section | What it covers |
|---|---|
| A Matter of Style | writing as intransitive; post-structuralism and language; the four writers' tactics; Foucault's "death of Man"; the trilogy |
| Multilingualism | translation without originals; accent; the writer/reader pact; potestas and potentia |
| Critique as Creativity | Spinoza, monism, vitalist materialism, radical immanence, matter-realism. George Eliot epigraph |
| Nomadic Becoming | becoming-minoritarian; the onrush of data; thresholds of sustainability; **style as navigational tool**; memory and the imagination |
| What Virginia Woolf Knows | Chronos and Aion; haecceity; the future perfect; desire as plenitude; Deleuze's ambivalence about becoming-woman |
| Global Hybridity, or: the Perverse Nomadism of Advanced Capitalism | the rewrite; **flexibility**; surveillance society; disposable bodies |
| The Critique of the Centre from the Centre | politics of location; **figurations are not metaphors**; Europe; the division of critical labour |
| Conclusion | the nomadic subject as political fiction; the imaginative deficit; "jet-lag" |

## 2. Writing as intransitive

The opening claim, and the one everything else hangs from:

> "At the beginning of it all, for my generation, is the commitment to writing.
> Presented as a form of political and ethical engagement, it is essentially a
> visceral gesture. **Writing is an intransitive activity, a variation on
> breathing, an end in itself**; it is an affective and geometrically rigorous
> mode of inscription into life."

And: "Writing is living intensively and inhabiting language as a site of
multiple others within what we call, out of habit and intellectual laziness,
'the self'."

The autobiographical detail: a diary since the age of eleven, 163 booklets, and
a neologism she invented for it, **"grapho-maniac"** (n. 4: "Neologism of my
invention to describe people, mostly women for whom writing is a life-giving
mediating factor"). When students boast of a digital second life she feels "a
touch of pain in my heart at the thought of the 163 booklets into which I have
replicated my life... some 'virtual reality' that is!"

### The four tactics table

She sets out the post-structuralist options as a matched set, twice over, and it
is worth having side by side:

| Thinker | How the writer handles language | What the writer makes the master signifier do |
|---|---|---|
| Deleuze | tricks | **stutter** |
| Foucault | decodes | falter |
| Derrida | unveils | expose its drive to mastery |
| Barthes | seduces | reveal its affective core |

**The Deleuze stutter is the same one Bolt uses**, where it is "he causes
language as such to stutter" and "the body becomes language." See
`APPARATUS_vs_ASSEMBLAGE.md` part 1. Braidotti and Bolt reach it from opposite
directions, Braidotti through writing practice, Bolt through handling. Neither
cites the other.

Two "styles," both available at once: **resistance** ("the writer's task is to
resist the gravitational pull of the master signifier and oppose it") and
**ethics** ("unveiling this complex and paradoxical political economy and
exploring its complexity and inner contradictions"). "The tactics of resistance
and the ethical approach are not only mutually compatible but also
inter-linked."

What the critique is aimed at includes the institution, named without hedging:
"the institutions that uphold and sustain that power, notably the university
structure of departments, institutes, faculties and the whole hierarchical
disciplinary machinery that spreads to specialized journals, citation indexes
and careers management."

And the instruction she was given by her teachers (Foucault, Irigaray, Deleuze):

> "I was trained never to write carelessly or just functionally: **style is of
> the essence. Style however is no mere rhetorical decoration, but rather the
> deployment of fundamental conceptual premises.** What those thinkers taught my
> generation is never to cater for the doxa, never to flatter our common sense,
> never play into the set habits of predictable readers. Writing, even and
> especially academic writing, has to challenge and destabilize, intrigue and
> empower."

## 3. Multilingualism, and writing with an accent

Second epigraph, Gertrude Stein: "What's the use of roots if you can't take them
with you?"

The core of this section:

> "My first book *Patterns of Dissonance* was originally drafted in French and
> had to be translated, but in the final version I re-wrote it directly in
> English. By the time it went to press, therefore, it had become **a
> translation without originals**. My work as a thinker has no mother tongue,
> only a succession of translations, displacements and adaptations to changing
> conditions. **Nomadism for me equals multi-lingualism.**"

Then the part that is about sound rather than text:

> "Although this entails large doses of lexical contamination and the occasional
> syntactical debacle, the real 'creolisation' effects have always been, for me,
> **acoustic**. Accents are the traces of my multiple linguistic homes. They
> spell my own **ecology of belonging**, my loyalty to parallel yet divergent
> lives. **I'm always writing with an accent.**"

"Creolisation" is sourced to Glissant, *Poetics of Relation* (n. 11). "Writing
with an accent" is her own title elsewhere: Braidotti, "Thinking with an Accent:
Françoise Collin, *Les cahiers du Grif*, and French Feminism," *Signs* 39, no. 3
(2014): 597-626 (n. 12).

On the translations she cannot read: "several selections of my essays have been
translated in many languages I do not speak (from Finnish to Korean, Hungarian,
Chinese and Spanish), creating a slight sense of estrangement from my own
brain-children: **they are assemblages or combinations that defy any original.**
I have accepted their resilient autonomy and have let them go: **nomadic writing
is an act of constant dispossession.**"

Her term for herself: **"di-vidual"** (n. 9: "In nomadic thought: a singularity
bounded by its own powers to endure intensities and relations to others").

### The pact with the reader

> "As readers and writers in an intensive mode, we are **transformers of
> intellectual energy, processors of the 'insights'** that we are exchanging and
> co-creators of affective inter-linkages. These 'in'-sights, however, are not
> to be thought of as plunging us inwards, towards a mythical 'inner' reservoir
> of identity-bound truth. On the contrary, **they are forces that propel us
> outwards** along the multiple directions of extra-textual collective
> connections and experiences."

And: "a web of connections to be drawn on the **zig-zagging paths** of shared
subjectivity and not merely on the **tightrope of identity**."

### Two words for power

| Latin | What it names |
|---|---|
| **potestas** | the reactive, restrictive, institutionalised pole. "can only replicate and perpetuate" |
| **potentia** | the active, creative pole. Only this "has the power to generate qualitative shifts" |
| **conatus** | the desire to become. "a founding, primary, vital, necessary and therefore original desire" |

"'Subjectivity' names the process that consists in stringing the reactive
(*potestas*) and the active instances of power (*potentia*) together, **under the
fictional unity of a grammatical 'I'**."

## 4. Monism, and where the materialism comes from

Third epigraph, George Eliot, *Middlemarch* p. 226: "If we had a keen vision and
feeling of all ordinary human life, it would be like hearing the grass grow and
the squirrel's heartbeat, and we should die of that roar which lies on the other
side of silence. As it is, the quickest of us walk out well wadded with
stupidity."

She notes that **George Eliot translated Spinoza into English**, and uses the
quotation as the Spinozist figure for the whole essay: "The roar which lies on
the other side of silence is the Spinozist indicator of the raw cosmic energy
that lies within, beneath and alongside the subjects."

The chain of terms, in her order:

| Term | Definition as given |
|---|---|
| monism | matter, world and humans are not dualistic. "matter is one, driven by the desire for self-expression and ontologically free" |
| vitalist materialism | the result of the active concept of monism: "matter as vital and self-organizing" |
| radical immanence | the same position named by its rejection of transcendentalism |
| **matter-realism** | "an auto-poietic notion that replaces more static definitions of 'materialism'" (n. 19, Fraser, Kember and Lury, *Inventive Life*, 2006) |

The historical claim: Spinoza's monism was attacked by Hegel and the
Marxist-Hegelians as "politically ineffective and holistic at heart," and was
rehabilitated in 1970s France (n. 17 gives the sequence: Althusser's group from
the mid-1960s, Deleuze's Spinoza study 1968, Macherey's Hegel-Spinoza 1979,
Negri on the imagination in Spinoza 1981).

**"The self as the internalized score of cosmic vibrations"** is footnoted to
**Deleuze, *The Fold: Leibniz and the Baroque*** (n. 16), together with *What is
Philosophy?*. That is the only place the fold is sourced in this essay.

## 5. Style as a navigational tool

This is the paragraph to come back to.

> "In terms of writing practices, the processes and flows of becoming, and the
> heightened states of perception and receptivity which they both assume and
> engender defy the canonical genre classifications and **instal a sort of
> parallelism between the arts, sciences and conceptual thinking**. The point of
> convergence is the quest for creativity, in the form of experimenting with the
> immersion of one's sensibility in the field of forces, formatted as by music,
> colour, sound, light, speed, temperature, intensity. Deleuze and Guattari
> argue for instance that writers speak the unsayable; painters make visible
> forces that previously were not, much as composers make us hear sounds that
> were unheard of. Similarly, philosophers can make thinkable concepts that did
> not exist before. **Artistic genres are variables co-existing along a
> continuum.** It comes down again to the question of style, where **style is a
> navigational tool. It negotiates our path across sets of material
> ('matter-real') coordinates** that, assembled and composed in a sustainable
> and enduring manner, allow for the qualitative transformation of the affects
> and the forces involved. They thus trigger the process of becoming."

Deleuze and Guattari on the three arts is n. 22, *What is Philosophy?*.

### The onrush, and holding it

The preceding pages are about reception, and they are unusually physical:

> "What gets activated is a seemingly absent-minded floating attention or a fluid
> sensibility that is porous to the outside and which our culture has coded as
> 'feminine'. ... It combines **the accuracy of the cartographer with the
> hyper-sensitivity of the sensualist** in apprehending the precise quality of an
> assemblage of elements, like the shade of the light at dusk, or the curve of
> the wind just before the rain falls."

> "**This onrush of data, information, affectivity**, is the relational bond that
> simultaneously propels the self out of the black hole of its atomized isolation
> and disperses it into a myriad of bits and pieces of data imprinting or
> impressions. **Conceptualized by Deleuze as the folding in and out of
> perception**, it also confirms the singularity of that particular entity which
> both receives and recomposes itself around the onrush of data and affects."

> "One needs to be able to sustain the impact with the onrushing affectivity, to
> 'hold' it, without being completely overwhelmed by it. But 'holding' it or
> capturing it does not occur on the paranoid or rapacious model of a dominant,
> dialectically driven consciousness. It rather takes the form of a sustainable
> model of relational interconnections by an affective and highly receptive
> subject which quite simply **is not one, not there, not that** - it is always
> becoming. The singularity of this nomadic, floating subjectivity rests on the
> **meta-stability of thresholds of sustainability.** ... What is mobilized is
> one's capacity to feel, sense, process and sustain the impact with the complex
> materiality of that 'outside'."

Memory, in the same register: "When you remember in the intensive or
minority-mode, however, you defeat linearity, to open up spaces of movement and
of de-territorialization that actualize the virtual possibilities which have
been frozen in the image of the past." And: "When you remember to become what
you are, a subject-in-becoming, you actually reinvent yourself on the basis of
what you hope you could become, with a little help from your friends."

"Subjects are collective assemblages, that is to say they are dynamic, but
framed: fields of forces that aim at duration and affirmative self-realization.
In order to fulfil them, they need to be drawn together along a line of
composition. **This is rather like pitching a musical tone.**"

## 6. Woolf, and two kinds of time

| | |
|---|---|
| **Chronos** | the linear one |
| **Aion** | "the undifferentiated one," "the 'pure empty form of time', free of content, which is shot through with vibrations of becoming. If this be chaos, it is not chaotic, but generative" (n. 23, Ansell Pearson, *Germinal Life*) |
| **haecceity** | "or individuated aggregate, is the specific and highly contingent actualization of a field of forces stable enough and consolidated by their structural affinity, so as to be able to constitute a plane of immanence" |

"Remembering in the nomadic mode is the active reinvention of a self that is
**joyfully discontinuous**, as opposed to being mournfully consistent, as
programmed by phallogocentric culture."

> "The tense that best expresses the power of the imagination is the **future
> perfect: 'I will have been free.'** Quoting Virginia Woolf, Deleuze also says:
> 'This will be childhood, but it must not be my childhood'."

(The Deleuze quotation is n. 24, *A Thousand Plateaus*, p. 294.)

On Woolf herself: "her ability to present her life as a gesture of passing
through, i.e. of **writing 'as if already gone'**, in a vitalist and productive
relationship to mortality." And: "She is the writer of multiple and intransitive
becomings, in-between ages, sexes, elements, characters. ... **She is an
intensive multiplier of affects.**" Also: "She expresses with stark intensity the
pain involved in trying to synchronize the heterogeneity of life as **zoe**, as
positive vitality." (This is the essay's only use of *zoe*.)

**The disagreement she registers with Deleuze**, stated plainly and then
deferred:

> "Although Deleuze recognizes the extraordinary position of Woolf as a conveyor
> or relay point for this passionate process of becoming in both *Dialogues* and
> *A Thousand Plateaus* he is very careful to disengage Woolf's work from her
> being-a-woman, and even more from the 'écriture féminine' style made popular by
> sexual difference feminism in the 1980s. ... Nonetheless, as I argued at length
> elsewhere, **Deleuze cannot resolve his ambivalence towards it.**"

The "elsewhere" of n. 27 is all three books of the trilogy.

### The text as laboratory

> "The intensive text is an experimental site, **a laboratory for the new** in
> the sense of the actualizations of experiments in becoming. The literary text
> as an experiment in sustainable models of change is grounded in accurate
> knowledge and **subjected to the same rigorous rules of verification as science
> or philosophy**. This fundamental parallelism cuts across different areas,
> disciplines and textual genres. Life, science and art are equally enlisted to
> the project of experimenting with transformations."

## 7. Flexibility

**Malabou is not cited in this essay. Neither is Barad.** Counts across the whole
text: Malabou 0, plastic 0, Barad 0, diffraction 0. And yet the flexibility
argument is the same one.

> "The perverse hybridization induced by advanced capitalism translates in
> socio-economic terms in the state of so-called **'flexibility'** of a large
> proportion of the working force. Interim, un-tenured, part-time, sub-standard,
> underpaid work has become the norm in most advanced liberal economies. **The
> universities and the research world are far from immune** from this
> fragmentation and exploitative approach. This negative and exploitative brand
> of capitalist flexibility induces the fracture of life-long careers or
> professions, offering little compensation in return."

> "Advanced capitalism is a **surveillance society, a system of a centre-less but
> constant security** which pervades the entire social fabric."

Set that next to Malabou as Mazet reports her: management structures "becoming
more decentralized and horizontal than ever," the individual "left to
themselves, fully responsible for every action and expected to adapt to every
situation," and flexibility as "a passive state, in the sense that one changes to
adapt instead of creating." Braidotti reaches the identical critique from
political economy and the politics of location; Malabou reaches it from
neurobiology. See `MAZET_BrainIsTheScreen_closeread.md` section 3.

Braidotti's own term for the thing is **"perverse nomadism"** or
**"pseudo-nomadism,"** credited to Hardt and Negri, Virno, and *Multitudes* (n.
29), with the difference that "genealogically it plunges its roots in feminist
theory and anti-racist politics."

> "Goods, commodities and data circulate much more freely than human subjects
> or, in some cases, the less-than-human subjects who constitute the bulk of
> asylum-seekers and illegal inhabitants of the world. A commodified form of
> pluralism is the capitalist brand of opportunistic nomadism that proliferates
> today. **The dense materiality of bodies caught in the very concrete conditions
> of advanced global societies flatly contradicts advanced capitalism's claims to
> being 'immaterial', 'flowing' or virtual.**"

Vandana Shiva is quoted on the two mobilities (n. 35, in Mies and Shiva,
*Ecofeminism*, p. 98): "One group is mobile on a world scale, with no country, no
home, but the whole world as its property, the other has lost even the mobility
within rootedness, lives in refugee camps, resettlement colonies and reserves."

Agamben is used for the global city and the refugee camp as "two sides of the
same global coin" (n. 34). Bauman for the tourist against the pilgrim, "a subject
that combines loyalty with itinerant life-patterns" (n. 36).

## 8. Figurations are not metaphors

Her own statement, in her own words, recorded here without comment:

> "**A figuration is a living map, a transformative account of the self; it's no
> metaphor. Figurations are not figurative ways of thinking, but rather
> materialistic mappings of situated, i.e. embedded and embodied, social
> positions.** Being nomadic is not a glamorous state of jet setting, integral to
> and complicitous with advanced capitalism."

> "Being homeless; a migrant; an exile; a refugee; a tourist; a rape-in-war
> victim; an itinerant migrant; an illegal immigrant; an expatriate; a mail-order
> bride; a foreign caretaker of the young or the elderly of the economically
> developed world; a high-flying professional; a global venture financial expert;
> a humanitarian relief worker in the UN global system; a citizen of a country
> that no longer exists (Yugoslavia; Czechoslovakia; the Soviet Union) - **these
> are no metaphors.** Having no passport or having too many of them is neither
> equivalent nor is it merely metaphorical. These are highly specific geo-political
> and historical locations - **it's history and belonging tattooed on your body.**
> One may be empowered or beautified by it, or be scarred, hurt and wounded by it.
> Learning to tell the difference among different forms of non-unitary,
> multilayered or diasporic subjectivity is therefore a key ethical but also
> methodological issue. Figurations attempt to draw a cartography of the
> power-relations that define these respective and diverging positions. **They
> don't aim to embellish or metaphorize**: they just express different
> socio-economic and symbolic locations."

Earlier in the same section: "This philosophical line of enquiry is not to
suggest, however, that the nomadic subject should ever be taken as **a new
metaphor for the human condition**. Such generalizations are not helpful in
times of fast changes."

The conclusion then opens: "**The nomadic subject is a myth, or a political
fiction**, that allows me to think through and move across established
categories and levels of experience." Both sentences are hers; the relation
between them is not worked out in the essay, and that is left as it stands here.

Also in the conclusion: "Not all nomads are world travellers; some of the
greatest trips can take place without physically moving from one's habitat. It is
the subversion of set conventions and the consciousness-raising that defines the
nomadic state, not the literal act of travelling."

And the diagnosis she ends on, "the **imaginative deficit**," a gap between how
we live and how we represent that living to ourselves: "This imaginative deficit
can be read as the **'jet-lag' problem** of being behind one's time, or inhabiting
simultaneously different time-zones."

Last line: "So as to be worthy of our times, while resisting them, we need to go
on becoming."

## 11. Things I noticed (mine, not tags)

Kept separate so nothing here lands on the material itself.

- The essay is organised around sound more than sight: accent, creolisation as
  acoustic, pitching a musical tone, the roar on the other side of silence,
  composers making us hear the unheard, hearing the squirrel's heartbeat. Vision
  shows up as the cartographer's accuracy, which is a measuring faculty, not a
  looking one.
- Four of the seven sections take their title or epigraph from a woman novelist
  (Woolf twice, Stein, Eliot). The philosophers are all in the body text.
- Three terms arrive with their own counter-term rather than a definition:
  potentia against potestas, Aion against Chronos, nomadism against
  pseudo-nomadism. Flexibility is the fourth, and it is the counter-term without
  its pair being named in this essay.
- Braidotti's "flexibility" and Malabou's "flexibility" are the same critique
  with no shared citation between them. If that join gets made anywhere, it
  looks like it would have to be made rather than found.

### What the art words are doing when they appear

In all six sentences, "art" is a **term in a list of parallel knowledge
practices**, never a practice she describes from the inside. The pattern is
always the same three-part set: philosophy, the arts, science. The one place a
specific art operation is described (painters making visible forces that were
not) is quoted from Deleuze and Guattari, not from her.

The sensory vocabulary, by contrast, is hers, and it is mostly acoustic rather
than visual: accent, creolisation as acoustic, "pitching a musical tone," the
roar on the other side of silence, hearing the grass grow and the squirrel's
heartbeat, composers making us hear the unheard. Sight enters as the
cartographer's accuracy and as the artist's eye understood as receptivity, not
as looking at anything.

The one list where the arts arrive as material rather than as a discipline: the
field of forces "formatted as by **music, colour, sound, light, speed,
temperature, intensity**."

### What this one supplies that the 2014 essay does not

- **zoe defined against Agamben.** "I refer to this generative force as 'zoe',

  that constructs possible futures." The 2014 essay uses *zoe* once and cites
  Agamben once, but never connects them. This does.

  Deleuze's critique of feminism. The 2014 essay defers that argument to
  "elsewhere." This is part of the elsewhere.

  formation of all possible forms," cited to Deleuze, *Logique du Sens* (1969).
  The 2014 essay makes the same claim about Aion and cites Ansell Pearson

  Braidotti*** (Columbia, 2011b), which is not on the drives and is not
  mentioned in the 2014 essay.

   110-127**. One is wrong, and the OCR could also have garbled the first.
