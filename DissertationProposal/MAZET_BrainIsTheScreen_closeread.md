## 2. What it opens with

The Deleuze sentence, from an interview in *Cahiers du Cinéma*, February 1986,
on *Cinema 2: The Time-Image*:

> "The brain is unity. The brain is the screen. I don't believe that linguistics
> and psychoanalysis offer a great deal to the cinema. On the contrary, the
> biology of the brain, molecular biology, does. Thought is molecular. Molecular
> speeds make up the slow beings that we are. ... The circuits and linkages of
> the brain don't pre-exist the stimuli, corpuscles and particles that trace
> them. ... Cinema, precisely because it puts the image in motion, or rather
> endows the image with self-motion, never stops tracing the circuits of the
> brain."

Cited as Deleuze, "The Brain Is the Screen: Interview with Gilles Deleuze on
'The Time-Image'," trans. Melissa McMuhan, *Discourse* 20, no. 3 (1998), JSTOR
41389498, quoted at pp. 48-49, and as quoted in Pisters, *The Neuro-Image*, p.
3.

Note "le cerveau, c'est l'écran" is the French. The thesis's point of departure
is that over thirty years have passed and the sentence needs recontextualising
in a screen culture.

### The four-term distinction, set out in one paragraph

This is the clearest statement of it anywhere in the folders:

| term | what it does | Malabou p. |
|---|---|---|
| **rigidity** | fixed, cannot change form. The opposite of plasticity | 4-5 |
| **elasticity** | changes form but always returns to the original | 15 |
| **flexibility** | changes to adapt instead of creating. A passive state, and only a small part of plasticity. The term that blinds us | 9, 12 |
| **plasticity** | gives and receives form, retains the new form, cannot necessarily go back. Creation and destruction at once | 5, 15 |

Mazet's own addition, on the politics: the misplaced focus on flexibility comes
partly from the neurosciences, "all the while however capitalism produces the
same flexibility reinforcing in turn the neuroscientific descriptions." The
loop. And: "this flexibility is also destructive since a lot of (plastic)
potential is discarded."

Deleuze's answer to the loop, via Pisters, *The Neuro-Image* p. 7: "According to
Deleuze this dynamic can only be truly resisted **through art**."

## 4. Brain and screen

The reading of the copula. "'The brain is the screen' does not mean that one
represents the other or vice versa. The bond between them is more than a simple
reflection or identity, but rather refers to a type of **cocreation, a shared
plasticity** and a potential to 'form and deform into the world'."

Deleuze, from the same interview (p. 49): "the screen, that is to say ourselves,
can be the deficient brain of an idiot as easily as a creative brain." Bad
cinema follows circuits already present, so it is representation. True cinema
works like a creative brain, "creating new paths instead of simply following
them."

The screen is placed neither inside nor outside: "its position is both in as
well as out, making it inherently contradict itself. This contradiction aids in
deconstructing the dichotomy between inside and outside." Cazeaux is cited for
thought boundaries being "indivisible from the kinds of boundary we encounter in
visual experience."

**Mirror neurons** (Pisters, pp. 30, 116-21) are used as the physical case: the
mechanism works the same whether the person acted is in front of us or on a
screen, and the action is stopped somewhere in the brain. "In this sense the
brain itself works as a screen, filtering stimuli that have entered it and
producing responses that do not directly represent what we see." Then:
"spectatorship is thus not a passive state and implies a becoming (a creation of
difference) as well, at least in neurological terms."

## 5. Nomadism

Via Claire Colebrook, "Nomadicism," in *The Deleuze Dictionary*, pp. 185-88, and
Dudley Andrew, "The Roots of the Nomadic," in Flaxman ed., *The Brain Is the
Screen* (Minnesota, 2000), pp. 215-49.

"Cinema now inhabits territory in a nomadic way. This means that its existing
structures and the digital space which our technology provides do not determine
it, but rather that cinema 'fills the space from within'." And: "nomadic space
is produced through its distribution."

The nomadic films she means "are specific, so specific that they actually might
not 'represent' anyone at all"; they create a people, through "a sort of
'personal fiction'," films about peoples that are missing or fragmented; they
have "no guiding principle" and no coherent aesthetic, yet remain political.

The archive goes with it: a "living archive," "deep remixability," film released
in alternative versions, anyone able to upload their own montages or voice-overs
and so change meaning and memory through the digital archive.

## 6. Diffraction, and where it meets plasticity

This is the section to read if only one is read. It joins the two things that
are already central in the comps (diffraction, Barad) to the one that is new
(plasticity, Malabou).

**The move against mirrors.** Deleuze praises mirrors for the crystal-image;
Barad rejects reflection. Mazet's resolution: Deleuze's mirrors "can also be
oblique, concave or convex, creating a distorted image of the actual," so they
go further than a truthful representation, which answers Barad's objection that
reflexivity supposes representations reflect reality and so puts "mirrors upon
mirrors" (*Meeting the Universe Halfway*, p. 88) without creating anything new.

And the line that ties the two together:

> "Reflections are representations; they do not make a difference, they do not
> create and thus they are **not 'plastic'**. ... As Malabou states as the final
> answer to her question: 'Not to replicate the caricature of the world: this is
> what we should do with our brain.'"

So reflection is to diffraction as flexibility is to plasticity. Both pairs
separate adapting from creating.

**The crystal is a diffraction grating.** The claim is made in one sentence and
sourced to Barad:

> "What Deleuze is leading up to with the crystal-image and its further
> development in contemporary cinema is in fact a type of diffractive cinema,
> cinema that makes a change, in our brains and therefore in the world. This
> already becomes clear in his terminology, since crystals are characterized by
> their quality as diffraction gratings."

Cited to *Meeting the Universe Halfway*, p. 83.

**Diffraction defined physically**, not as metaphor: a wave meeting a barrier it
can pass through, with one or more slits, so that the bundle spreads "as if
their bundle was sliced and rearranged." "Diffraction thus has to do with
manifold cutting and reconfiguring which results in expansion of the waves."
Then the point against binaries: "diffraction conflicts with the notion of
opposing dichotomies, because these are formed by a **single cut in two**."

Barad quoted: diffraction as "a tool of analysis for attending to and responding
to the effects of difference" (p. 72), and not only what the differences are but
"how they matter and for whom" (p. 90). "In this sense, diffraction is also
closely tied to the political."

**The cutting techniques.** Pisters is quoted on the digital: "not only can
content be remixed and recombined, but also different technologies (such as
design, animation and live action) can be recombined." Mazet: "These 'cutting
and combining' techniques encourage diffraction in cinema and **stimulate its
plastic power**."

The closing Barad quote, from the Dolphijn and van der Tuin interview in *New
Materialism: Interviews & Cartographies* (Open Humanities Press, 2012), p. 66:
"The 'past' was never simply there to begin with, and the 'future' is not what
will unfold, but 'past' and 'future' are iteratively reconfigured and enfolded."

And the last Deleuze quote, *Cinema II*, p. 130: "[t]he screen itself is the
cerebral membrane where immediate and direct confrontations take place between
the past and the future, the inside and the outside, at a distance impossible to
determine, independent of any fixed point."

## 7. What it gives this project

Stated flatly, not as an argument.

- A full page map of the Malabou book, enough to work with until the book itself
  is in hand. Section 3 above.
- The four-term distinction, plasticity against rigidity, elasticity and
  flexibility, with page numbers.
- Malabou's own art examples are films (Resnais, Kubrick, Deleuze's cinema
  books), so the book is already running through artworks, not only through
  neurology.
- A worked case of **diffraction and plasticity in the same argument**, which is
  the join that Yergeau's p. 133 leaves unmade.
- Reflection is to diffraction as flexibility is to plasticity. Both are the
  adapting half, not the creating half. The same halving Yergeau finds in ABA
  discourse.
- Barad, p. 83: the crystal is a diffraction grating. A physical fact, not an
  analogy, and it is why the thesis's cover image is a grating.
- Pisters as a second route into Malabou, through *The Neuro-Image* and through
  "Temporal Explorations in Cosmic Consciousness: Intra-Agential Entanglements
  and the Neuro-Image," *Cultural Studies Review* 21, no. 2 (2015): 1-12, DOI
  10.5130/csr.v21i2.4323, which is open access.
