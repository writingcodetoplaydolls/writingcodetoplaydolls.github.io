## 1. The short answer

The worry is correctly identified, and all four sources treat it as the central problem rather than a side issue. Warfield states the clash directly: autoethnography rests on a humanist "auto," posthumanism rests on a decentered subject (Warfield 2019, p. 148; Wilde quotes the same line).

But the four sources converge on an answer that reframes the problem. Anthropocentrism is not a question of **who narrates**. It is a question of **what is granted agency** and **what is claimed as mastery**. A first-person account is anthropocentric when the human narrator is positioned as author, owner and master of the account. It is not anthropocentric merely because a human is speaking.

Two of the sources go further and argue the opposite of the worry: *refusing* the first person is the more anthropocentric move. Wilde's formulation is that writing "as" a nonhuman would be "not only arrogant but anthropocentric too," because it claims a human can know what it is not (Wilde, section "Not Mine"). She routes this through Kohn (2013, p. 21) on projecting human assumptions onto nonhumans and then asking them for "corrective reflections of ourselves." Her closing line in that section is the usable one: she does not know what it is to be not-herself entirely, but she knows the self is not itself entirely.

Herbrechter arrives at the same place from deconstruction: poststructuralism never actually proclaimed the death of the subject, because anything human is unthinkable without subjectivity. Posthuman conditions therefore produce "a proliferation of subjectivity," not an end to it (Herbrechter, "Addressing the Posthumanist Subject").

So the defensible position is: keep the first person, and change what it is *for*.

## 3. The structural answer, which is the one that survives review

Warfield's article is the model because its defense against the anthropocentrism charge is built into the design rather than stated in the prose. Four structural features do the work:

1. **The object of study is not her life.** It is the becoming of a research method. The first person is the recording instrument; the method is the subject matter. The "auto" is a vantage point, not a topic.
2. **The unit of analysis is the jarring moment**, defined as the point where the values driving the method were destabilized and something had to change (pp. 149-150, 154). This is a selection rule, which means the account can be audited. A reader can ask whether a given moment meets the criterion.
3. **Nonhuman and material forces change outcomes, not just scenery.** The Lookback app's Android orientation glitch, the price gap between iPhone and Android, on-device storage limits, and the discovery that data transits US servers before Ireland (pp. 160-163) collectively force the method to be rebuilt. The participants' own response to the invisible recorder ("I know it's there, but I can't see it") kills the original design. This is the real test of a posthuman account: remove the nonhumans and the outcome changes.
4. **The conditions of production are on the page**: grant emails, the dean, the four-course teaching load, the Trump-era classroom, the salad bar in Manhattan, a customer service agent who may be a robot. Footnotes argue with the author ("Was it a 'fail?' Reword?").

Wilde's answer, by contrast, is rhetorical: nonlinear text, indented lines of flight, multiple voices on the page. It is a performance of decentering. Performances cannot be audited, which is why it is the weaker defense in a dissertation, even though it is the better reading experience.

**Recommendation:** build on Warfield's structure, take the theory from Herbrechter (especially Butler), use one Wilde paragraph to disarm the objection before a reader raises it, and use Pin-Ru Su only as a sibling example.

## 4. A test to run the chapter against

Derived from the four sources. Each item is a question a reader could actually check.

| # | Test | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Does at least one nonhuman or material force **change an outcome** in the account, rather than appearing as setting or metaphor? | Warfield (the app, the phones, the servers) |
| 2 | Is the first person positioned as instrument or site rather than author and owner? | Jackson & Mazzei via Pin-Ru Su; Wilde |
| 3 | Is there an explicit refusal to write "as" the nonhuman, stated as a refusal rather than an omission? | Wilde, "Not Mine" |
| 4 | Is there a stated selection rule for what counts as data, so the account can be audited? | Warfield (jarring) |
| 5 | Are the conditions of the "I's" own emergence shown as unavailable to it, not just as modestly acknowledged? | Butler via Herbrechter |
| 6 | Is humanism named as entangled in the writer rather than disavowed? | Wilde |
| 7 | Is anything held fixed, and is holding it fixed admitted? | Warfield's unexamined values (the thing she does *not* do) |
| 8 | Are the production conditions of the text visible (funding, institution, tools, workload, time)? | Warfield |
| 9 | **Failure-mode flag:** does the account end by restoring a competent, improved, self-mastering researcher? | Herbrechter on Warwick's *I, Cyborg* |

Item 9 is the one to watch. It is the trap all four sources are trying to avoid and that Herbrechter says Warwick falls into. Warfield comes close to it: her third jarring moment resolves into a better method and a better methodologist. A section ending on a resolved, improved self reinstates the humanist subject no matter what vocabulary surrounds it.

## 5. What these four do not cover

- **Multispecies and ecological posthumanism.** All four come from the feminist, Deleuzian and deconstructive wing, which is the human-centred wing. Herbrechter gestures at Haraway's companion species and "multi-species flourishing," and Wilde leans on Kohn, but none of the four does animal or ecological work. A reader coming from Wolfe, Haraway or Kohn will not be satisfied by these four alone. If that flank needs covering: Haraway, *When Species Meet* (2008); Wolfe, *What Is Posthumanism?* (2010); Kohn, *How Forests Think* (2013), which Wilde already uses.
- **The decolonial objection, which is more dangerous than the anthropocentrism one.** Decentering the human arrives after certain humans were never granted full humanity in the first place. Wilde gestures at this (Cole, decoloni(ali)sation, the "Western subjectivities" caveat) and Pin-Ru Su gestures at it (paperson, anti-Orientalist, Global North), but neither faces it squarely, and Herbrechter does not raise it. For a situated first-person account this objection lands harder than the anthropocentrism one and is worth pre-empting in the same breath.
- **Disability and access as material conditions of the writing "I".** Nearly absent. Wilde has two passing nods: a parenthetical about her "limited and able-bodied perspective" when discussing dictation, and a footnote directing screen-reader users to request a differently structured version. That footnote is interesting because the nonlinear form, offered as resistance, is also an access barrier, and she notices it without resolving it. This is an open lane, not a flaw in the sources.

## ADDED 29 Oct 2026: Barad answers this from the other side, and gives it a criterion

Read in `BARAD_three_texts_read.md`. **"Nature's Queer Performativity"** (*Kvinder, Kon & Forskning* nr. 1-2, 2012, pp. 25-53), held in `K:\OneDrive\UNM\Comps\Used texts`, takes up the anthropomorphism charge directly and does not concede it:

> "it would be a serious error to conclude that I am out to recount the sins of anthropomorphizing. On the contrary, I am deeply interested in 'anthropomorphizing' as an intervention for shaking loose the crusty toxic scales of anthropocentricism, where the human in its exceptional way of being gets to hold all the 'goodies' like agency, intentionality, rationality, feeling, pain, empathy, language, consciousness, imagination."

> "What I am suggesting is not strategic anthropomorphism per se, but **using the anthropomorphic moment to call the question**, not in order to reiterate the habits of projection, but rather, in fracturing the presumptions of the 'anthropos' of 'anthropocentrism', and in so doing opening up a space for response, that is, making an invitation to the other to respond by **putting oneself at risk and doing the work it takes to truly enable a response**."

This file already concluded that the defensible position is to keep the first person and change what it is for. **Barad reaches the same conclusion from the opposite direction**, starting from the nonhuman rather than from the writer, and she adds what this file did not have: **a criterion**. The anthropomorphic moment is permissible where it puts the writer at risk and does the work of enabling a response. That is a test a passage can be held against, not a position to be defended in the abstract.

Two things follow for the method. Her stated approach is "**not to invite nonhuman others into the fold of queerness, but to interrogate the binaries that support the divisions** that are at stake", which is a different operation from extending a category. And she insists the organisms are "not merely as tools to think with, but in thinking with them to face our ethical obligations to them, for they are not merely tools for our use but real living beings (and I include in this category 'inanimate' as well as 'animate' beings)."

Her underlying claim is that the cut comes first: "the very practices of differentiating the 'human' from the 'nonhuman', the 'animate' from the 'inanimate', and the 'cultural' from the 'natural' produce crucial materializing effects that are unaccounted for by **starting an analysis after these boundaries are in place**." On moralism, which is the register the objection usually arrives in: "**Moralism, which feeds off of human exceptionalism**."

**ADDED the same day, a first-person precedent from Barad.** In "Diffracting Diffraction" (*parallax* 20(3), 2014, 168-187, also held) she writes a dated, placed, named scene into the argument: "Crossroads/Diffraction: Santa Cruz and Claremont, CA 1991. I am sitting outdoors with Gloria Anzaldua talking about quantum physics and mestiza consciousness." She gives her institutional position (teaching physics at Pomona, sitting in on Deena Gonzalez's Latina Feminist Traditions class) and what they did: "Gloria and I talk about quantum physics, the two-slit diffraction experiment, waves and particles and *mita' y mita'*. We are happily making diffraction patterns." For the purposes of this file that is worth more than another theory of the first person, because it is the first person used as apparatus by the theorist whose apparatus the proposal borrows.
