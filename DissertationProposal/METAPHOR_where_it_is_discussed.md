# Where metaphor's standing is discussed, candidates

29 Oct 2026. Written in answer to a recollection of having seen a discussion in which a metaphor is not a lesser version of the thing it describes but as valid as the thing, and a thing in its own right. **That wording is Amy's paraphrase and is not quoted from any source below.** What follows is a list of the passages in the searched material that make an argument of that shape, with verbatim quotations and locations, so the one actually seen can be identified.

**None of the eight uses her wording.** They are ranked by how close the argument comes to the shape described, which is my ranking and not an identification. Candidates 1 and 2 were found first; 1a and 1b came from searching the texts this file had listed as unsearched; and **0a and 0b came from a sweep of 378 PDFs run afterwards, which is where the two strongest are.** If one of the eight is the passage, **0a, Bateson's "Metalogue: Why a Swan?", is the likeliest**: it is a whole dialogue about exactly this question, it is in a comps text, and Bateson is one of the Santa Cruz people.

## 0a. Bateson, "Metalogue: Why a Swan?". A whole discussion of exactly this question, in a comps text.

**Bateson, G. (1972), "Metalogue: Why a Swan?", in *Steps to an Ecology of Mind*, pp. 43-47.** `K:\OneDrive\UNM\Comps\Used texts\Bateson_Gregory_1972_2017_Aronson_Jason_Steps-to-an-Ecology-of-Mind.pdf`. **Found 29 Oct in the sweep described at the end of this file, and it produced more windows than any other text in the sweep, 54.** `K:\OneDrive\UNM\Comps\verified_pages` holds single-page extracts of this book at **pp. 272, 462 and 463**, so it was used in the comps work, though not at these pages.

**It is a dialogue, and the whole of it is the question described.** A metalogue, in Bateson's form, is a conversation whose structure is itself an instance of its subject. Father and Daughter are talking about the ballet *Swan Lake* and the puppet in *Petroushka*.

The question is set up as the difference between "sort of" and not "sort of":

> "F: No, I get confused when I speak of the 'swan' and the dancer as two different things. I would rather say that the thing I see on the stage, **the swan figure, is both 'sort of' human and 'sort of' swan**."

> "F: All right, let's try to analyze what 'sort of' means . . . If I say 'the puppet Petroushka is sort of human,' I state a relationship. D: Between what and what? F: Between ideas, I think. D: Not between a puppet and people? F: No. **Between some ideas that I have about a puppet and some ideas that I have about people.** D: Oh. . . . D: Well then, what sort of a relationship? F: I don't know. **A metaphoric relationship?**"

Then the turn, which is the part that matches the description:

> "F: And then there is that other relationship which is **emphatically not 'sort of.'** Many men have gone to the stake for the proposition that **the bread and wine are not 'sort of' the body and blood.**"

> "F: I mean that **if for some people the bread and wine are only a metaphor**, while for others, Catholics, the bread and wine are a sacrament; then, if there be some for whom the ballet is a metaphor, **there may be others for whom it is emphatically more than a metaphor, but rather a sacrament.**"

> "F: I mean that if we could say clearly what is meant by the proposition 'the bread and wine is not "sort of" the body and blood'; then we should know more about what we mean when we say either that the swan is 'sort of' human or that the ballet is a sacrament. D: Well, how do you tell the difference? F: Which difference? D: **Between a sacrament and a metaphor.**"

His first answer is that the difference is not in the work: "**my answer must deal with the person and not the message** . . . You ask me how I would decide whether a certain dance on a certain day is or is not sacramental for the particular dancer", and when asked he says it is "a sort of a secret", not one "that one must not tell" but "**something that one cannot tell**". The dancer herself "**can have no direct control over the matter**", and "great art and religion and all the rest of it is about this secret; but **knowing the secret in an ordinary conscious way would not give the knower control**".

**And then the resolution, which is the thing-in-its-own-right claim:**

> "F: The swan figure is **not a real swan but a pretend swan. It is also a pretend-not human being. It is also 'really' a young lady wearing a white dress.** And a real swan would resemble a young lady in certain ways.
> D: But which of these is sacramental?
> F: Oh Lord, here we go again. I can only say this: that **it is not one of these statements but their combination which constitutes a sacrament. The 'pretend' and the 'pretend-not' and the 'really' somehow get fused together into a single meaning.**
> D: But we ought to keep them separate.
> F: Yes. **That is what the logicians and the scientists try to do. But they do not create ballets that way, nor sacraments.**"

So the figure is not a weaker version of the thing, and it is not reducible to the comparison either. It is the fusion of pretend, pretend-not and really into one meaning, and the separating-out that logic and science perform is what destroys it.

**Two things that come with it.** The next metalogue, "What Is an Instinct?", carries **a seventh etymological precedent for the 1.5 row**, and the punchline with it: Newton's *hypotheses non fingo*, where "**fingo is a late Latin word for 'make.' It forms a verbal noun *fictio* from which we get the word 'fiction'**", and then "D: But didn't he discover gravity? With the apple? F: No, dear. **He invented it.**" And **Bateson is one of the Santa Cruz people** in `SANTA_CRUZ_place_and_people.md`, where he is recorded at Kresge College from 1972 and as a Regent until his death in 1980. The book is from 1972, the year he arrived.

Schwab's own summary of it, in the introduction (which was already held and read):

> "he argues that **science deploys metaphors, and hence transpositions, even when it seemingly operates with information** . . . This is particularly important **when concepts are lacking** rather than being less familiar, such as when **metaphors in cutting-edge science occupy the places of emergent knowledge over time, becoming the reality physicists work with rather than a reality 'out there' that they aim to represent** . . . Highlighting the creative act in science through the invention of metaphors, Malum Fitje argues for **a closer proximity between artistic and scientific research**."

And Fitje's own statement of it:

> "**Metaphor operates at every level of physics.**"

> "modern physics also exhibits **a more active character of metaphor** than the forthright comparative quality: an evocation of the inner connection between things, an awareness of duality, and a semblance of two different senses as one. In these terms, **the metaphor may be regarded as a transpositional act that hints at the very creation of things, blurring the gap between them and even between them and their names.** Viewed extendedly, the metaphors of physics not only appertain to linguistics but **could well affect the very process of scientific thought**."

> "I chose to refer broadly to these transpositional movements, **calling them metaphors** . . . the concept of the metaphor will be explored extendedly, **as a relocation of meaning: from idea to word, from information to image, from theory to model**. I will consider such alterations **not merely as linguistic or visual manoeuvres, but as acts of imagination, or figments of mind**."

**He names the canonical source for this position**, which nothing in this folder had: **Paul Ricoeur, *The Rule of Metaphor* (1977)**, "a book consisting of eight interrelated studies on the metaphor", which Fitje says "arrives at a similar characterisation". He also quotes **Roger Jones (1982: 115)** on models: Newton's mechanics became "the prototype for all physical theories . . . **It was only natural that people began to believe in the reality of the models.** The particle metaphor was transposed from the macroscopic to the microscopic level."

**The chapter is also three other things this folder wants, which is why it is worth reading whether or not it is the passage recollected.** It is a **camera and image chapter for 1.1**: it opens "**No one has ever seen an atom**", runs through the **Abbe diffraction limit** ("the smallest objects you can single out have to have a size of about half the wavelength of the light employed"), Bronowski's line that genes "simply won't cast a shadow", and the production of **shadow images** from electron and X-ray data, "**usually constructed through computer simulations**". It is a **traces chapter for 3.5 and 3.6**: "We can map out atoms and prove that they exist, but **what the images represent are merely their traces transposed.**" It carries an **apparatus** sentence: "**Each of these apparatuses possesses an individual limitation, and to claim that one in particular sets the standard of authenticity may turn out to be a deceitful prospect.**" And it does **the etymological argument twice in footnotes**, on Aristotle's *allotrios* ("belonging to another person . . . foreign, strange") and *kurion* ("from *kurios*, he to whom a person or thing belongs"), which with Bateson's *fingo* makes eight precedents for that row.

> **READ IN FULL, 29 Oct, and the summary I quoted was partial.** The chapter is now read end to end in `FITJE_MetaphorsOfModernPhysics_read.md`. **Fitje's own position is not the one Schwab's summary gives.** He distinguishes an **active** character of metaphor from a **stagnant** one and spends most of the chapter on the second: "the analogies have become equivalent to the thing, as if they were derived from it. **But they are solely derived from the faculty of imagination** . . . there is no thing to derive the model from. **Curiously, the models come into existence because there is no base for modelling.**" His verdict is "**Metaphors merely pose problems when they are not recognised as such; they also represent a fertility of language and a comparative capacity of mind**", and he **grades them by aptness**, calling the billiard ball inapt and dark matter apt. He also puts the objection to his own method without resolving it: "Are we not then running the risk of advocating a position from where everything may be regarded metaphorically? **This is exactly what quantum theory has already done, but without providing any replacement to fill the semantic vacuum it has left behind.**" His last sentence, prefaced "Much like in painting": "**the transpositional layering of names, concepts, and anecdotes can add meaning to an image but never make the image complete**."

## 1a. The *Posthuman Glossary*, "Neuronal Aesthetics". The best fit, and it is held.

**Braidotti, R. and Hlavajova, M. (eds) (2018), *Posthuman Glossary*, Bloomsbury**, entry **"Neuronal Aesthetics"**, around printed pp. 285-288; `K:\OneDrive\UNM\Comps\Used texts\Braidotti_Hlavajova_2018_posthuman-glossary.pdf`. The reading plan names **Patricia Pisters** as the author of this entry. Its own cross-references are "See also: Alienation; Plasticity; Neo/New Materialism", so it sits inside the plasticity strand that has been running in the other window.

The passage, reading the left column straight through (the Glossary is printed in two columns and the extraction interleaves them, so **check this against the page**):

> "As Hayles points out, computation as a relational process that can run in the brain as well as in other media is **more than a metaphor**. Or rather, **the computational metaphor is so powerful because if the technology for fast networked processing did not exist there would be no metaphor.** And so '**means and metaphor are dynamically interacting**' (ibid.: 163). What is important again is to see that the prevailing knowledge of the brain and the computer are supported by data provided by the empirical evidences of the sciences **but just as much by cultural and artistic models** that propose the organization and re-organization of our transforming conditions on another level."

Three things in it, and together they are the closest of anything found to the shape described. **The metaphor is not prior to or separable from the thing**: without the material technology there would be no metaphor at all. **"Means and metaphor are dynamically interacting"** is a parity statement, and it is quoted from Hayles at p. 163 of a work cited there only as "ibid.", so **the Hayles title and page need picking up from the entry's reference list.** And **"but just as much by cultural and artistic models"** puts artistic models level with empirical evidence as a source of what is known, which is the validity claim in six words.

The entry closes on a programme worth having for its own sake: neuronal aesthetics "would be a **new materialist approach of aesthetics** that calls for a revival of the salons of the early twentieth century where artists, writers, physiologists, medics and philosophers came" together.

## 1b. Bolt, on Deleuze: performing rather than describing

**Bolt, B. (2004), *Art Beyond Representation: The Performative Power of the Image*, I. B. Tauris**, around printed p. 161; `K:\OneDrive\UNM\Comps\compsnotused\Bolt_Barbara_2004_art beyond representation.pdf`. Read and recorded earlier in `APPARATUS_vs_ASSEMBLAGE.md`. Metaphor runs 20 times in 86,129 words, and most of them are Heidegger's "metaphorics of light" rather than a theory of metaphor.

The one passage of this shape:

> "Deleuze frequently uses visual and musical metaphors in his elaboration of the literary arts. In his view, writers paint with words whilst the words themselves paint and sing (Deleuze 1994b: 28). **However, Deleuze's understanding is not exclusively metaphoric. He argues that in literature, linguistic performativity involves setting language in motion so that it comes to perform rather than merely describe.**"

With the Deleuze passage above it: "It is not a painting or a piece of music, but **a music of words, a painting with words**, a silence within words . . . The words paint and sing but only at the limit of the path they trace through their division and combination" (Deleuze 1994b: 28). And on the next page, the stutter, which is already in the plan through Braidotti: the writer is "a foreigner in his own language . . . **The point is to make language itself cry, to make it stutter, mumble or even whisper**" (Deleuze 1994b: 25).

"Perform rather than merely describe" is the same move as "not a lesser copy", made about language rather than about metaphor as such.

## 1. Neimanis, on Basia Irland's ice books

**Neimanis, A. (2017), "Nature Represents Itself: Bibliophilia in a Changing Climate", in Kirby (ed.), *What if Culture was Nature all Along?*, ch. 9, pp. 179-198.** Held and read on 29 Oct; `KIRBY_2017_collection_FULL_read.md`. This is the art chapter, the one that opens on the Rio Grande near Albuquerque.

It is the only passage found that argues explicitly about a metaphor's **standing**, and it uses that word:

> "On such a view, Irland's books would be **nothing more than metaphor, icy replicas of cultural text**."

> the chain "comes full circle: at first unreliable and inaccessible, then turned into privileged text, and finally recast back into or as the 'real world', but **with even further diminished standing**."

> "if metaphor is a mode of linguistic representation, **what kind of representation represents in advance**, in anticipation of the language or humanicity that supposedly comes later?"

> "if on the representationalist view the text is already a stand-in, then Irland's ice books are **a stand-in for a stand-in**: ice before the book, represented metaphorically as book, now passing through the book to return as **a literalised material metaphor**, that is, a repetition both before and after the textuality it re-presents."

> "Irland's sculptures strongly **resist a representationalist metaphorisation** of ice as 'book' or river and riparian lives as literate. Instead, Irland configures representation in language as **a material process that unfurls us all**; these sculptures suggest an **originary literacy** that makes our human textual capacities intelligible and even possible."

> "ice is not to be written and read in a representationalist sense; **its primary task is not as stand-in (whether more authoritative or less) for text**."

The two phrases nearest the shape described are "**not as stand-in (whether more authoritative or less)**" and "**a literalised material metaphor**".

## 2. Haraway, on nature as a trope

**Haraway, D. (2008 in this printing), "Otherworldly Conversations; Terran Topics; Local Terms", in Alaimo & Hekman (eds), *Material Feminisms*, ch. 5.** Held in `K:\OneDrive\UNM\Comps\Used texts`; the chapter is summary-only in `MATERIAL_FEMINISMS_2008_read.md`, so this passage has not been read in context.

She does not argue that the metaphor is as valid as the thing. She argues there is no untroped thing behind it:

> "**Nature is also a *trópos*, a trope. It is figure, construction, artifact, movement, displacement. Nature cannot preexist its construction**, its articulation in heterogeneous social encounters, where all of the actors are not human and all of the humans are not 'us,' however defined. Worlds are built from such articulations. Fruitful encounters depend on a particular kind of move, a *trópos*, or 'turn.' **Faithful to the Greek, as *trópos*, nature is about turning.**"

Note that this is **a sixth Barad-adjacent etymology** for the 1.5 row, and it is Haraway's rather than Barad's: *trópos*, to turn.

**One thing in this chapter worth knowing separately, because it has the exact grammatical shape of the sentence Amy described but is not about metaphor.** Her third epigraph:

> "**Animals are not lesser humans; they are other worlds.**" (Barbara Noske, *Humans and Other Animals*)

Her second epigraph is also on the subject: "Nothing is ultimately contextual; all is constitutive" (Robert Young, *Darwin's Metaphor*).

## 3. Barad, on the IBM atom cascade

***Meeting the Universe Halfway*, in the nanotechnology discussion**, position about 66 per cent through the extraction; held, and **found by search rather than read in context, so the printed page needs confirming**.

This is the passage where she states the parity directly:

> "one gets the distinct impression that this 'cascade' experiment is not only a miniature mechanism for making computers on a scale that may soon leave silicon technologies in the dust, **but also a metaphor** for the increasingly rapid development of nanotechnologies that awaits us. **But matter and meaning, the literal and the figurative, are never as separate as we like to pretend**, and therefore no argument will be able to arrest the expanding public sentiment that the cascade experiment is **much more than a metaphor**, that the tiniest changes, rearrangements in the configurations of atoms, **hold the literal potential to tunnel across different scales of space, economy, and imagination**."

"The literal and the figurative are never as separate as we like to pretend" is the sentence to cite if this is the one.

**And a few pages later, on the brittlestar**, with a microscopist's sentence as the test case:

> "**Was the cell biologist Daniel Mazia being merely metaphorical** when he remarked that 'the gift of the great microscopist is the ability to think with the eyes and see with the brain'? Surely a plethora of statements about tacit knowing, including a wealth of testimonials offered by scientists, **suggests some more literal, material meaning**."

## 4. Kirby, in the preface to *Quantum Anthropologies*

***Quantum Anthropologies: Life at Large* (Duke, 2011), preface, printed p. xi-xii.** Held and read, `KIRBY_QuantumAnthropologies_close_read.md`.

> "if the quantum implications of what we do in the humanities and social sciences **is not an idle metaphor**, then intertextuality suggests that in some quite uncanny sense, so-called cultural critics are already practicing science, **we really are in the business of material production** . . . **Life reads and rewrites itself**, and this operation of universal genesis and reproduction **is even internal to the tiny marks on this page, which are effective transubstantiations**."

"The tiny marks on this page, which are effective transubstantiations" is the thing-in-its-own-right claim, made of writing itself.

And in her chapter 2, posed as the question and then refused:

> "Is the weirdness of this evidence rendered explicable because it reflects the epistemological intertextualities, the criss-crossings of **what can only be metaphors and models, whose cultural origins have little if anything to do with the actualities of the universe at large**? **Or** is there a more worldly form of intratextual referencing in these scientific results that collapse concept (model), observer, and observed, and disperse authorship, identity, and causality?"

Elsewhere in the same book, the same move about dismissal:

> "If we explain away such questions by attributing their strangeness to **poetic license or mere metaphor we discount the workings of biological poiesis**."
