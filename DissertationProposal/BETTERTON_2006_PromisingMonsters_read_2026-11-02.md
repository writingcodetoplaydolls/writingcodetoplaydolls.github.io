## 1. The three epigraphs, because they set the article's method

First, a midwife's casebook entry. "1733 on 10 November with Maryken, wife of the servant to the orphanage. A son. But had a face like an ape. At the back of the neck an opening as big as a hand. Its genitals were also not as they should be. She had seen apes dancing. It did not live long. Oh Lord, save us from such monsters." Catherina Schrader, eighteenth-century Friesian midwife, from Marland's 1987 edition of the memoirs.

Second, Susan Hiller on the other, from *The Myth of Primitivism*: "The other is always distant as well as different, and against this difference the characteristics of self and society are formed and clarified."

Third, Kristeva: "Within the body, growing as a graft, indomitable, there is an other. And no one is present within that simultaneously dual and alien space to signify what is going on. 'It happens, but I'm not there.' 'I cannot realise it, but it goes on.' Motherhood's impossible syllogism."

The article's opening question comes straight out of the three: "But what if that otherness is enclosed in our bodies, as yet unknown, neither friend nor enemy, growing inside our own flesh and blood?"

## 2. The argument

Stated plainly in the abstract: the paper "engages with theories of the monstrous maternal in feminist philosophy to explore how examples of visual art practice by Susan Hiller, Marc Quinn, Alison Lapper, Tracey Emin, and Cindy Sherman disrupt maternal ideals in visual culture through differently imagined body schema", and asks "whether the 'monstrous' can offer different kinds of figurations of the maternal that acknowledge the agency and potential power of the pregnant subject."

Her thesis sentence: "In this essay, I argue that pregnancy continues to be haunted by monsters in the Western visual imagination."

Her stated gap: "Less attention has been paid to the ways in which the monstrous maternal body has been represented in other aspects of visual culture", as against science-fiction cinema, where Creed's "archaic mother" in the *Alien* films has been worked over, and the mass media "celebrity body" of pregnancy, where she cites Tyler 2001.

Her four questions, kept in her order. How do these artworks "negotiate the relations between the socially constituted maternal body and the particularities of the embodied materiality of pregnancy?" If the pregnant body "exceeds regulatory social norms in certain respects, how are these processes of regulation and excess articulated in visual images, and in what ways?" And "how do women artists explore the split between self and other as productive pregnant subjects?"

## 3. The Braidotti frame

She takes it from Braidotti's 1996 essay "Signs of Wonder and Traces of Doubt: On Teratology and Embodied Differences". The etymology does the structural work: *teratos* in Greek is "both prodigy and demon, evoking fascination and horror and, as such, structurally ambiguous", so that "the monstrous helps organize structures of difference in a same/other binary between what is sacred and mutant, normal and abnormal, whether it be between sexual, racial, or human/nonhuman categories."

Two Braidotti quotations she leans on. "Monsters are linked to the female body in scientific discourse through the question of biological reproduction. Theories of the conception of monsters are at times extreme versions of the deep-seated anxiety that surrounds the issue of women's maternal power of procreation in a patriarchal society" (1996, 139). And the one that gives the pregnant body its contradictory double function: "The female, pregnant body is posited both as a protective filter and as a conductor or highly sensitive conveyor of impressions, shocks and emotions. It is both a 'neural' and somewhat 'electrical' body. There is an insidious assimilation of the pregnant woman to an unstable, potentially sick subject, vulnerable to uncontrollable emotions" (149).

Betterton's own historical list of what monstrous births were once attributed to: "women's sexual excess or perversion, the mixing of different sperm or between different races, intercourse during menstruation, eating forbidden food, or demonic possession, and in a modern twist to the theme, to toxic or genetic damage." Then the line that joins the eighteenth century to the present: "The maternal imagination was deemed to have the power to kill or deform the fetus merely through an act of illicit looking: 'She had seen apes dancing.'" And the continuity she draws: "Women in their maternal function, therefore, had to be disciplined to control their desires for the well being of the unborn child, a regulatory model that persists in contemporary injunctions on pregnant women not to smoke, drink, or take drugs."

The turn, via Haraway 1992: Braidotti "does not see the monstrous as wholly negative". Her redefinition, quoted: "I would like to propose a re-definition, the monster is a process without a stable object. It makes knowledge happen by circulating, sometimes as the irrational non-object" (1996, 150). And from *Metamorphoses*, the question "how to represent mutations, changes and transformations, rather than Being in its classical modes", with the aim of finding new figurations of hybrids and monsters as models for a "materialist theory of becoming" (2002, 2).

Betterton's own claim for art inside that frame, which is the claim the whole article rests on: works of art "can explore the tensions that pregnant bodies evoke through their 'mutations, changes and transformations,' and thus offer 'new figurations' rather than theoretical closure."

## 4. Hiller, *Ten Months*, and the refusal of the creativity metaphor

This is the section most directly on the live metaphor question in this folder, and the argument is Betterton's reading of Hiller's own text panel.

The work: a photographic installation made after Hiller's "first and only pregnancy", in "ten horizontal rectangular panels that follow the ten lunar months of pregnancy, each unit made up of a typed text and twenty-eight black and white photographs taken each day of her pregnant belly". Hiller in her own words on the subject: "the section of the body you couldn't talk about, the pregnant part". And on her position going in: "As someone who was already a mature artist [I was] aware of the metaphors of creativity that come out of pregnancy."

Hiller's panel TWO, quoted in full as the article prints it: "She must have wanted this, this predicament, these contradictions. She believes physical conception must be 'enabled' by will or desire, like any other creative process. (Pregnant with thought. Brainchild. Giving birth to an idea.)"

Betterton's reading of it is a flat refusal of the figure, not a qualification of it. "Hiller plays on the traditional metaphor of creativity in which the (male) artist claims to conceive and give birth to imaginative ideas, but as a woman artist this metaphor has become literally embodied as a paradox." And then: "Hiller exposes the contradiction that the metaphor of male creativity conceals, that actual conception and pregnancy are bodily conditions that cannot be 'enabled' by will or desire and, in this sense, are quite unlike the practices involved in making art."

Hiller's panel TEN, the split she ends on: "'seeing' (& depicting) . . . natural 'fact' (photos) / 'feeling' (& describing) . . . cultural artifact (texts)". Betterton: "Split between 'seeing' and 'feeling,' 'fact' and 'artifact,' the artist as maker of the photos and writer of the texts reproduces the doubling of the pregnant subject and her ontological uncertainty." And the division is a division of labour between the two media: "In giving voice as the pregnant subject, she separates her 'self' in the text from 'the section of the body you couldn't talk about,' which is located in the objective visible realm."

The formal reading, which is about containment failing: the pregnant part is "confined within a minimalist grid, which imposes order through formal sequence and repetition. But, as the panels progress through the ten months, the curve of the belly swells until it presses against the edge of the photographs. Its distended volume suggests an autonomous growth that can be barely contained within the rectangular frames." What emerges is "an aesthetic of embodied becoming rather than familiar closure in birth."

## 5. Quinn, Lapper, and the reading she marks as her own

She states the shape of her reading before she gives it: "But, does this subversion of bodily norms free disabled women from a confining aesthetic? I offer a more ambivalent reading here."

The work is Marc Quinn's *Alison Lapper (8 Months)* (2000), white marble, later enlarged to 4.7 metres for the fourth plinth in Trafalgar Square. Quinn's own account: he uses "the weight of tradition to undermine itself" and tries to use "beauty to conquer prejudice". Lapper's own account, as model and collaborator: "I regard it as a modern tribute to femininity, disability and motherhood. It is so rare to see disability in everyday life, let alone naked, pregnant and proud." The press reaction she records is the *London Evening Standard* headline: "So, Is This Really What We Want on Trafalgar Square's Empty Plinth?"

Her objection is to the material, and she supports it with Price and Shildrick: in order to guarantee the autonomy of the subject, "the body must appear invulnerable, predictable and consistent in form and function, above all free from the possibility of disruption" (1998, 232, her emphasis). Hence: "The choice of marble has the effect of stabilizing the potentially disruptive figure of the disabled pregnant mother, whose embodiment is immobilized in memorial form. Rather than 'becoming,' as in Hiller's work, the temporality of pregnancy appears here suspended at a fixed moment in time."

Then she declares the condition of her own viewing, and its limit, rather than leaving it implicit. "My reading is informed by viewing the sculpture in the empty 'white cube' of the gallery in a solo exhibition of Quinn's work in 2002, which in turn raises questions about the context for any reading of an artwork that are beyond the scope of this paper. The crowded urban setting of Trafalgar Square may more precisely invoke the vulnerability, unpredictability, and inconsistency of the figure as a public monument."

**The metaphor Quinn accepts, and why it matters here.** In the Tate catalogue conversation, "Quinn accepted an analogy 'between the hidden process going on in the artist's mind and the hidden process going on inside the mother's womb'". Betterton's reading: "In a double move, his assumed correspondence between the conscious creative process of the artist and the unconscious process in the maternal womb effaces Lapper's artistic autonomy and denies her agency as a maternal subject."

That is the same figure she has just shown Hiller exposing, and she immediately finds it a third time, in theory rather than in art criticism. On Kristeva's "Motherhood According to Giovanni Bellini": "While for Kristeva the maternal is a privileged realm, it remains split between the semiotic and the symbolic and the agency of the mother is erased." She closes the point with Michelle Boulous Walker's sentence: "The maternal is a metaphorical site that precludes reference to women", and Boulous Walker again on the mechanism, "the productive subject silences woman's (maternal) body by setting itself in her place."

**Lapper's own work is given as the answer.** Her self-portraits took their inspiration from the Venus de Milo; in *Angel* (1999), "Lapper's naked torso, a single wing replacing her absent arms, projects forcefully into a dark void edged with pink and red flower shapes." Betterton: "In contrast to the stasis and monumentality of Quinn's sculptural portrait, her body floats and spins through the air as though it were released from gravity." Lapper's own words, from a video transcript: "My body isn't this ugly that I had always assumed it was because I'd been told it was. Even in society today you still do it. You are disabled so therefore you are ugly. So now, I think, I almost throw myself at the public, . . . then say, well actually, look again." And on the frame she refuses: "She rejects the label 'disabled mother' and, in a demonstration of the power of maternal imagination to create monsters, she gives birth to images of herself and her child in an imaginative process of transformation."

## 6. Emin, and the question of voice

The works are the monoprint *Terrebly Wrong*, *My Bed* (1998), and the installations and films listed at endnote 12.

On the technique, which is the part of this section that does analytic rather than biographical work: "The technique of monoprint, drawing an image directly onto the plate, which is then reversed in the printing process, conveys both the immediacy of the direct trace of Emin's hand and articulates a sense of otherness, of that which is familiar having become strange. The intimacy and distance effected by the technical process mirrors the immediacy and time lag involved in its production."

On what she declines to treat it as: "Emin's insistent registration of herself as the text of her work is often misread as a direct expression of identity"; the work is "the purposeful reconstruction of the past as a set of stories and images, rather than the truth of a life"; and "while this representation of her experience gives Emin agency, it does not imply an unmediated outpouring of the truth."

On where she locates the abjection, against the critical consensus: "While the critical reception of her work usually focuses on the artist's sexuality, I suggest that abjection can be more specifically situated in the loss that it repeatedly reenacts." The loss has a formal consequence she quotes from Corby: termination "leaves the subject with nothing to show for it", registered "from the viewpoint of a failed maternal subject".

**The passage on voice.** She quotes Annette Kuhn on working-class girlhood: "Though perhaps for those of us who have learnt silence through shame, the hardest thing of all is to find a voice: not the voice of the monstrous singular ego but one that, summoning the resources of the place we come from, can speak with eloquence of, and for, that place." Her own framing of Emin around it: the work gives "to her mourning self the opportunity to speak back through her art, a means of resistance against silencing and thus a voice in relation to an experience that was literally unspeakable."

On the abortion-politics stakes, via Phelan: "the illegibility of the materiality of a pregnant body within a visual economy that everywhere marks the boundary between self and other. Embodied in and by what is and is not one body, the visibly pregnant woman makes the possibility of a continuous subject/ivity real" (1993, 171). And the contradiction Phelan names, which Betterton adopts: the maternal subject is both "the embodiment of lawful desire for reproduction within a patriarchal economy" and "the spectre of the monstrous, forever murdering/castrating, mother" (1993, 135).

Her closing judgement on Emin refuses the consoling reading: "Her work does not offer consolation or reparation for loss, but insists on its material presence traced through images and mementoes of her (un)maternal body."

## 7. Sherman, Bakhtin's hags, and prosthetic pregnancy

Two works: *Untitled #205* from *History Portraits* (1989), and *Untitled #250* from *Sex Pictures* (1992). Endnote 14 identifies the source of the first: Raphael's portrait of his mistress, *La Fornarina*.

What she says has changed from the earlier work: "a significant difference in her more recent photographs is the very obviousness of the masquerade. In *Untitled #205* (1989), from the *History Portraits* series, the old master nude is visibly faked: the face is a cosmetic mask; the prosthetic pregnant torso is hooked over her shoulders; and the open-weave, textured material of her drapery and the knotted headdress make no attempt to simulate the lustrous silks and satins of its Renaissance prototype." And the look is returned: "this is a staged encounter in which we know, as she knows, that we are being had."

Her phrase for the mechanism, borrowed from Haraway 1992: Sherman's fake pregnancies are "one means of denaturalizing the maternal body, or as Donna Haraway puts it (1992, 300), 'queering what counts as nature.'"

The disclosure she reads in it: "Sherman's figure discloses in the ambiguity and duplicity of its maternal and sexual identity the repressed understanding that the asexual maternal is always also the sexual feminine."

**Bakhtin on the "pregnant senile hags".** She quotes *Rabelais and His World* at length because the grotesque body is defined there as incomplete. Its components are "copulation, pregnancy, birth, growth, old age, disintegration and dismemberment . . . contrary to the classic image of the finished, completed man, cleansed . . . of all the scoriae of birth and development" (25). On the terracotta figurines: "There is nothing completed, nothing calm and stable in the bodies of these old hags. They combine a senile, decaying and deformed flesh with the flesh of new life, conceived but as yet unformed. Life is shown in its two-fold contradictory process; it is the epitome of incompleteness. And such is precisely the grotesque concept of the body. . . . Moreover, these old hags are laughing" (25-26). Betterton's bridge: "While potentially misogynistic, the figurines for Bakhtin do not evoke horror but represent a 'principle of growth' (26). As in Braidotti's argument, they materialize 'becoming,' a reiteration of life rather than decay."

**She then puts the counter-case rather than resolving it.** Mary Russo: "women and their bodies, certain bodies, in certain public framings, in certain public spaces, are always already transgressive, dangerous and in danger" (1986, 216), and the grotesque "might be used affirmatively to destabilise the idealisations of female beauty or to realign the mechanisms of desire" (221), which Betterton calls "necessarily a tricky enterprise". The three questions she leaves open, in her own words: "What then is the effect of Sherman's use of grotesque realism in *Untitled #250*? Does it risk reinforcing what Russo called the 'connotations of fear and loathing around the biological processes of reproduction and of aging'? Or does it expose a problematic representation from which women are unable to escape?" She does not answer them.

The difference from Bakhtin she does state: "Unlike Bakhtin's figurines, Sherman's figure is not laughing, but she is degraded in a very Bakhtinian sense."

And the conclusion of the section is a question about ontology rather than about reading: "One difficulty of encountering Sherman's images is that of thinking what category of embodied experience they belong to, just what kind of body do they evoke? On the one hand, the figures are literally 'made up,' their assemblages of fake body parts show that there is no prediscursive body, but only an unstable locus of contested meaning. At the same time, she deliberately engages with the material specificities of bodies that are aging and sexually abject. What queers nature in Sherman's work, I suggest, is that she pushes at this gap between performance and material embodiment in a way that is uncomfortable for the viewer to encounter."

Endnote 18 records that the last point came from a reader: "My thanks to one of the readers of the final draft for making this valuable point although there is not scope to develop it within this paper."

## 8. What she claims at the end, and what she declines to claim

The conclusion is titled with a question mark: "Promising Monsters?"

She summarises each artist in one clause, then makes the claim, and the disclaimer inside it is the important half. Such bodies are "promising monsters", and then, in her own words, "not because they offer better aesthetic models for the maternal embodiment", with the reason given in the same sentence: "that is not art's function". The positive reason: "but for the ways in which they explore relations between culturally constructed maternal bodies and embodied and imagined differences."

Braidotti again at the close: "The challenge that the hybrid, the anomalous, the monstrous others throw in our direction is a disassociation from the sensibility that we inherited from the nineteenth century, one which pathologized and criminalized difference" (2002, 213).

And the last sentence keeps the limit: these figurations "do not offer blueprints or solutions, but they may help us understand cultural anxieties that surround the maternal body and offer different kinds of figurations that acknowledge the agency and potential power of the pregnant subject."

## 9. The endnote that states the unresolved problem

Endnote 4, quoting Kathy Davis: "recent feminist theory has displayed a marked ambivalence towards the material body and a tendency to privilege the body as metaphor." Betterton's response to it is a deferral, not an argument: "I discuss the problematic of the maternal body as both a discursive category in language and representation and material site of somatic and psychic attachment in my forthcoming book." The book is *Maternal Embodiment in Visual Culture* (I. B. Tauris, 2007), which is not in this folder.

Endnote 2 lists what she takes the critique of the rational disembodied masculine subject to rest on: "Bordo 1993, Butler 1993, Grosz 1994, Gatens 1996, Davis 1997, and Shildrick and Price 1998." Endnote 5 lists the critique of the individuated subject in connection to pregnancy and birth: "Battersby 1998, Boulous Walker 1998, Young 1998, Tyler 2000, and Shildrick 2002." Endnote 1 adds Young's formulation of pregnancy as "a paradigm of bodily experience in which the transparent unity of self dissolves and the body attends positively to itself at the same time as it enacts its projects" (1998, 274). Endnote 7 points to Jackie Stacey's *Teratologies: A Cultural Study of Cancer* (1997) for the science of monsters "in relation both to the monstrous maternal and to tumorous cells".

## 10. Where this sits beside the other reads in this folder, without settling anything

**Shildrick.** The strongest tie in the November 2 folder. Shildrick is cited four times here: Price and Shildrick, *Vital Signs* (1998), quoted on the body that must appear "invulnerable, predictable and consistent in form and function"; Shildrick and Price 1998 in endnote 2; and Shildrick's *Embodying the Monster: Encounters with the Vulnerable Self* (2002) in endnote 5 and the reference list. Betterton's quarrel with Quinn's marble is argued from that Shildrick sentence. The 2015 *Hypatia* article read before this one, in the same journal nine years later, argues that the prosthesis is constitutive and that "none of us is entitled to claim a singular body". Betterton's prostheses are Sherman's strapped-on torso and the fake body parts, read as denaturalising rather than as constitutive. The two accounts of the prosthesis are not the same account and neither text addresses the other. Nothing here decides between them.

**Butler.** Cited once, in endnote 2, as one of six items in a list of body theory, with no engagement in the body text. Abjection here comes through Kristeva, not Butler, and Betterton uses Kristeva's formula "clean and proper" self (1982, 8) twice without going by way of Butler's reading of it. The chapter of *Bodies That Matter* on the constitutive outside and the chapter on Kristeva's abjection are therefore adjacent to this article's whole subject and are not brought into it. That is an absence to note and not a fault to fix.

**The metaphor question, and the Neimanis divergence already standing in this folder.** Betterton's article contains a case against a specific figure, built three times over: Hiller's panel exposing that pregnancy "cannot be 'enabled' by will or desire and, in this sense, are quite unlike the practices involved in making art"; Quinn's accepted analogy between artist's mind and mother's womb effacing Lapper's agency; and Kristeva's maternal as "a metaphorical site that precludes reference to women". All three turn on a metaphor of creativity-as-gestation being taken literally in one direction and not the other. Alongside that she quotes Davis in endnote 4 on feminist theory's "tendency to privilege the body as metaphor" and defers the problem to her book.

Three positions on figure are now in this folder and none of them is settled by any of the others. Neimanis: her water figures are "always more than metaphorical", working by "contiguity or continuity (rather than analogy)", and "concepts are always also material". McKnight and Whitburn: a figure's entailments arrive whether the writer intends them or not, and the test is to notice the wince. Betterton: a particular figure of creativity, applied to pregnancy, erases the agency of the person it is applied to, and the artists who matter are the ones who expose it rather than extend it. Recorded as three positions. Not adjudicated here.

**Fries and Fahey.** Closest in kind, because this is an article about artists' own refusals of illustration. Fries's standing formula across her artwork discussions is that a work is "manifested as an encounter rather than as an illustration or description"; Fahey names a failed body of work as "illustrating an idea, rather than embodying an exploration of the question". Betterton's version of the same distinction is her claim that art "can explore the tensions that pregnant bodies evoke", offering "new figurations rather than theoretical closure", and the disclaimer that better aesthetic models are "not art's function". Whether those are the same refusal is not something any of the three texts decides.

**The voice thread, and the next item in the read order.** Kuhn's sentence here, on finding "not the voice of the monstrous singular ego but one that, summoning the resources of the place we come from, can speak with eloquence of, and for, that place", sits directly against the next two documents in the November 2 order, Jackson's "Rhizovocality" (2003) and Mazzei's "A voice without organs" (2013), both of which are about what voice can be taken to be in research. No contact in citation: Jackson and Mazzei are not in Betterton's references, and Betterton is not a name that came up in the McKnight and Whitburn read.
