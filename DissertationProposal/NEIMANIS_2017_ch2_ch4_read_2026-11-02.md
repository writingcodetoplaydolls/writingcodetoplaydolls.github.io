## 1. Chapter 2 in one paragraph

Water on Earth is finite (all of it has always been here) and yet never the same twice. From that, Neimanis argues that water is "both finite and inexhaustible; both the same and always becoming different", and that **"what repeats is always difference."** She reads Irigaray (chiefly *Marine Lover of Friedrich Nietzsche*, 1991) as a **posthuman phenomenologist** rather than an essentialist, pairs her with Deleuze on difference and repetition, and names the result two ways: **amniotics** (the onto-logic) and **posthuman gestationality** (the capacity). The ethical payoff is **becoming-milieu**: the gestational capacity belongs to every body of water, not to the reproductive female body, so "we might also become milieu for multispecies ecologies in our guts, or milieu for gardens that will grow from our deathly and discarded matters" and "as ally, as neighbour, as passerby, as teacher."

## 2. Amniotics: a membrane logic, with three jobs at once

The term is taken from the anatomy, and she keeps the anatomy in it: "An amnion is the innermost membrane that encloses the embryo of a mammal, bird, or reptile (animals otherwise known as amniotes)."

> "Amniotics thus articulates three modes of watery embodiment, in a common logic: **first, bodies of water as facilitative and gestational; second, bodies of water as differentiating, as well as the material accomplishment of this differentiation; and third, bodies of water as necessarily interconnecting and interpermeating, all at the same time.**"

**The membrane is the load-bearing piece, and the sentence to keep is this one:**

> "This membrane is not a divisive barrier, but **an interval of passage: solid enough to differentiate, but permeable enough to facilitate exchange**. Importantly, although science may make a cut that identifies the amnion in a specific time and place, Irigaray has already described . . . how the separation between the amniotic and maternal body is always obscure. **The membrane is not divisive, but nor is it decisive.**"

She routes this straight into Barad: "Anticipating Barad's concept of agential separability, Irigaray's figuring of waters that make a difference also suggests that there is always a membrane that separates the gestational body from the body it proliferates", and earlier, "In Karen Barad's (2007: 176-177) terms, differentiation can be thought in terms of 'agential separability'; in matter's ongoing intra-actions, cuts between bodies are continually enacted."

**An onto-logic is defined, deliberately, as less than an ontology:**

> "An onto-logic is a common way of being that is expressed across a difference of beings. As opposed to the way in which 'ontology' might be traditionally understood, **an onto-logic does not propose to solve the question of 'Being'**, nor does it purport to reveal or describe all of being's facets or potential expressions. An onto-logic can rather gather or highlight something that helps us understand **a common how, where, when, and thanks to whom** that certain seemingly disparate beings share."

And she refuses the headline version of her own thesis: "**The point is not just that 'we are all 60-90 per cent water'; the brute materiality is not the real take-away here.**"

## 3. The reading of Irigaray, and how she handles the disagreement

This chapter is a long worked example of reading a theorist against her own stated position without either dismissing her or pretending the disagreement is not there.

**She states the problem rather than smoothing it.** Irigaray holds that "the most fundamental locus of irreducibility is between a man and a woman" (1996: 139) and that sexuate difference is "insurmountable" and "unsurpassable", which leaves "little consideration of bodies that do not align simply with either of the gendered sexual poles". Neimanis names that as a "critical limit" and asks: "**How might we think about the sexuate difference of our bodies of water beyond the dichotomous chasm that Irigaray's descriptions seem to suggest?**"

**She refuses to solve it by discarding either side.** On the one hand: "**I am unwilling to enact yet another effacement of the maternal, even if . . . I don't want to hold it up as the essentialized model of gestationality.**" On the other: "**I find it exciting to swim with Irigaray beyond the bounds of where she may have intended I stop.**" And on what she is not doing: "**My aim here is not to classify Irigaray 'once and for all', or to find the 'right' way to read her.**"

**The Deleuze move**: Deleuze's embryo gives her sexuate difference as *virtual* rather than as an accomplished dimorphism ("the egg is the virtual subject"; "as egg, sexual difference is at the threshold of the virtual"), so "**we do not . . . do away with sexual difference, but we should be wary of pinning down too precisely what it is, what potential it holds, what it means.**" And where Deleuze falls short she says so, through Lorraine: "Deleuze's images of a line of flight and the highly populated desert are oddly solipsistic", while Irigaray insists on "the participatory communion of mutually constitutive creativity"; "**Deleuze, after all, bases his theory on the image of a rather lonely and singular egg.**"

**She does not let matter off the hook, either:**

> "**Gestationality is not just a brute capacity of wet matter; it is oriented and thwarted by the flows of power, meaning, and values in which we all swim.**"

> "**Not all gifts are life-affirming.** As bodies of water, our capacity to proliferate the not-yet is also always bound up in our capacity to bear toxic messages. **Gestation is always non-innocent.**"

The chapter also keeps a short survey of how Irigaray's materiality has been read, as synecdoche or catachresis (Gallop 1988), as strategic essentialism (Whitford, Braidotti, Butler), as tactical intervention (Lorraine), as realist essentialism (Stone), as virtuality, and it registers her dissatisfaction with all of the rhetorical readings: "**In calling attention to women's material bodily difference as primarily rhetorical, strategic or tactical, it seems that these readings acknowledge the difference of feminine embodiment only reluctantly, for effect, without true commitment. Relegated to such a position, how can materiality be fully embraced as a teacher and a guide?**" **Catachresis appears here as one of the readings she declines, which is worth noting against `BUTLER_catachresis_and_the_real_read.md` and `METAPHOR_and_the_real_search2.md`.**

## 4. Metaphor and the real, in chapter 2

Three passages, in her own voice, on exactly the question that `METAPHOR_where_it_is_discussed.md` and `METAPHOR_and_the_real_search2.md` were built around. **None of them was in those files, because this chapter had not been read.**

**First, the distinction stated as a choice of word:**

> "In some instances, these connections of our human bodies with other natural bodies **do indeed seem metaphorical**, that is, descriptions of the way that the sexuate difference of bodies is **mirrored in (rather than contiguous with)** the nature of weather, plants, and animals (see Stone 2003, 2006). **Yet crucially, the language Irigaray deploys always remains connected to the materiality of the phenomena she describes.**"

> "Take breathing, for example. When Irigaray (2002a: 80) suggests that woman engenders with her breath and shares her breath even before she shares the nourishment of her body, **this is a true description of some women's bodily experience, even as Irigaray's prose opens to a broader metaphorical or metonymical significance. As always more than metaphorical, we can trace in Irigaray's work a contiguity or continuity (rather than analogy) with the elemental.**"

**Second, the same move made about a figure that would otherwise read as pure symbol:**

> "**the sea is not simply a metaphor for the female mother or the womb; it has also provided the womb's very condition of possibility, and continues to leak into our human wombs, in various extended traces.**"

She supports that with Steingraber, quoted at second hand through Alaimo, on her own amniocentesis: "I drink water and it becomes blood plasma . . . Before it is drinking water, amniotic fluid is the creeks and rivers that fill reservoirs . . . **Whatever is inside the hummingbird eggs is also inside my womb.**"

**Third, the pre-emption of the objection, twice, in the same formula:**

> "**While this might sound metaphorical, we are literally the condition for one another's possibility**, as demonstrated by our watery interpermeations."

> Of her own description of elemental passions: "can be read as **an extended material metaphor that is effective precisely because our physiological reality is intimately connected to a meteorological and geophysical one**."

**And chapter 4 completes the pair from the other direction**, making the concept material rather than the material figurative:

> "**Somewhat unlike Grosz (and her Deleuzian inspiration), I suggest that concepts are always also material.**"

> "**Water does not precede the concepts we use to engage it, any more than the words we use to fabricate material reality.**"

> "all of these notions, so often relegated to the side of culture, the immaterial, the representational, and so on, are **material-discursive phenomena, tethered to materialities they might purportedly only describe**. Throughout this book I have stressed that **concepts are embodied**, but this doesn't mean that they are simply given. **Words too (a theory, a concept, a poem) are multivalent intra-actions of matter and meaning.**"

> "**If water is an idea, it is a material, embodied idea.**"

**This is a position, not a settlement, and it sits alongside the others already logged rather than replacing them.** Her terms for the relation are **contiguity**, **continuity** and **more than metaphorical**, set against **analogy**, **mirroring** and **mere metaphor**; and her stated route is to keep the material claim literal ("literally the condition for one another's possibility") while letting the figurative sense run on top of it.

## 5. Chapter 4: three waters, and a fourth

The chapter's spine is Jamie Linton's *What Is Water* (2010), restated and then extended.

| | |
|---|---|
| **modern water** | Eighteenth-century chemistry (Lavoisier) turns "the culturally impregnated waters of places and times" (Illich) into H2O: "colourless transparent tasteless scentless", an "abstract, isomorphic, measurable quantity". Nineteenth-century hydrology then adds the cycle diagram, which does not re-enchant it but makes it manageable: "water now becomes available to central planners . . . as a quantum". Water becomes **placeless**: "One water is the same as any other" |
| **global water** | Post-1918 international coordination, intensified by the **International Hydrological Decade**; the driving question becomes "how much water is there in the world?" Linton calls the result "a completely unworkable concept", since a global paradigm "suffers a critical breakdown when brought to bear on specific water issues" |
| **Anthropocene water** | **Neimanis's own addition.** Same placelessness and abstraction, plus two new things: the making is **ours**, and the "we" doing it is **undifferentiated**. "A persistent but undifferentiated 'we' begins most of the video's sentences" |
| **bodies of water** | Her alternative, offered as one imaginary among others rather than as the truth: "**The question again is not whether this imaginary is 'right'; it just is.** More helpful is asking: what kind of alter-imaginaries might gestate other kinds of worlds?" |

**Her correction to Anthropocene discourse is that it is too stony.** "the lithosphere is the key slate onto which accounts of the Anthropocene are written", so she asks for attention to "**the hydrosphere as the (again, oft-overlooked) fascia that lubricates and connects the Earth's lithosphere to its biosphere and atmosphere**", and names the keyword: "**adopting an aqueous orientation to the Anthropocene reminds us that the keyword of this epoch is control.**"

**The "we" problem is given a name and a chain of alternatives.** Lesley Head's "**species error**": "we have conceptualized the Anthropocene with an undifferentiated human, again contrary to the abundant evidence of spatial and temporal differences in influences below the species level." Head's second objection is political: an undifferentiated "we" produces "**political paralysis**" and "paralytic fatalism". Then the renamings: **Capitalocene**, **Econocene**, **Plantationocene** (Haraway), **Chthulucene** (Haraway), and the **Orbis hypothesis** (Lewis and Maslin 2015), which puts the golden spike at European arrival in the Americas, with Audra Mitchell's gloss that "the wounds inflicted by colonization" remain "engrained in, and integral to, very lively Earth systems that persist today".

**And Colebrook's name for the affect of apocalypse**, which is a useful thing to have a name for: "**hyper-hypo-affective disorder**", where "there is neither panic nor any apparent affective comportment that would indicate that anyone really feels or fears" the threat. Neimanis's addition: "**In ostensibly affirming that we're all doomed, an Anthropocene imaginary actually seeks to ignite the belief that we're not** . . . The horror of it all paradoxically comforts us: it's not really going to happen."

## 6. The one empirical case, carried through

The chapter's worked example is **contaminants concentrating in Arctic breast milk**, built as a chain each link of which is a body:

> "human-machine assemblage, to waterway, to ocean and atmospheric current, to plankton, to whale, to breastfeeding body, only then to be downloaded to the infant body many times magnified"

The breastfeeding body "serves literally as what Foucault would have called a '**dense transfer point**' of power", and at one point "the greatest body burden known to occur from environmental exposure [was] found in Inuit mothers" (Dewailly, quoted at second hand through Trainor et al.). Her reading of it: "**'Body burden' is both a biochemical descriptor, but also a way of naming social, cultural, gendered, and species-related inheritances of response and responsibility**", and the Arctic case shows "**incursion 'at a distance'**", a colonial relation that does not require the colonizer's body to be present. Lorde is cited for the method: "there is no such thing as a single-issue struggle because we do not live single-issue lives", with the extension that "**the intersecting axes that she refers to must now also be thought across species and elemental lines, across timescales both vast and molecular.**"

## 7. Two artworks, and what happens to a slogan when it is repeated

**Rebecca Belmore** (Anishinaabe) carries the chapter, and the chapter opens with a prose description of the video rather than an analysis of it. ***Fountain*** (2005, single-channel video on a four by three metre screen of running water, Canadian pavilion, Venice Biennale): a woman drags a bucket out of the sea and throws it at the viewer, and what runs down the screen is blood. **Neimanis ends the description with a question to the reader instead of a reading: "Maybe, you look at your hands. Are they clean?"** ***Temple*** (1996, Power Plant gallery, Toronto): hundreds of litres of Toronto tap and lake water bagged in plastic milk bags, shingled over a wedge, with a working drinking fountain beneath and a telescope above; "many reported that they thought they were looking at a video, when what they were actually seeing was Lake Ontario, just on the other side of the Power Plant wall."

**On what she is doing with another people's cosmology, she states the limit herself** (and this is a usable model): "I don't claim to speak for this imaginary"; artworks are "sites of collaborative inquiry that can introduce me to Anishnaabe understandings of water **without any pretense of full knowledge or subsumption**"; "**I will have to leave the troubling question of whether First Nations and other indigenous people are closer to Nature or better custodians of the Earth to others more willing to engage with those terms of debate.**" She quotes Belmore that her relation to traditional Anishinabe culture "is marked as much by rupture and dislocation as it is by continuity", and Deborah McGregor that "water is a relation", noting the double sense: water connects, and water is kin.

**Then the chapter's hardest section: "water is life" repeated, and going wrong.** The slogan belongs to Idle No More. She tracks it elsewhere:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Nestlé "Pure Life"**, "Hungry for Life", home ionizers, office coolers, a Giorgio Armani perfume | the slogan as product |
| **One Water**, "ethically branded" | "When you drink One, the world drinks too", which lands back where it started: "**Water is water, one water is same as the next: isomorphic, exchangeable, displaced, abstracted. In a queer way, 'water is life' comes to support the very idea it hopes to challenge**" |
| **WATERisLIFE**, "The Girl Who Couldn't Cry" (2014, 1:20) | a girl in an unnamed Indian slum who never cries; the reveal is "Extreme Dehydration prevents the body from producing tears". Neimanis is explicit that she is not judging the filtration straws or the pump repair, and equally explicit about the film: "**Rather than Idle No More's call for nation-to-nation sovereignty, we have a repetition of racist discourses of white saviourism and of a gendered, brown body**" |
| **UN Rio+20 "The Future We Want: Drop by Drop"** (2012), 3,500 entries from 45 countries | the volume is the method: scrolled at speed, "**single frames of the message are transformed into a moving image**". Blue gives way to white and to transparency; brown and black bodies appear "mostly predictable[ly]" as parched; "water is life" morphs into "choose life", teardrops into wombs, and decades-old anti-abortion imagery returns. The winning entry: a hand holding a blue water gun to the head of a white baby, "Wasting water will kill the future / Change begins at home" |

Her frame for this is **reproductive futurism** (Edelman 2004, via Seymour 2013): "concern for the future qua the planet can only emerge, or emerges most effectively, from white, heterosexual, familial reproductivity", and in such images environmental problems are "**domesticated**" rather than linked to sexism, racism and classism. Her conclusion about repetition, which is the chapter's method applied to itself: "**Repetitions have no teleology, no set course** . . . Repetitions can go askew . . . **Not: will I repeat as a body of water, but: how.**"

**The chapter ends as a litany of unanswered questions**, laid out as verse, including "What is water? / here/not here/and mine/not mine/and / (it belongs to itself, Lee Maracle reminds us)", "What is water? / (KWE)", "What is water? / tiny ocean, and sweat, and pipe, and urine, and PET bottle, and stream, and", "What is water? / an alibi, a lover, a debt, a promise," and then a line that stops without its question mark. The last sentence of the argument proper: "**The answer will always be a question.**"
