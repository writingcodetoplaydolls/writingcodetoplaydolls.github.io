## 1. Diffraction as she actually operates it

Four commitments, each stated before the analyses and then visibly honoured in them.

**Selection is a cut, and the cuts are hers.** "I treated the selection of data for diffractive analysis as the execution of a series of **agential cuts**", so "that which had been excluded is still there, waiting to come to 'matter' (in more than one sense of the word)", and "**different 'cuts' produce different knowledges**". Her conclusion about analysis itself: "analysis is a continuous, iterative, entangled process, just like the establishment of material-discursive entities themselves: **never totally complete, and never done and established once and for all**".

**Not linear, on purpose, and she says what she gave up by it.** "I have therefore elected to not treat the project as a linear process (**even though it was given to the learners in a format where each activity was intended to flow from and build upon the previous one**) in order to maintain the openness that the rhizomatic form of a diffractive analysis creates". The rhizome gives **lines of flight**, "centrifugal, decentering, dispersing discourses and practices" that let one escape "the hegemonic actual and the manifested knowledge", and she takes three of them explicitly, marking each as a digression and once marking one she will not take ("a line of flight that I will have to leave unexplored due to constraints of space, but yet feel needs mentioning").

**The researcher is in it, bodily.** "the researcher actively participates in a 'process of knowing-in-being (onto-epistemology)', rather than doing an analysis in a position of separation"; **and she re-ran the project in order to be in it**: "I decided to repeat the project with the Grade 10 learners to afford myself the opportunity of doing the project with them, so that I could become '**folded**' into it with the learners".

**Data that "glow".** Her selection rule, taken from MacLure, is stated as a rule: data "seem to 'glow' as they 'resonate in the body as well as the brain'", so "**I selected (as agential cuts) the data that, in a way, also seem to have selected me**." Also kept, from Hofsess and Sonenberg, the honest constraint: while treating "data as messy, shifting and unsteady" one must "[acknowledge] the constraints of scholarship", which is why she splits the burning event into separate sections "with the understanding that they are all in actual fact inextricably interwoven".

## 2. Her own account of nearly having nothing

Section 4.2.1 is titled "The researcher: Thoughts and feelings" and is the most unusual part of the dissertation. **It should be read as a model of how to write the part of the research where it is not working.**

> "it is one thing to read, think and talk about all of these notions . . . **I discovered that it is a different thing altogether to try to harness them in a diffractive analysis** of the project that formed the basis of my study."

> "mindful of Gayatri Spivak's (2012) injunction that **truthfulness is a necessary quality for an educator** (which in my case is entangled with my role as researcher), I have to confess that **I felt insecure, uncertain, unmoored and destabilised, fearing that I might be writing a dissertation that went nowhere and came up with nothing**."

**Then she turns it, by naming the turn rather than hiding the first state:** Alice Fulton, quoted via Barad, "nothing will unfold for us unless we move towards what looks to us like nothing"; Springgay, Irwin and Kind on a life of inquiry as one of "**dis/comfort**" and "a continual process of not-knowing"; and the conclusion, "**I concluded that I should not view the situation that I was experiencing as the impasse and stumbling block that my first instincts made me inclined to believe it to be. Rather, it in fact already offered me a starting point for the employment of diffraction as a way of reading.**"

**And she lists her own identities as a set, interchangeably:** "struggling researcher, immersed teacher, insecure doubter, hesitant writer, cautiously optimistic thinker, jubilant discoverer (when some bright insight flames up for a moment or two), **and other indeterminate identities not yet thought of, and with all the adjectives being interchangeable with one another**", read through Barad's "identity is multiple within itself" and Hekman's "'I' . . . a mangle of multiple elements".

**Her stated purpose in writing**, after Bridges-Rhoads, is not interpretation: "I am writing as an '**experimenter**' who writes '**in order to change myself and in order not to think the same thing as before**'".

## 3. Analysis one: the burning in the garden

**The assignment**: create and enact an intervention in the art school's courtyard garden, alone or in pairs, after being shown **Ana Mendieta, Andy Goldsworthy and Christina Oiticica**, "all of whom work directly with the earth and natural materials and processes". Anna made "a small cage-like structure made of twigs, metal wire and glue"; Sarah made "a nest-like structure woven of green grass"; the two were to be joined and burned.

**She begins with the room, not the people, and says why.** The deliberate move: she "attempted to avoid focusing on the humans by resisting presenting them as 'pre-constituted subject[s] entitled to prioritising treatment'", and adds the parenthesis that makes it credible: "(**Although, being so thoroughly saturated with my humanist upbringing and education, I found it difficult**)."

**The comparison of the two spaces is the analysis's foundation, and it is concrete:**

> "The art classroom consists of a rectangular, walled-in cubicle that is a strictly confined, geometric space filled with mostly rectangular objects ranging from tables to cupboards and picture frames to windows, **in which neither the eye nor the body is invited or drawn to wander**."

> "the courtyard garden is open to the sky and edged with beds of plants and trees with organic shapes where hardly a straight line is to be perceived . . . one is constantly surprised by unexpected bits of matter that momentarily 'detach' themselves . . . **spider webs glistening in rays of sunlight that penetrate the shadows, cables of spider silk stretching from treetop to treetop, shed spider exoskeletons and snail shells hidden inside plants.**"

**And the pedagogical point is made from the furniture, which is what makes it usable:** the garden "is devoid of structures, such as chairs and teacher's desks, with the kind of '**thingpower**' that speaks of restrictions and hierarchies of power, as well as specifically allocated spatial demarcations for separating learners and teachers", so its spacematter "managed to trouble specifically the distinction between '**the teacher and the taught**'". Her own position followed from where she sat: "My colleague and I sat down on the grass with the two learners."

**On what the camera did, she reports a decision she did not make:**

> "Bits of discourse were becoming constituted by and entangled with the entanglements of objects, the shadows of hands falling over them, the hands as they worked, the moving human bodies, the flames, the grass, the garden . . . **Not once during the conversation did it even occur to me to focus the camera on the faces of the speakers.**"

**The senses are reported as penetration, with the theory attached:** "the pungent smoke and the heat invaded our bodies, through eyes, ears, skin and nose", which is Tuana's "**viscous porosity of flesh, [our] flesh and the flesh of the world**", porosity being "a hinge through which we are of and in the world", viscous "due to various types of membranes that affect and mediate intra-actions".

### Touch, and the line of flight into her own memory

The trigger is small: asked how she felt about burning her object, Anna said, "**It feels a little bit . . . like, in a way I'm reluctant to burn it, because it's so much work**". A second learner, Danae, had built a "tree-woman" and was to destroy it with an axe, and "**in the end she did not have the heart to complete the mission**". **The refusal was accepted and the work survives**: "Her decision to let Tree-lady live on was applauded by everyone. Tree-lady is still standing in the courtyard garden and will be fondly watched over as she slowly disintegrates."

From that, Kotzé takes the line of flight into touch, and tells her own story: researching migration for an artwork, she found old *Time* magazines on the Rwandan genocide, with two adjacent photographs of a mass grave, in one of which the body of a girl of about seven lay curled on cardboard and in the next was being lowered in. She cut the figure out.

> "The moment I touched (the image of the) girl and started cutting around the contours of her body, my emotions seemed to greatly intensify, and I was overwhelmed with such deep sorrow that it took me totally by surprise: **it was as if, as my hand covered the image of the body, the image became the body.** Time and space seemed to rearrange themselves, folding back on themselves . . . **My touching of and (agential) cutting out of the little figure made her materialise, made her come to matter**, in a way so intense and real that it affects me to this day."

**She then turns it into a hypothesis and names the test it would need, which is the careful part:**

> "I postulate that Anna and Danae's strong affective responses to the objects they had created were at least in part due the fact that the relationship between creator and object were to a large extent fostered by touch. **It would be interesting to determine, for instance, whether they would have expressed similar emotional involvement with a structure that they had created on a computer screen and had not touched at all. By this I do not mean that such images have no emotional impact, I simply suggest that touching seems to intensify such impacts.**"

Her mechanism for why touch differs from sight is physical: "the object could be touched almost everywhere simultaneously, for instance, by folding the arms around it and holding it against the body." She leaves the section open by quotation rather than conclusion, with Barad on touch ("All touching entails an infinite alterity, so that touching the Other is touching all Others, including the 'self', and touching the 'self' entails touching the strangers within . . . **Ethicality entails hospitality to the stranger [Other] threaded through oneself**"), and **marks the limit of her own competence to go further**: Barad "grounds these ideas in what she calls the 'queer' behaviour of matter in terms of quantum physics, **the explanation of which is beyond the scope of this dissertation**".

### What the learners said, including the part she first dismissed

**Anna, unprompted, mid-task**, transcribed with the hesitations kept:

> "it's almost like weird, because we're now involving a lot more how nature is, we're like almost making art with nature. I mean, we really are, like **we're taking nature, not painting nature, we're not trying to like copy nature, we're actually using it**, and on along the way we learn it, like, oh, now, wow, this is actually, you know, like alive, this is what we're actually doing to it . . . it's almost like this [the burning] is a statement, like this is nature, and we all bundle it together and we are burning it, we are destroying it and **what's left is just the metal** . . . nature is now being held together by manmade stuff."

> "If I could put this whole project in one tiny quote: **We used to rely on nature, now nature actually relies on us.**"

**And Kotzé records her own first reaction to that line, then corrects it:**

> "although I initially dismissed her conclusive quip . . . **as a rather naive statement not worthy of much attention**, when I reread it diffractively through the ethical dimensions . . . it actually struck me as being insightful in an almost intuitive way. As a matter of fact, these words echo very closely Guattari's admonition . . . that the health of the planet is '**increasingly reliant upon human intervention**'."

**Sarah's answer is the one that undercuts the teacher most, and it is kept in full:**

> "When we started this project I sighed and, wow, not again . . . I've done quite a few courses on this kind of thing, but this has turned out to be completely different and I've actually really enjoyed it, **whereas in the beginning I thought: This is going to be boring!** [laughter]"

> "I've done marine biology courses and that kind of thing . . . **It was pretty boring, because it wasn't art, and I like art!**"

> On lighting fires as a scout: "**it's quite different, because we don't normally do it like this, we would make fire to eat with [for cooking] and not to . . . not to experience what's happening.**"

**The analysis ends not with a conclusion but with the transcript of everyone watching**, which she introduces by declining to read it: "They speak for themselves, so to speak . . . **and that's where I will leave them.**"

> "Anna: It's lovely! Sarah: How beautiful! My colleague: Look how the colours change inside. Anna: I love how the smoke comes out of it . . . Me: Look at the glue . . . the glue is doing the burning. My colleague: Now it's coming undone . . . [There is a short silence, with the sound of the crackling fire]. Sarah: It's pretty cool that we have the green one and then the dry one. Me: . . . still that smell . . . Sarah: I love the smell of fire . . . My colleague: Skeleton-like . . . look how pretty it is inside . . . Sarah: It's like the nature part of it has been burned, but there's still something left . . . **Maybe there's a deeper meaning . . . you said [to Anna] that nature's coming to rely on us, but maybe we just think it is, where it's actually still there, thriving beneath, we can't see it.** Anna: Hmm."

**A second line of flight, into a newspaper photograph**, is the one place the analysis leaves the school: a burning bus in Attridgeville, west of Pretoria, photographed by Aki Anastasiou, which she found in *Die Burger* of 23 June 2016. Her reading, which she explicitly does not finish: the people who burned it did so because of "'die rook wat roep' (**the smoke that calls**)", and "I thought about how '**thingpower' was being appropriated and harnessed by those who felt themselves to be powerless**. Therefore, what others would regard as senseless destruction made perfect sense to these disaffected, even desperate, people. **I am not certain yet what other kinds of sense to make of these thoughts.**"

## 4. Analysis two: a painting, and then the bin

**The painting** (Anna, untitled, 2016, paint and photocopies on board) is read as a product of successive cuts rather than of imagination: "**The image manifested itself not as a preconceived entity painted from Anna's imagination: she could not have imagined it in its specific, material particularities.**" Its chain is traceable, and she traces it: Anna's self-adornment images, plus the spider orchid she had chosen to research, collaged into "a new floating, squid-like creature", with "bits of images of the fire, smoke and burnt matter of the intervention event" painted into it.

**The claim about collage is the most portable thing in the chapter:** "collages in which images and ideas are re/worked and re/configured, **are as such diffractive processes**, as 'diffraction is not a set pattern, but rather an iterative (re)configuring of patterns of differentiating-entangling'".

**She reports her own response as a datum, and names it as one:** "it affected me in an almost visceral way through **the thrilled fascination I felt at its existence**, and yes, its liveliness, and its strangeness and beauty". And she takes a method for looking at it from Tiainen and colleagues: **framing**, which is "not meant to 'explain and contextualise so much as to enable the liveliness of the object [to come to the fore] and to live on'", against an analysis of "meaning", which "tends to result in concerns with things 'through or beyond the art work'". Also kept: that the painting will keep moving, since "there is always imperceptible movement, such as paint shrinking, cracking and/or reacting with other chemicals (**and when this happens in the painting, the creature will 'move' too**)".

**She does not throw interpretation away, and says so plainly:** "**although my focus of analysis relates to tenets of new materialisms, interpretative analysis still remains an important factor when dealing with artistic production**", quoting Chaplin that embodied making and responding "should always be recognized [also] as symbolic practices and therefore as open to . . . interpretation and metaphoric understanding".

**The practical admission about grading is the sort of thing usually left out:** "it remains an inescapable fact that **learners have to be given marks for the work they produce, and a 'final' work is usually a curricular requirement**", which is why she analyses one at all. And the regret: "**I did not attempt a discussion, such as the one here, of the final works with the learners**", which "deprives them of an opportunity to apply the theoretical knowledge they have acquired".

### The line of flight into waste

**This is the analysis's genuine find, and it arrived from her own bin, not from a theory:**

> "It was specifically when I did my own collages for the project that I realised **how much of the images, images printed on paper, I chose not to use, and therefore how much material 'waste' I was producing**."

> "I became acutely aware of the paper, card board, and paper towel impregnated with printers' ink and turpentine that gradually filled a large trash can during a printing session my colleague conducted with the learners. **However much we might choose to ignore it, with an 'out of sight, out of mind' attitude, the waste produced in the processes of art-making is intrinsically connected to, or entangled with, us and our art through our intra-active agential cuts.**"

She traces where it goes (Aykanat on landfill assemblages releasing chemicals back into soil, water and air: "such as the turpentine, inks and glues in our case"), notes the artists who make work *from* waste (**Vik Muniz**, **Jeff Wall**, and **Chris Jordan's *Gyre*** of 2009, an 8 by 11 metre triptych after Hokusai's *Great Wave*, built from 2.4 million pieces of plastic, "which is according to Jordan the estimated number of pounds of plastic that enter the world's oceans every hour"), and then draws the distinction that is hers: "**although wonderful contemporary artworks such as these are being created with waste products, I propose that the waste produced by art itself should be considered and investigated.**"

**And a third line of flight extends it to her own equipment**: Martin Howse's *Earthboot*, a computer booted from the earth rather than a disk, via Parikka, with the e-waste recovery labour that sits behind it, and the sentence that closes the loop: "**As these very same (data-bearing) devices are integral to the writing of this dissertation**, it is important to emphasise once again that the researcher and his/her research devices are . . . always already intimately entangled."

## 5. Analysis three: the assignment that failed, and the two things she did with the failure

**The assignment**: self-adornment, to get each learner physically involved with the actual place where they live. **The result**: "most of the learners were extremely tentative in their explorations. **Some learners did not do the assignment at all, they had nothing about it in their source books**, which compromised their further engagement with the project, as following assignments were built upon this one."

**First she gives her own first explanation, then refuses to stop there:** "I initially ascribed the learners' lack of engagement . . . to factors such as lack of dedication and being loathe to put in anything beyond the barest minimum of effort, and in some instances they are probably applicable. **However, all the learners I show in Figure 4.13 are diligent workers**, yet they did nothing more in terms of the assignment beyond what is shown here."

**Then she assembles four reasons, each one specific to her own setting:**

1. **Education itself.** In most Western systems learners experience "modes of disembodied learning that are based on the disavowal or suppression of the body", because Cartesian dualism "had become central to our 'general self-understanding'".
2. **Religion, with evidence from her own gallery.** "learners, as well as adults, who visit the art centre express disapproval and sometimes even disgust at the slightest hint of human nudity in exhibited images, **to the point where the staff were even requested to cover up such images when certain groups visit the centre**."
3. **Body image.** "the shame of not having the 'perfect' body, entangled with the kind of shame generated by religious taboos, exacerbates the problem".
4. **Race and culture in that classroom.** At the Tygerberg Art Centre "we receive learners from all races, cultures and walks of life", and "**how learners from different groupings experience their embodied selves**, in terms of emotion and affect" must be investigated, in a class that is "a relational matrix" including "aspects of otherness".

**And then she turns the finding on herself, which is the second thing she does with it.** Having asked the learners to do it, she had done it too, and reports that she remembers almost nothing:

> "despite my eagerness to bodily experience the environment . . . **I paid scant attention to the actual sensory experiences I surely must have had. I cannot recall experiencing the tightening of my skin that the mud I smeared onto it must have caused**, or the smell of the soil . . . or the uneven pressures on my back as I lay on the soil . . . **in fact, I can recall no sensory experiences as such, except for the unpleasant smell of the garbage into which I immersed my body** at some point during the execution of the assignment."

**So she practises, and reports the practice as data.** A winter morning on her balcony with coffee: she is distracted by movement under a tree (a hadeda), her mind goes to her writing, and "**suddenly it struck me that the world around me had virtually 'disappeared', even visually, for I was gazing out to somewhere in the distance without seeing anything**". She then stops the thinking and inventories what is there: "the incessant early-morning chattering of the birds in the trees all around me (**loud as it had been, I hadn't been consciously hearing it up to that point**); the hard coldness of the metal balcony railing under my right hand; the smooth, warm surface of the porcelain coffee mug around which my left hand was folded; the pressure of the tiles on the balcony floor against the soles of my feet; and the rising steam and heat of the coffee touching the skin of my face", and finally "the very subtle movement of air against my skin, **despite there being no visible sign of even a breeze**".

Then a cat climbs onto a garage roof and she watches only the cat, "**until at last I also noticed its undulating shadow as it fell across the corrugation of the roof, and from there, suddenly, everything around and beyond the cat came into focus with it**", so that tree, leaves, sky, light and dark, shadow and roof "all now bore equal visual weight to the cat as I took everything in, **somehow managing to simultaneously see both the detail and the whole**".

**And that is where she defines her own method, from the experience rather than from the literature:**

> "**It struck [me] that what I had experienced in an almost physical sense was exactly what a diffractive reading is all about. It means to focus on the detail, but also to simultaneously be intensely aware of everything else that is encompassed by the whole.**"

> "this learning to focus on the totality of sensory experience, **even if the importance of it had been cognitively grasped and understood, requires continuous practice**. This is necessary in order to overcome a lifetime of conditioning."

**The one learner who did engage** is Danae, the one who would not cut down her tree-woman: "she, more than any other learner, seemed to feel completely comfortable in and with her embodied self", "never hesitating to get her hands (or the rest of her body) 'entangled' with the materials", and "**truly seemed less concerned with the end result than with the processes of exploration**". Kotzé's explanation is checked rather than assumed: "Part of the explanation for her bodily and embodied astuteness might lie in the fact that she is **also a drama student**", and in chapter 5, "**In an informal conversation I had with her recently, she confirmed my observations.**" Danae also made, "completely unprompted and of her own volition", a collage Kotzé reads as a visual counterpart to Barad's "the self is dispersed in an un/doing of self . . . **[b]oundaries don't hold; times, places, beings bleed through one another**", and credits her with having "**unwittingly displayed an uncanny, almost instinctive grasp**" of the theory.

**Her conclusion from the failure includes herself in it:** "**I certainly cannot expect them to become consciously involved with and experience the physicality of their environments, if they cannot engage with and explore their own embodied physicality and are insensitive to their own sensory experiences (and this goes for me as both teacher and researcher as well).**"

## 6. Chapter 5: what she concluded, including about her own design

**The chapter's epigraph** is Spivak: "What I cannot imagine stands guard over everything that I must/can do, think, live".

**She states what the dissertation turned out to be**, which is not what it set out to be: "on a meta-level . . . it could be said that **the writing of the dissertation was almost as much a documentation of my grappling with the execution of such an analysis** as it was an effort to find out what (or if any) insights . . . might possibly be gained".

**Her findings about her own project, as faults:**

| | |
|---|---|
| **too many parts** | "my analysis of the project led me to the conclusion that **it has too many components for sufficiently deep and meaningful engagement** (even for those who work diligently) when one considers the time that the learners have at their disposal, specifically as we have only a single two-hour practical session once a week". Each of the three activities "could potentially form the basis of a project" on its own |
| **not enough said out loud** | "I have therefore identified **the lack of (enough of) such 'deliberate', conscious and cognitive awareness-making as a weakness in the analysed project**" |
| **wrong starting point** | "for an introductory project, I postulate that activities that focus on the learners' own lived experiences might be more meaningful than something such as the self-adornment activity", because the learners are "entangled with other phenomena, beyond the art class, that include home life, school life, social life and other subjects and their demands" |
| **the final works not discussed with the learners** | see section 4 above |
| **waste never considered** | "future art projects should therefore include the exploration of ways to consciously 'deal with' the waste produced in the creation of art works" |

**Why she used neither interviews nor questionnaires, with the evidence:**

> "The attempts that I made at gauging the learners' thinking and feelings about their experiences **by means of questionnaires received very little response anyway, and the 'answers' of those who did respond somehow often came across as forced and/or artificial**."

> "I concluded that isolating [voice] and putting special focus on it might have led to less honesty and greater distortion of actual experiences **if learners felt that they had to fulfil particular expectations in their answers by saying, or writing, what they thought I might have wanted to hear or see**."

> "by the time one writes about something in retrospect, **the immediacy and directness, the freshness of it, is already lost**, which of course is also true of data analysis, a factor of which I am constantly mindful."

**Her positive proposals, concretely:** silences during a making session, followed by sharing; a few minutes a day of sensory practice, reflected on in the source books in writing and images that "could become part of their artworks"; mapping "the rhizomatic entanglements of each learner's everyday life" through "writing, drawing, photographic and sound recordings and collage-type techniques"; short performance pieces made from everyday movement and sensory experience "in spaces chosen by themselves, **with the actual materials that produced the sensory experiences brought into play where possible**"; "intra-art" transdisciplinarity, bringing in drama, movement and music; taking the learners into "unfamiliar and maybe even challenging spaces" more often; and a paired visit to **Kirstenbosch Botanical Gardens and the Kraaifontein Integrated waste management Facility** ("**the lower-case letters are correct**", she notes), so the juxtaposition does the work.

**On teaching across religion and culture her recommendation is research first, then negotiation:** the educator must "**verse him-/herself well in the specific views of the body held by different cultures and religions**, and the rituals and practices associated with these views"; "**learners should be afforded the courtesy of exploring the body as a site of learning in ways with which they will feel comfortable, and this needs to be negotiated with them**"; and the difference in the room is an asset, since "different learners with different backgrounds will come up [with] different ideas, and '**different bodies [will] produce knowledge differently**'".

**Two limits she sets on her own authority as teacher:**

> Van Boeckel: "most probably, as an adult, **one can never fully understand how a child perceives and experiences the world**", so one must be wary of "attempting to 'expropriate the child's way of being and moulding it into an adult's scheme of reference'".

> "**it is not my task to prescribe how and what learners should think and become**", and, with Springgay, "'[i]t is not a matter of consideration of which method is better suited to extricate "experience" but rather **the realisation of the impossibility of ever understanding the other's experience**'", which "demonstrates **the impossibility of designing non-didactic projects that will bring learners to particular, measurable insights about any preconceived ideas**".

**Her own answer to the obvious objection, stated as an objection:** "**It is possible that this study might be critiqued for not being 'objective' or 'scientific' (enough), but my defense would be that post-qualitative research and a diffractive analysis . . . do not necessarily aim to be so.** I concede, and have done so elsewhere in this study, that I could have made different choices, different cuts." And a warning the reader may feel: "there exists the possibility that **the reader might experience this awkwardness being reflected in the way the dissertation has been constructed and written**."

**What she says the dissertation was for, in the end:** "one of the most meaningful materialisations of this dissertation is **how the entangled, intra-active acts of reading, learning, thinking, listening, watching, partaking, feeling and writing have changed me**".

**Two further-research gaps she names rather than leaves implied**: the project's **second half, on globalisation**, which involved "a considerable amount of collaborative work between the learners" and which she could not fit in; and inter- or transdisciplinarity, which she "could not explore more deeply". Also a finding about the field in her own country: "**From the rather poor results of my search for posthumanist- and new materialist-based studies in a South African context, I concluded that up to now not many studies or research projects have been conducted from the perspectives of these theories**", and of those she found, most "still favour the human".

**Her closing empirical note is new material added after the earlier chapters were written**, and she marks it as such: "Subsequent to writing these chapters, it came to my attention that the findings of recently published research that was undertaken in five of Durban's estuaries show that **microplastics were found in 77% of the horse mackerel fish (which sits at the bottom of the food chain) that was sampled**, and that a handful of sand from a Durban beach contained a considerable amount of tiny plastic particles that were **indistinguishable from the grains of sand, except when held under ultra-violet light, which causes plastic to glow**."

## 7. What transfers

| | |
|---|---|
| **A selection rule you can state** | Data that "glow", that "resonate in the body as well as the brain"; "I selected the data that, in a way, **also seem to have selected me**" |
| **A definition of diffractive reading derived from doing it** | "**to focus on the detail, but also to simultaneously be intensely aware of everything else that is encompassed by the whole**", arrived at on a balcony, not from the literature |
| **A chapter on nearly having nothing** | "I felt insecure, uncertain, unmoored and destabilised, fearing that I might be writing a dissertation that went nowhere and came up with nothing", followed by the turn, with both states kept |
| **Her own humanism admitted mid-method** | "Although, being so thoroughly saturated with my humanist upbringing and education, I found it difficult" |
