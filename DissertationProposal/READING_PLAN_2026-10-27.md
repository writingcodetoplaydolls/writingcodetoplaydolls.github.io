> **FRAYLING (1993), "Research in Art and Design", *RCA Research Papers* 1(1), is the big one: the founding reference of the field, and this plan did not have it.** Rows in 2.2 and 1.5 below. It is the source of the triad, taken from **Herbert Read**: **research into** art and design (historical, aesthetic or perceptual, theoretical, "by far the most common"); **research through** art and design (materials research, development work, and **action research**, where "a research diary tells, in a step-by-step way, of a practical experiment in the studios", and "**both the diary and the report are there to communicate the results, which is what separates research from the gathering of reference materials**"); and **research for** art and design, "the thorny one", "**where the thinking is, so to speak, embodied in the artefact, where the goal is not primarily communicable knowledge in the sense of verbal communication, but in the sense of visual or iconic or imagistic communication**". **Three things in it that the standard citation of it leaves out.** It **opens on an etymology of the word *research* itself**, little-r "the act of searching, closely or carefully, for or after a specified thing or person", first used of **royal genealogy in 1577**, then in **Godwin's *Caleb Williams* (1794)**, then by **Charlotte Bronte in 1847** for the search for overnight accommodation: **an eleventh precedent for the 1.5 etymology row and the one closest to this project's own subject.** Its first case is **Picasso refusing the whole idea** in a 1923 interview, "**When I paint, my object is to show what I have found and not what I am looking for** . . . the spirit of research has poisoned those who have not fully understood all the positive and conclusive elements in modern art and has made them attempt to paint the invisible and, therefore, **the unpaintable**", with Frayling's gloss, "**He's a maker not a researcher**". And **Frayling's own reservations, which are the objection from the person who built the vocabulary**: "**why people want to call it research with a big 'R' at all. What's the motivation?** . . . **Research has become a status issue**, as much as a conceptual or even practical one. **And that, I must confess, worries me**", and the RCA "**do not at present offer research degrees entirely for work where the art is said to 'speak for itself'. Rightly or wrongly, we tend to feel the goal here is the art rather than the knowledge.**" Plus **Kenneth Agnew** on the documentation problem, that research through design has been "**hindered by the lack of any fundamental documentation of the design process which produced them. Too often, at best, the only evidence is the object itself, and even that evidence is surprisingly ephemeral.**"

> **NEIMANIS, *Bodies of Water*, is the big one, and three of this project's lanes are its chapters.** Introduction and chapter 1 read in full, chapter 3 at the evolution and memory sections. **Its introduction answers the metaphor question from inside the permeability material**: figurations are "**embodied concepts**", Haraway's "material-semiotic knots" and Braidotti's "living maps", and "**never conceptual fantasy or metaphor, these imaginative 'interventions' describe what we already are, but amplified**"; of the ecriture feminine tradition, "**the wateriness of bodies is always more than metaphorical, and watery matter is recognized as a literal wellspring for new ontological and ethical paradigms**". **Its chapter 1 is a method stated in three verbs, "Access, amplify, describe"**, from Mallin's body hermeneutics, which then hits its own hard problem ("**how do we grasp the virtual?**") and answers it with "**proxy stories**": "**these stories are not substitutes for embodied experience; they are its amplifiers and sensitizers. Art, for example, is an amplifier**", and they are defensible because "**these stories too are pulled from a material world, but then condensed, concentrated, and given back to us such that we can more readily access and amplify them, anew**". **Two artworks are then used in the first person as the method working**, a Felix Gonzalez-Torres bead curtain ("a baptism of string and glass . . . even though this 'water' isn't 'wet', it activates and amplifies in me the lived experience of water as undertow") and **Rebecca Belmore's *Fountain* (2005)** ("I feel the waters of my white settler body flow into the colonized waters of Turtle Island"). **For the permeability lane it supplies Hypersea**, the McMenamins' name for "**the interconnected system of terrestrial life that has extended the sea and taken it along for the ride**", with the adaptation literature attached (the porous surfaces of woodlice, the Namibian fog beetle, and "tough skin . . . absorptive intestines . . . big lungs . . . and tears", Zimmer 1998: 109), and the claim itself: "**as bodies of water we leak and seethe, our borders always vulnerable to rupture and renegotiation**", against "discrete individualism, anthropocentrism, and phallogocentrism", since "**discrete individualism is a rather dry, if convenient, myth**". **Three new Merleau-Ponty pages for the map, from two books not in it**, below in 1.5. **One caution, recorded in the citation-check file: Masaru Emoto's water-crystal photographs**, which Neimanis reports and hedges ("**Just a good story? > **BUTLER gives the metaphor question its technical name, and it is in a comps text.** *Bodies That Matter* holds **24 passages on catachresis**, a figure used where no proper term exists, so that it cannot be a lesser substitute for a literal original, because there is none: "**the use of a proper name to describe that which does not properly belong to it**". Plato "**proceeds to name what cannot be properly named, invoking a catachresis**"; Spivak's subaltern feminine is one "**whose exclusion from representation has become the condition of representation itself**". **Chapter 7, "Arguing with the Real", is the chapter on the figure and the real**, where Zizek's "rock" of the Lacanian real "**figures the unfigurable, and so emerges not only as a catachresis, but as one that is supposed to secure the borders between . . . symbolization . . . and the 'real'**", and "**as a figure it appears to slide from substance to dissolution**". **The position from which a figure could be called lesser than the real is itself held open by a figure.** Also **a ninth measurement instance for 2.5**, with naming as the instrument: "**the very iterability of the name produces the catachrestic divergence from the chain that the referent is meant to forestall**", so that the referent is "**always only tenuously fixed by this regulation of its use**". **This is the same book the drafts already cite at p. 30**, so the catachresis material is a gap rather than an error.

> **AND A LEONARDO TERM, 29 Oct.** `METAPHOR_and_the_real_search2.md` section 4: **"real metaphor" is a defined term in *Leonardo*'s own Terminology**, number 885, coined by **Keith Muscutt** in "Projected Kinetic Displays and Photomicrographs Based on the Use of Polarized Light", *Leonardo* 11(2), 1978, 97-102. **The criterion is causal, not visual**: a resemblance between a pattern under a microscope and a pattern in nature at much larger scale counts as a real metaphor "**when the two patterns have a factual connection (for example they were formed by the same or related physical or chemical forces)**", and Muscutt's own words are "**the connection between them is real, despite differences of scale**", against **imaginary metaphor**, which "point[s] to resemblances between patterns that share little or no factual connection". **An artist working a camera through a microscope, making a scale argument and a metaphor-status argument at once, in her own catalogued corpus.** Also in Leonardo, 14(3), 1981, 248-9: **Carl R. > **FITJE READ IN FULL, 29 Oct**, in `FITJE_MetaphorsOfModernPhysics_read.md`. Tor-Finn Malum Fitje, **"Transposing the Unseen: The Metaphors of Modern Physics"**, in Schwab (ed.), *Transpositions*, 2018, pp. 117-131, author's line **"Artist, Oslo"**, held in the full volume. **It is the most useful single text found in this folder for 1.1**, and it also lands in 1.5, 2.2, 2.5 and 3.5. **A correction first**: Schwab's editorial summary, which I quoted earlier the same day, reports only the chapter's positive account of metaphor, and **the chapter spends most of its length on the opposite case**. Fitje distinguishes an **active** character of metaphor ("a transpositional act that hints at the very creation of things, blurring the gap between them and even between them and their names") from a **stagnant** one, "a kind of reproduced regression", where "**the analogies have become equivalent to the thing, as if they were derived from it. But they are solely derived from the faculty of imagination . . . there is no thing to derive the model from. Curiously, the models come into existence because there is no base for modelling.**" His verdict: "**Metaphors merely pose problems when they are not recognised as such**", and he **grades them by aptness** ("in my opinion, the billiard ball is an inapt metaphor"; dark matter is apt because it "does not make any claims about the material composition, except that it is physically present and yet unseen"). **For 1.1**: the chapter opens "**No one has ever seen an atom**", because "optical technology merely enables us to enlarge an image, never to improve it", bounded by the **Abbe diffraction limit** (Born and Wolf 1997: 63), **so diffraction appears here as the resolving limit of an apparatus rather than as a figure**; electron and X-ray images are **shadow images** "constructed through computer simulations", and "**what the images represent are merely their traces transposed**"; electron cloud pictures "**are not pictures of electrons; they are pictures of probability**"; the electron defeats every picture, so "**they are undepictable by definition**"; **Bohr in 1925 withheld *anschauliche Bilder*, "intuitive images"** (Miller 1986: 173), and "the intuitive images remain; **only the admission regarding them as such seems to have vanished**"; **transposition is defined photographically first**, as reversing tonality "from negative to positive, from latent to manifest", then as "**a movement from information to figure**"; and the apparatus sentence for 1.5, "**each of these apparatuses possesses an individual limitation, and to claim that one in particular sets the standard of authenticity may turn out to be a deceitful prospect**", held in check by "**still, treating electron microscope images exactly like any other photography may lead to worse tragedies**". **For 2.2**, the programme: "**transpositions are always needed to make scientific data readable**", but "**where physicists tend to cover up these processes by minimising their impact on measures and results, artistic researchers may instead want to accentuate the erratic nature of the data**", engaging with data "**not necessarily to arrange or resolve anything, but to reveal and exhibit its natural obscurity**". He names **reproducibility as the standing objection to artistic research** and answers it with the *Nature* survey of 1,500 researchers: **over 70 per cent had failed to reproduce another scientist's experiment and over 50 per cent their own** (Baker 2016), "most confident in their published literature were the physicists", and at CERN's **COMPASS** "experimentalists will never be able to reproduce any one of the incidents occurring", so the norm "in time may turn out to be an outdated norm even for the natural sciences". He also notes that scientists and artists both **conceal failure**, "even destroying sketches and studies, releasing only the finished work", and **audits his own chapter against his own criticism in the last paragraph**. **For 2.6 and the art-and-physics layer**: Bohr to Heisenberg, "**When it comes to atoms, language can be used only as in poetry. The poet, too, is not nearly so concerned with describing facts as with creating images**" (Bronowski 2011: 256); and "**Pablo Picasso took the world to pieces while Niels Bohr puzzled it back together**", against the rainbow metaphor of analysis and synthesis. **For 2.5**, transposition is defined by distinction from metaphor: metaphor "relates to the whole process of relocation", while "**the transposition merely refers to the movement itself . . . says nothing about the meaning created by the metaphor, and nothing about the initial meaning before the metaphor entered the equation**", and it may be the better umbrella term because metaphor "is already stained by its long history of proposed definitions". **The one thing to get from it is Roger S. Jones, *Physics as Metaphor* (Minnesota, 1982)**, his main source, with its **four cardinal metaphors, space, time, matter and number**, and the five-word version of the whole question, "**Which is really a metaphor for which?**"; then **Arthur I. Miller, *Imagery in Scientific Thought: Creating 20th-Century Physics* (MIT, 1984)**, which is the 1.1 book this section has been looking for outside the folder.
