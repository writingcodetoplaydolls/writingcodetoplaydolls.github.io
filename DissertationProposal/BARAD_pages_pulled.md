## 2. Printed 18-20. Heisenberg against Bohr, and a warning about analogy

The distinction, in Barad's words at 19:

> "For Bohr, what is at issue is not that we cannot know both the position and momentum of a particle simultaneously (as Heisenberg initially argued), but rather that particles do not have determinate values of position and momentum simultaneously."

> "In essence, Bohr is making a point about the nature of reality, not merely our knowledge of it. What he is doing is calling into question an entire tradition in the history of Western metaphysics: the belief that the world is populated with individual things with their own independent sets of determinate properties."

**The sentence for 1.5**, at 19, which answers the charge that this is subjectivism:

> "Which properties become determinate is not governed by the desires or will of the experimenter but rather by the specificity of the experimental apparatus."

And at 20: "Thus there is still an important sense in which experiments can be said to be objective." Complementarity means "different quantities become determinate using different apparatuses, and it is not possible to have a situation in which all quantities will have definite values at once, some are always excluded." Her summary: "**it is complementarity that is at issue, not uncertainty**." She also records the historical point, that Heisenberg acquiesced to Bohr in a postscript to the uncertainty paper.

### The warning, at 18, and it is aimed at this literature

The book opens on Michael Frayn's play *Copenhagen*, and the critique Barad makes of it is the critique the physicists make of her readers:

> "As with many such attempts to discern the implications of quantum mechanics on the basis of mere analogies, the alleged implications that are drawn . . . ultimately do not depend in any deep way on understanding the lessons of quantum physics."

> "it seems that such methods (intentionally or otherwise) are only out to garner the authority of science for some theory or proposition that someone wanted to advance anyway and could have advanced without understanding anything at all about quantum physics."

**Barad condemns the analogical use of quantum physics on page 18 of her own book.** For the citation-discipline file that is the single most useful thing here: the author the field cites for quantum authority is on record against borrowing quantum authority. Her alternative is "a much more nuanced and careful reading of the physics", and her claim for why it is worth it: "quantum theory leads us out of the morass that takes absolutism and relativism to be the only two possibilities."

## 3. Printed 46-56. Representationalism, and the practice-as-research sentence

**The definition**, at 46: "representationalism is the belief in the ontological distinction between representations and that which they purport to represent; in particular, that which is represented is held to be independent of all practices of representing." The world as "composed of individuals, presumed to exist before the law, or the discovery of the law, awaiting or inviting representation."

At 47, the tripartite version (knowledge, known, knower) and its consequence: "representations are presumed to serve a mediating function between independently existing entities. This taken-for-granted ontological gap generates questions of the accuracy of representations."

At 48, **Hacking's genealogy**: the problem of representation traces to Democritus's atoms and the void. "The word 'real' first meant just unqualified likeness" (Hacking 1983: 142), and with atomism "'appearance' makes its first appearance." Barad's gloss: "**The problem of realism in philosophy is a product of the atomistic worldview.**"

At 48, **Rouse's point that the two sides share a premise**: scientific realists and social constructivists both hold that scientific knowledge mediates our access to the material world, and differ only on the referent. At 49 the Rouse quote in full (1996: 209), that we cannot assume "we can know what we mean, or what our verbal performances say, more readily than we can know the objects those sayings are about", which is "a Cartesian legacy".

### The sentence for chapter 3

At 49:

> "a performative understanding of scientific practices . . . takes account of the fact that knowing does not come from standing at a distance and representing but rather from **a direct material engagement with the world**."

With the guardrail in the same paragraph: "Not any arbitrary conception of doings or performances qualifies as performative. And humans are not the only ones engaged in performative enactments (which are not the same as theatrical performances)."

### And at 50-51, Hacking on learning to see

This is the part no secondary source pointed at, and it is the best thing in the chapter for a making practice. Hacking's criterion of the real is manipulability: "We shall count as real what we can use to intervene in the world to affect something else, or what the world can use to affect us" (1983: 146). "Don't just peer, interfere" (189). "Electrons are tools" (263). He holds realism toward entities and not toward theories, so "intervening (i.e., experimenting) rather than representing (i.e., theorizing) is the basis for realism", and "experimentation should be understood as a complex practice in its own right."

Then, via microscopy:

> "**You learn to see through a microscope by doing, not just looking**" (Hacking 1983: 189)

And Barad's gloss, which is a description of a maker with an instrument:

> "To 'see,' one must actively intervene . . . obtaining a reliable image free of all artifacts entails experimental know-how, intuition, ingenuity (all three of which are acquired through practice), a good deal of tinkering, the honing of tactile techniques in tune with the specificities of the instrumentation (including any of its idiosyncrasies), learning how to discriminate between unwanted noise and desired signal, between fact and artifact."

"And part of seeing is also being convinced about what one sees." Convergence across instruments built on different physical principles is what convinces: "a large number of interlocking low level generalizations that enable us to control and create phenomena in the microscope" (209).

**This is the same claim Tanya Latty makes in the Dalziell interview**, that you have to work with the thing. Here it is as a philosophy of science, from 1983, quoted by Barad at 51.

### Barad's own position, at 56

"Realness does not necessarily imply 'thingness': what's real may not be an essence, an entity, or an independently existing object with inherent attributes." Not Hacking's realism toward entities "but rather realism toward phenomena and the entangled material practices of knowing and becoming." And the italicised claim:

> "**experimenting and theorizing are dynamic practices that play a constitutive role in the production of objects and subjects and matter and meaning**"

> "theorizing and experimenting are not about intervening (from outside) but about **intra-acting from within**, and as part of, the phenomena produced"

## 4. Printed 146-149. The apparatus, with the citable definition

**At 146, the six points.** Barad's own numbered list of how her account goes beyond Bohr, and the thing 1.5 needs:

> 1. apparatuses are specific material-discursive practices (they are not merely laboratory setups that embody human concepts and take measurements)
> 2. apparatuses produce differences that matter, they are boundary-making practices that are formative of matter and meaning, productive of, and part of, the phenomena produced
> 3. apparatuses are material configurations / dynamic reconfigurings of the world
> 4. apparatuses are themselves phenomena
> 5. apparatuses have no intrinsic boundaries but are open-ended practices
> 6. apparatuses are not located in the world but are material configurations or reconfigurings of the world that re(con)figure spatiality and temporality as well as dynamics

**Also at 146**, Barad's own statement of her method, in one clause: "I **diffractively read** the insights of Bohr, Foucault, Butler, and other important theorists **through one another**."

And her definition of discourse: "Discourse is not a synonym for language . . . **Discourse is not what is said; it is that which constrains and enables what can be said.**"

At 147, what an apparatus does in Bohr: "particular physical arrangements that give meaning to certain concepts to the exclusion of others . . . they enact a local cut that produces 'objects' of particular knowledge practices within the particular phenomena produced." And the stronger claim, that Bohr's point that concepts are "actual physical arrangements" is "an insistence on the materiality of meaning making that goes beyond what is usually meant by the frequently heard contemporary refrain that writing and talking are material practices."

At 148: "apparatuses are the material conditions of possibility and impossibility of mattering; they enact **what matters and what is excluded from mattering**." Agential cuts are "at once ontic and semantic", and so "**apparatuses are boundary-making practices**".

At 149, two sentences worth having:

> "Knowing is a matter of **differential responsiveness** (as performatively articulated and accountable) to what matters."

> "Knowing is not about seeing from above or outside or even seeing from a prosthetically enhanced human body. Knowing is a matter of intra-acting."

And: "In some instances, 'nonhumans' (**even beings without brains**) emerge as partaking in the world's active engagement in practices of knowing." **That clause is the warrant behind Dalziell's whole slime-mould chapter**, which never cites it.

## 5. Printed 279, the real location, and the answer to the question about bodies

This page answers the question that started the quantum thread, and it does it with physics rather than analogy.

> "No empirical evidence exists to support the assertion that there are two different domains of physical laws: one described by quantum physics and another by classical mechanics. (On the contrary, empirical evidence supports the wider applicability of quantum physics.) **Quantum physics supersedes Newtonian physics; it does not merely supplement it.**"

**Three reasons quantum effects are not evident in everyday life**, in her own order:

1. The size of the effects goes as the ratio of Planck's constant to the object's mass. For macroscopic objects that ratio is tiny. "**It is not that we live our daily lives in a classical world, rather than a quantum one; the point is that we generally don't notice quantum effects because they are very small** (too small to notice without special equipment)."
2. The difficulty of shielding an object from its environment, "which continually fluctuates in an erratic fashion in such a way that a superposition is 'randomized' into a mixture 'for all practical purposes' (but not in principle). **This randomization process is called 'decoherence.'**"
3. "one has to know how to identify an entanglement (e.g., where to look for correlations and how to measure them), and generally speaking, this is far from evident."

Then: "however difficult it is to realize in practice, in principle we ought to be able to observe quantum behavior in macroscopic systems." And it has been: macroscopic quantum tunnelling and macroscopic quantum coherence, "the most well known example is probably Tony Leggett's 1984 experiments on superconducting quantum interference devices, or SQUIDs". **"So the question of macroscopic quantum states is not an idle matter. Nonetheless this is not what concerns Schrödinger."**

**Two things follow.** Barad's own definition of decoherence, at 279, is better than the one assembled from reviews in `QUANTUM_PERMEABILITY_lens.md`, and her qualifier "for all practical purposes (but not in principle)" is the precise form of the claim. And her third reason, not knowing where to look, is a methodological reason rather than a physical one, which is not in that file at all.

**And the honest note**: Kirby uses this page to answer the scale objection, and Barad does supply the answer, but she adds "Nonetheless this is not what concerns Schrödinger" and moves on. Kirby drops that clause.

Also at **270**, Barad's own definition, italicised, which anchors the whole superposition thread:

> "**Entanglements, like superpositions, are uniquely quantum mechanical, they specify a feature of particle behavior for which there is no classical physics equivalent. In essence, the notion of an entanglement is a generalization of a superposition to the case of more than one particle.**"

So in physics, entanglement is superposition extended past one particle. The six other readings of superposition in this folder can now be read against that, which is what the 1.5 row is for.

## 6. Printed 393. The permeability sentence

The question that started this, asked on 28 October, has a Barad page.

> "Matter itself is always already open to, or rather entangled with, the 'Other.' The intra-actively emergent 'parts' of phenomena are co-constituted. Not only subjects but also objects are permeated through and through with their entangled kin; **the other is not just in one's skin, but in one's bones, in one's belly, in one's heart, in one's nucleus, in one's past and future.** This is as true for electrons as it is for brittlestars as it is for the differentially constituted human."

And in the parenthesis after it: "memory and re-member-ing are not mind-based capacities but marked historialities ingrained in the body's becoming."

**That is the microchimerism material in one sentence**, written by a physicist in 2007, in a book held in `Comps\Used texts` the whole time. Note the word: *permeated*. Not *porous*, not *permeable*, but permeated, which is why no count of "permeab\*" in this book would have found it.

The rest of the page is the ethics: "We (but not only 'we humans') are always already responsible to the others with whom or which we are entangled, not through conscious intent but through the various ontological entanglements that materiality entails." "**agential separability is not individuation.**" And the sentence Neimanis quotes: "Ethics is therefore not about right response to a radically exterior/ized other, but about responsibility and accountability for the lively relationalities of becoming of which we are a part."

Plus the reworking of causality: "Intra-actions do not simply transmit a vector of influence among separate events. It is through specific intra-actions that a causal structure is enacted . . . 'emergence,' in an agential realist account, is dependent not merely on the nonlinearity of relations but on their intra-active nature."

## 7. Printed 409, which is an endnote

Endnote 9 to chapter 1:

> "Rather blasphemously, agential realism denies the suggestion that our access to the world is mediated, whether by consciousness, experience, language, or any other alleged medium . . . Rather like the special theory of relativity, agential realism calls into question the presumption that a medium, an 'ether', is even necessary."

Kirby's page is right. **The passage is in the back matter.** The same note also records that "a substantial number of feminist science studies scholars, including many of the most highly regarded scholars in the field, are scientists or at least have significant training in the sciences", which is a useful fact in its own right.
