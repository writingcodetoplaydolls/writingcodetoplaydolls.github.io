## 1. Appalachian born

**The single most surprising result of the sweep.** Across all 13.8 million words, "Appalachia" appears in almost nothing. In the curated top-level set it appears in exactly one document, and it is not a minor mention.

### Haraway's Camille Stories are set in Appalachia

The closing chapter of *Staying with the Trouble* (2016), "The Camille Stories: Children of Compost," is a speculative fabulation across five generations. It is sited in southern West Virginia:

> "Camille's people moved to southern West Virginia in the Appalachian Mountains on a site along the Kanawha River near Gauley Mountain, which had been devastated by mountaintop removal coal mining. The river and tributary creeks were toxic, the valleys filled with mine debris, the people used and abandoned by the coal companies."

The community in the stories is named **New Gauley**. The monarch butterfly, West Virginia's state insect, is the companion species the stories follow.

Three things follow, and the third is the one that matters most.

1. The most-cited speculative text in contemporary posthumanism grounds itself in a coalfield Appalachian landscape, and almost nobody notices, because it is in the last chapter and not in the abstract.
2. Haraway's note cites **Beth Stephens, "Goodbye Gauley Mountain"** for an account of growing up in and returning to West Virginia. Stephens is an artist; *Goodbye Gauley Mountain: An Ecosexual Love Story* is her and Annie Sprinkle's film. Haraway's Appalachia is routed through an Appalachian artist's practice, not through an ethnography of Appalachia. Stephens and Sprinkle appear twice more in the book as "ecosexual artists."
3. So the Appalachian node and the artist node are already joined inside the canon, by an artist who is from there. That is a different and stronger position than bringing Appalachia to posthumanism as new content.

The caution: Haraway is writing about Appalachia as a damaged place and allying with it, from Santa Cruz. The relationship between a fabulation sited in a coalfield and a scholar born in one is not symmetrical, and the asymmetry is available to be worked rather than smoothed.

Other Appalachia hits sit in the subfolders: a piece on kinship and archive ("Kinstitution: A Topia Between Archive and Proposal"), Jackson and Mazzei's "Plugging One Text Into Another," and several in the arts-based-research folder. None is substantive on the region.

So across 15.4 million searched words the position is unchanged: **Haraway's Camille Stories remain the only substantive Appalachian siting in the entire corpus.** That was already the most surprising result of the sweep; it is now the most robust one.

## 3. Mother

Densities are highest in **Shildrick, "Monstering the (M)Other"** (104 hits), and this is the chapter to use. Its argument is not about mothers as a topic but about the maternal as a position in a binary structure:

> "the monster, however alien it may appear to human consciousness, is always encompassed by the order of self and other"

and that the monstrous, like the feminine, "carries the weight not just of the other, but of *différance*." She places the disabled body and the black body in the same structural position as the maternal, and quotes Braidotti on the woman's body as "morphologically dubious," which is a direct line back into the Braidotti spine already in the bibliography.

Shildrick's (M)Other is doing the thing the node list needs: it is a single figure that is simultaneously maternal, monstrous and disabled, and it refuses to resolve into one of those.

Secondary: Haraway's "Promises of Monsters" (34 hits) is the companion piece, and the folder has it.

### [OCR UPDATE] A commissioned glossary entry on the maternal

The *Posthuman Glossary* carries **"Placenta Politics" by Anneke Smelik and Elisa Fiore** (around p. 415), which is a definitional entry on the maternal as assemblage rather than as topic:

> "Placenta politics is a term that I coined to indicate the materialist feminist biopolitics of the relation between the material maternal body, the placenta and the foetus. I transpose this maternal-placental-foetal connection into a nomadic frame so as to argue that it composes a generative assemblage."

It runs through Helene Rouch (1987) and Irigaray on the placenta as "a third party that redefines the relationship between the maternal body and the other body, the foetus," splitting "the subject from within, in a non-dialectical process of internal differentiations that predicates the primacy of the 'other within'," and then faults Esposito and Derrida's immunity theorists for never taking "the maternal body and the placental assemblage into consideration."

Two uses. It is a maternal figure built as an assemblage, which is what the node list needs and what Shildrick's (M)Other does not quite give (hers is structural-positional). And it is the one place in the glossary where **transposition is used as a verb doing actual work** ("I transpose this... into a nomadic frame"), which is a usable demonstration of the term the draft cannot otherwise source.

**The conclusion to draw is sharper than "superficially engaged."** Computing education's "embodiment" is a different literature that happens to use the same word: grounded cognition and embodied cognition out of psychology (Barsalou, Lakoff, Shapiro, Gibson), with a phenomenological wing (Dreyfus, Merleau-Ponty, Varela). It is embodiment as a theory of how individual minds use bodies to think. It is not embodiment as the dissolution of the bounded subject. The field has taken the word and not the ontology, and it did so in the one issue where the ontology was most available to it.

That is a citable claim with a denominator, and it is a cleaner framing than accusing the field of lagging.

## 6. Key texts for defining terms

Best definitional passage found in the corpus for each term, by searching for definitional phrasing adjacent to the term across all 13.8 million readable words.

| Term | Where it is actually defined in this corpus |
|---|---|
| **Diffraction** | **Burnard, Colucci-Gray & Cooke (2022)**, under a heading literally titled "Transdisciplinarity as Diffraction": "A term originally derived from physics, diffraction refers to the pattern of light and dark resulting from the passage of light waves through a slit." Then **Kaiser & Thiele (2014)** for the genealogy, Haraway 1992 to Barad. Use both: the first defines, the second historicises |
| **Agential realism** | Two clean glosses, both in ScholarDownloads. The better one: it "refers to the idea that it is neither adequate to think of our research being conducted on passive objects awaiting accurate representation, nor as if those objects are 'social constructions' whose boundaries are determined entirely by human" activity. That double refusal is the useful shape |
| **Assemblage** | **Nail (2017)** for *agencement* versus assemblage, **Price-Robertson & Duff (2016)** for why an assemblage is not decomposable: assemblages are "intensive multiplicities" in which each element "is transformed in its relations with other elements," and **Atkinson (2024)** for DeLanda's subject as "an assemblage of experiences" territorialised by habit and deterritorialised by the acquisition of new skills. That last one connects assemblage directly to enskillment, which is the Krzywoszynska thread |
| **New materialism** | A ScholarDownloads chapter gives the cleanest account: the term was "coined in the late 1990s, in separate writings, by Manuel DeLanda and Rosi Braidotti," with Dolphijn and van der Tuin's framing of it as rewriting dualist and universalist traditions |
| **Posthumanism vs postanthropocentrism** | **Braidotti's own distinction**, quoted in the bioart volume in the folder: posthumanism "denotes disciplinary problems within philosophy, history, cultural studies and ... classical Humanities," while postanthropocentrism "embraces transdisciplinary attentiveness." | **Transdisciplinarity** | **Borgdorff**, in the *Journal for Artistic Research* piece in BooleLeibniz: in artistic research transdisciplinarity "refers to ways in which artistic research may engage with other life domains, with the physical or social environment, with politics, with globalisation, with identity."
