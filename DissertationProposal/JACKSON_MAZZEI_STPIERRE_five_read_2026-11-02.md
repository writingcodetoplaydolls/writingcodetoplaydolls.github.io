# PART ONE: THE THREE OPEN QUESTIONS, ANSWERED

## 1. Mazzei's position on metaphor: it is in 2010, under its own heading

The earlier file recorded that "A voice without organs" (2013) uses neither "metaphor" nor "figuration" once in 5,171 words, and treated that as an absence. **It is not an absence. It is a position stated three years earlier and then not repeated.**

Mazzei and McCoy's introduction has a section headed, in full, "**Against metaphor, against easy understanding**". The claim:

"The challenge for these authors and for our readers is to experiment with the idea that thinking with Deleuze is not merely to 'use' select metaphors presented by Deleuze and Guattari (e.g., nomadism, rhizome, lines of flight, smooth and striated spaces) and to illustrate these metaphors with examples from data, but to think with Deleuzian concepts in a way that might produce previously unthought questions, practices, and knowledge. **Such a stance sets the stage for a critique of those who 'use' Deleuze by merely appropriating metaphors that were never intended as metaphors.**"

And the operational version: "what we mean by 'use' of Deleuze is that which resists a figurative use of the vocabularies, for example, to describe a connection of layers of data and responses as rhizomatic."

Their endnote 1 locates the critique in a tradition: "See Scheurich and McKenzie (2005) for a similar critique that they level against those who similarly 'use' Foucault."

**And the position has a second half, in Mazzei's own article in the same issue**, which is the part that keeps it from being a simple ban: "when Deleuze and Guattari write about the body without organs, they do so in an attempt to enact thinking without a subject and to confront our reliance on essential objects or material representations to understand and explain. **To take the metaphor seriously, in other words, to try to conceive of a literal body without organs, is to miss the point entirely.**"

So the position is two-sided and symmetrical: do not use the concepts as decorative metaphors to be illustrated from data, and do not take them literally either. Neither figurative nor literal. That is a more precise claim than any other in this folder's metaphor thread, and it belongs beside the other six.

**A third instance, worth having because it is about her own practice.** In the same article she quotes Deleuze and Guattari on the map and the tracing, then says what she will not do with it: "I do not wish to trace Deleuze's concepts and graph them onto my project, but rather to negotiate the map provided by Deleuzian concepts via an enactment of methodological practices." And the introduction gives the rule for handling the inevitable lapse, quoting them: "the tracing should always be put back on the map."

## 2. Where "a voice without organs" came from, in her own words

The 2024 article gives the sequence, and it runs four steps across six years.

**Step one, 2007.** "Following a week spent at an intensive summer **Deleuze Camp held at Cardiff University in 2007 organized by Ian Buchanan**, I immersed myself for the next year in a careful reading and study of texts and concepts. Haunted by Buchanan's questions of desiring machines as well as the teachers that I had worked with, thoughts and questions began to emerge that were not possible without the contours offered by these new (to me) concepts."

**Step two, 2010.** The phrase first appears in "Thinking data with Deleuze", three years before it gets its own article, and appears there as a question rather than a concept: "Is it possible then to think a voice without organs, a voice too easily taken as a 'thing', or a voice without a subject, in order to think the very limit of how voice is constituted, constrained, manipulated, and held constant in our efforts as researchers to craft a narrative, tell a story, give voice, and make meaning?" It recurs twice more in that article, each time as a possibility being tested.

**Step three, the reading that produced it.** "A careful reading of Deleuze's (1986, 1989) *Cinema I* and *Cinema II* **with my colleagues at Manchester Metropolitan University** produced questions as to how voice could be thought without a subject in this new image of thought (Mazzei, 2013, 2016)." The 2010 article's acknowledgements name that circle: Alecia Jackson, Hillevi Lenz Taguchi, Maggie MacLure, Kate McCoy and Phillip Prince, with earlier versions given at BERA Edinburgh 2008, a Feminist Methods conference in Stockholm 2009, and AERA San Diego 2009.

**Step four, 2013.** "A voice without organs: interviewing in posthumanist research", already read.

So the 2013 article is the fourth station of a line that starts at a summer school. And the 2010 article is where the concept is being built, which makes it the better place to see the reasoning.

## 3. The two 2013 articles are two readings of one dataset, through two theorists

They are adjacent in the same issue: Mazzei at *IJQSE* 26(6), 732-740, and Jackson at 741-748. And they work the same material.

Jackson's endnote 2: "This work comes from my and Lisa Mazzei's recent book, *Thinking with Theory in Qualitative Research*." Her participant is **Cassandra**, "a first-generation, late-career academic African-American woman" at a comprehensive university in the rural southern USA. Mazzei's participant, in the adjacent article, is **Fran**, from the same study of ten first-generation women faculty and administrators.

Jackson reads Cassandra through **Andrew Pickering's mangle**; Mazzei reads Fran through **Deleuze and Guattari's body without organs**. Pickering appears in Mazzei's article too, in a single line, for the claim that "material and human agencies are mutually and emergently productive [or constitutive] of one another".

**And the two articles cite Pickering in three different forms.** Jackson gives the 1993 article (*American Journal of Sociology* 99: 559-589), the 1995 book (*The Mangle of Practice: Time, Agency, and Science*), and a 2002 article on cybernetics. Mazzei gives the 1999 reprint in Biagioli's *Science Studies Reader* at pp. 372-393. Same argument, three locations. Check which one before citing.

# PART TWO: EACH ARTICLE

## 4. Mazzei and McCoy 2010, the special-issue introduction

**What the issue was for**, in Lather's terms: to ask "questions about the limits of our research practices and the kinds of knowledge production enabled and disabled by them", and to try "to grasp what is on the horizon in terms of new analytics and practices of inquiry". The editors' own statement of what they are not doing: "the authors in this special issue . . . are not arguing one epistemological position against another, nor are they attempting to '**get Deleuze right**', but are straining meanings and representations."

**The epigraph that governs the whole issue**, from Tomlinson and Habberjam's introduction to *Bergsonism*: "Deleuze's work is characterized not by a fidelity to any master, but by a series of transformations of concepts borrowed from a range of writers from many disciplines." Their closing use of it: "It is our hope that these works provoke not a fidelity to a mastery of Deleuze's work, but a fidelity to a 'series of transformations of concepts'."

**The sentence worth keeping on failure of understanding.** They quote Lather quoting Britzman's hope that educational research become "unintelligible to itself", and then Lather's own formula: a "mode of thought that refuses to secure itself with the consolations of foundationalism and nostalgia for presence, the lost object of correct knowledge, the security of understanding", positing "**not being understood as an ethical imperative**".

**The quotation they chose to open the analysis section, from a personal communication**, which is the most honest line in the issue: "The difficult theories of Deleuze are now becoming the easy part, whereas the handicraft and inventive creative process of doing analysis becomes what is difficult. **No wonder that Colebrook and others can write book after book when they are not dealing closely with data.**" (Lenz Taguchi, personal communication.)

**And their own admission about the issue they are editing**: "As much as the authors in this special issue would like to avoid tracing in favor of a Deleuzo-Guattarian 'mapping', such a complete departure is not possible. Traces remain, despite our cartographic intentions." With the consolation that "Lapses, then, into tracing are telling."

**Endnote 2 is a careful correction of a quoted author**, and is worth noting as practice: "The word 'maps' here presents a bit of a problem for thinking with Deleuze. Deleuze and Guattari (1980/1987) distinguish between the map and the tracing. . . . **Lather's meaning here more closely corresponds to what Deleuze and Guattari call tracing.**" They quote Lather's word, keep it, and mark in a note that it is the wrong one.

**What else was in that issue.** The introduction summarises all nine contributions, so the full contents are now known: Mazzei, "Thinking data with Deleuze"; Hultman and Lenz Taguchi, "Challenging anthropocentric analysis of visual data"; MacLure with Holmes, MacRae and Jones, "Animating classroom ethnography: overcoming video-fear"; Marg Sellers, "Re(con)ceiving young children's curricular performativity"; **Jackson, "Deleuze and the girl"**; Warren Sellers and Noel Gough, "Sharing outsider thinking"; McCoy, "Into the cracks: a geology of encounters with addiction as disease and moral failing"; and Eve Tuck, "Breaking up with Deleuze: desire and valuing the irreconcilable".

**Of those, the folder holds two**: Mazzei's and Jackson's "Deleuze and the girl", filed as `03-Deleuze_and_the_girl.pdf`, which is still unread. The other seven are not held. Two of the missing ones are worth naming for what the introduction says they do: Tuck "recounts falling in love with Deleuze and then moves through a series of break-up rituals inspired by her disenchantment with his refusal to say what she wants him to say about desire", and notes that "Deleuze is widely credited with an innovative and generative approach to thinking with interconnectedness, which has been an unrecognized and unacknowledged feature of indigenous knowledge systems"; and McCoy "explores the failure of 11 years of funded research".

## 5. Mazzei 2010, "Thinking data with Deleuze"

**The project**, stated three times in nearly the same words: "a consideration of the problem of voice in qualitative inquiry and the ways in which voice is constituted, constrained, fictionalized, or multiplied, all in attempts to offer an authentic essence or voice that is present, stable, and self-reflective." And the limit she puts on it: "My project is not to solve the problem of voice, but to question the promise, problems, inadequacies, and deficiencies of voice."

**The method is a borrowing from cinema**, and she explains why Deleuze's cinema books rather than his others: "it was only with cinema as a technological event of modern life that 'a mode of "seeing" that is not attached to the human eye' (Colebrook 2002, 29) was enabled", and "he allowed the creation of films and cinematic 'seeing' to transform philosophy."

**The four questions she sets herself**, printed as a numbered list, and they are the article's structure: what becomes naturalised and denaturalised in the move from silent to talking film; what it means to "see" a speech-act; what follows if talking cinema is more than filmed dialogue; and how a disequilibrium of voice occurs in film.

**The finding she builds on, from Deleuze**: "The silent film was not silent, but only '**noiseless**'." Her application: "we might consider how our participants give voice, not in ways that are deemed absent as silent, but in ways that are meaningful as noiseless." And: "If we depend on the 'filmed' dialogue in the form of tapes and transcripts, then we miss the noiseless properties of voice."

**The reversal the talkie performs**, quoted from Deleuze and then applied: the talking picture "not only naturalizes speech or voice, but it denaturalizes the visual image", so that "we now see based on what we hear, rather than hearing based on what we see."

**Out-of-field voice**, her term from Deleuze, with his example and hers. His: "the sound of boots when marching soldiers are not seen in the frame." Hers, from her own fieldwork: "music with sexist or racist lyrics being played by a teacher on an iPod." The aim: "not to improve the hearing, nor to probe for deep-seated meanings, but to rethink what it means to hear and listen to voice."

**She re-reads her own earlier work and says what was wrong with it.** The data is Anne, "a new teacher (less than five years of experience) in a primary school in a large urban district . . . white, a single mother", whose long quotation about her family, her relationship and the multicultural education class is printed in full. Mazzei's account of her own first reading: "I treated these teachers as speaking subjects and while I did deconstruct voice as a way of considering the silences that communicated a great deal, I still continued to try to make meaning based on each of the teachers as a singular subject or a character in the film who could be studied, dissected, and understood. **I framed voice, attempting to create an image (or story) based on what was heard rather than what was presented.**" And: "I focused too much at the time on what was being voiced directly, rather than a cognizance of indirect speech-acts silently voiced."

**What she asks of the same transcript on the second pass**, which is the article's practical demonstration: "Why does Anne fail to name her boyfriend and to mask her relationship with a Hispanic Other who in some circles can pass as white? . . . We may also miss the other speech-acts in the exchange – the body language of the others present, the changing register of her voice, her failure to answer questions or to deflect the question and answer one of her own choosing."

**The sentence about researchers that cuts both ways**: "As researchers, we desire to maintain equilibrium, control, and a clear sense of self as articulated through easily discernable and transparent speech-acts – evidence to support our claims. **Such desire is also shared by our research participants, and so we are complicit in the production of a narrative that tells a neat story.**"

**And she admits the trap she is in.** "I admit the trap that I am setting for myself, speaking of data, voice, research, etc. – all terms which risk the very thing Deleuze resists – a repetition based on prior thought. But perhaps, if I continue to strain against such repetition, I might succeed in the act of '**creative stuttering**'." Her ending is equally unresolved: "While I am exhausted by all this talking and thinking with theory, I have not yet pushed to exhaustion the potential in thinking with cinema."

Her endnote 1 points directly at St. Pierre's afterword, the fifth text in this batch, and summarises it: St. Pierre "discusses the fact that while poststructural qualitative researchers have deconstructed data, validity, authenticity, the interview, and voice, what is needed in thinking post-methodology might be an abandonment of the old terms all together, including qualitative inquiry."

## 6. Jackson 2013, "Posthumanist data analysis of mangling practices"

**The target is coding**, and the argument is genealogical. Humanism's essentialism "imposes itself on qualitative methodology by assuming that people (authentic, stable subjects of research) who speak (from a conscious center) give us (the researchers, also authentic) rational, coherent truths that serve as foundation (data) for data analysis and interpretation." So: "the practice of coding data that essentializes people and their experiences – and that leads to representations of the real and true knowledge – is an epistemological project flavored with humanism."

**Pickering's mangle**, quoted in his own words: "Traditional sociology of science, like traditional sociology more generally, is humanist in that it identifies human scientists as the central seat of agency. Conversely, traditional sociology of science refuses to ascribe agency to the material world . . . I think that the most direct route toward a posthumanist analysis of practice is to acknowledge a role for nonhuman – or material, as I will say – agency in science." And the definition she works with: "human and material agencies [are] reciprocally engaged in the play of resistance and accommodation. **This reciprocal play of resistance and accommodation is the mangle.**"

**The figure she uses to make it concrete**, from Hekman: "Sometimes experiments go according to the scientists' expectations and sometimes they do not. When they don't, when matter resists, the scientists must deal with this resistance and adjust their concepts accordingly. In other words, **nature 'punches back,'** and scientists must deal with these punches."

**The case: Cassandra's office.** Recruited to Regional State University in 1992 "to teach and enhance diversity", after fifteen years at a historically black university, into an institution "that, as of 1992, had never promoted to full professor an African-American woman." She kept her open-door practice: "My office is small now, but it was even smaller then and they [the black students] would be sitting all on the floor and everything and we just hung out together. I was a mother figure . . . Now there was never a time when I didn't give the same amount of attention to any white student who wanted it." White students wrote "long, very critical letters" accusing her of reverse discrimination; the accusations reached the provost; she wrote "letter after letter of rebuttal"; the case resolved in her favour.

**Her reading, and the reason the office is the analytic object rather than Cassandra**: "The 'block' in this case was the function of her office – the work of the office was not the same for white students as it was for black students." And: "White students seeing black students 'hanging out' constructed a different ontology of the office." Which gives the article's best sentence: "Cassandra's office is also transformed in what it does: it is a material place that holds black bodies together in both real and symbolic ways, and it becomes a material place that incites Cassandra to advocate for herself. **The desk chair that she inhabits as she 'mothers' black students with confidence is the same desk chair she inhabits as she composes letters in her own defense.**"

**The formula for what posthumanist analysis is for**: "The point in analyzing mangled practices is not what they are but what they do." And on agency: "In a post-humanist analysis of practices, agency does not lie in human-centered intentionality but in mangled responses to non-human forces."

**Hekman supplies the ending**, twice: "the mangle is both a noun and a verb. It is the entity in which the interactions take place, but it is also the action that occurs"; and "it allows us to stop expecting to separate the elements of the mangle and find the 'right' answer. **The right answer is that we are in the mangle.**"

**Her endnote 1 is a plain self-limitation**, and it names exactly what is missing: "it is indeed possible (and desirable) to complicate a mangled analysis to include more critical discussions of race, class, gender, embodiment, power, and so forth. Because I want to focus on particular aspects of Pickering's mangle (i.e. agency) . . . I refrain from pulling in other analytic threads. **This analysis of mangled practices, therefore, is admittedly selective and narrow**, but nonetheless detailed."

That matters, because Cassandra's case is about a Black woman's office in a predominantly white university, and the author says in a note that she is setting race aside to isolate agency. Recorded as her own stated choice, not as a criticism.

## 7. Jackson and Mazzei 2024, "Thinking like a feminist and reading with love"

**The question**, taken from a 1981 Foucault interview with Didier Eribon: "what is thinking doing?" Foucault, quoted: "There is always a little thought occurring even in the most stupid institutions; there is always thought even in silent habits." And on critique: "A critique does not consist in saying that things aren't good the way they are. It consists in seeing on what type of assumptions, of familiar notions, of established, unexamined ways of thinking the accepted practices are based . . . **To do criticism is to make harder those acts which are now too easy.**" With the consequence: "As soon as people begin to have trouble thinking things the way they have been thought, transformation becomes at the same time very urgent, very difficult, and entirely possible."

**Deleuze's "reading with love" passage, quoted at length**, which is the article's title and method: "You see the book as a little non-signifying machine, and the only question is 'Does it work, and how does it work?' How does it work for you? If it doesn't work, if nothing comes through, you try another book. This way of reading's intensive: something comes through or it doesn't. **There's nothing to explain, nothing to understand, nothing to interpret. It's like plugging in to an electric circuit** . . . as tearing the book into pieces, getting it to interact with other things, absolutely anything . . . is reading with love."

**Grosz supplies the feminist half**: "Ontology enables us to address how thinking thinks itself; how thought connects with and makes relations between other forms of thinking . . . Thought acts, it does things, it enables things to happen." And thinking like a feminist is "the freedom to create strategic concepts that function not as solutions to problems—but as new ways of living with or addressing problems."

**The teaching lineage, stated plainly and worth recording**, because it explains why St. Pierre's afterword closes the book these two edited: "**Lisa's teacher was Patti Lather, who also taught Bettie St. Pierre, who was Alecia's teacher.**" They met by chance at AERA in 2006, each having read the other's publications. And on their training: "Though not formally taught how to 'think with theory,' we had been enacting that concept by thinking like feminists and reading philosophy (Butler, Derrida, Foucault, Deleuze, and Spivak)."

**The three concepts they claim**, named in the same order twice: "thinking with theory, thinking without method, and minor inquiry."

**The terminological change between the two editions of their book, and this is the most consequential fact in the article.** "while we had troubled 'data' and 'voice' in the first edition, new attunements emerged to reveal that such deconstruction still reflected a stance that relied on a static capture of events and experience. As we read with students, 'taught' the process of plugging texts into one another, and **re-read Butler's theory of performativity (1990, 1993), our use of the signifier 'data' began to fade. Thought moved in the direction of difference: 'data' (as generated texts) transformed into what we named performative accounts**—performative in that the accounts are not merely a narration of experience but a bringing forth of life."

So the second edition of *Thinking with Theory* (Routledge, 2023) does not use "data" in the way the first (2012) did, and the change came from re-reading *Gender Trouble* and *Bodies That Matter*. Anyone citing the book needs to know which edition, and which vocabulary, they are citing.

**The Manning episode**, which connects to a book already read in this folder: "One way this happened was in between the first and second editions of *Thinking with Theory* when we were both reading Erin Manning's work. We considered adding an additional analytic chapter using her concept of affect, and we together read her book ***Always More Than One***—without a plan or method, but with the anticipation that the involuntary would erupt. The unexpected occurred: . . . Rather than an additional analytic chapter for our book, our reading together gave rise to the unthought, and **Manning showed up in the final chapter of the book** to help us theorize the relations between thought, preindividual affect, and authoring."

**Jackson's own account of writing "Deleuze and the girl"**, which confirms two things at once. On the commission: "I did not set out to write a paper about a cheerleading backflip. Rather, I was invited by Lisa Mazzei and Kate McCoy to contribute to a special issue . . . themed 'Thinking with Deleuze in Qualitative Research.' I did not have a paper topic in mind." On the method: "I had always been intrigued by the concept of becoming, so I opened the book, and started in the middle with 'Plateau 10.' As I read, I noticed that I was highlighting and underlining phrases like, 'all becomings are already molecular' . . . and I kept circling concepts like contagion, imperceptible, and unleashing. I read the plateau repeatedly, and in each reading, **I sensed a flicker of the cheerleading backflip that I had encountered many years before—that I had never captured as 'data' or even wrote about in my dissertation**." And the working rhythm: "Like a zig zag, I would read, then write. Go back a few pages to re-read, then write."

The second paper came the same way, from the next plateau: "On the first page of Plateau 11, when I read the line, 'One launches forth, hazards an improvisation' (p. 311)—**the backflip came through**."

**So the backflip is the cheerleader study.** It appears in Jackson 2010 ("Deleuze and the girl"), again in Jackson 2016 ("An ontology of a backflip"), and underlies Jackson 2017 ("Thinking without method"). That is the trilogy she names, and it is the same material Kotzé's method chapter refers to.

**Mazzei's parallel account**, which gives the same arc for her own concepts. On her first move: "Reading with love and following the contour of Derrida's deconstruction activated thought that enabled a theorizing of silence not as absence, but as presence." Then the shift: "I was troubled by a continuing persistence of silence in the accounts offered by the white teachers. As I recounted, '**The problem vexing me had changed**'." And the Deleuzian reframe: "Instead I began to ask a series of questions as prompted by Deleuze to consider, 'how it [silence] works,' 'who it works for,' and what it does."

**And she says the concept was not chosen**: "What gets left out of this telling is that I didn't deliberately seek Deleuze and Guattari's concept of desire to rethink voice; **their concepts grabbed me by the throat**."

**One sentence on collaboration that is worth keeping on its own.** Mazzei: "Even when I (Lisa) write apart from Alecia, I question whether there can ever again be an 'apart' when I read, think, and write. My thinking like a feminist is entangled with her thoughts, our thoughts."

Their closing line is three words: "Reading makes things work."

## 8. St. Pierre 2009, the afterword

**This is the most radical of the five, and it says so in its second paragraph.** "To be fair, I will out myself here at the beginning of this chapter and confess that **I find the unexamined celebration of voice in qualitative research increasingly tiresome. I believe we have burdened the voices of our participants with too much evidentiary weight.** I suggest we put voice in its place as one data source among many from which we produce evidence to warrant our claims and focus for a time on other data we use to think about our projects that we've been ignoring for decades."

**Her four troubles**, each named and each with its own source: with the subject (after Deleuze and Guattari, Butler, Derrida, Foucault, she "no longer knew who either the researcher(s) or the participants in my studies were"); with language ("I no longer believed, first, that meaning was a 'portable property' . . . or, second, that language could transport meaning, unmediated, from one unified subject to another, say, in an interview"); with space and time ("The voice in its presentness, if there is such a thing, vanishes immediately, and our poor attempts to capture it on tape or in fieldnotes always fail. So where/when is the interview?"); and underneath all three, with presence.

**The question she puts most simply**, and it is unanswerable in the terms it is asked: "We know that people say all sorts of things, so why is what they say treated as more authentic than, for example, something they write or draw?"

**The cartoon on her office door**, which is the whole argument in one image: "an old New Yorker cartoon taped to my office door in which a woman stands in the middle of her garden and says, '**The garden is my resume.**' Indeed. She doesn't have to say anything. So why do we privilege voice as the carrier of the truest meaning?"

**Her account of what qualitative inquiry claims for itself**, written sympathetically before she dismantles it: "Presence, 'being there,' is what makes qualitative inquiry unique because we believe that our face-to-face interactions with people make our work especially valid. We are not armchair researchers . . . Qualitative inquiry is not distant; it's live and in person; it happens right now. And, of course, extended time in the field – being there and being there longer – makes our work even more valid."

**The division of labour she records**, which is a useful map of who deconstructed what: "Lather deconstructed validity (1993), the presumed clarity of language (1996a), in addition to empathy, voice, and authenticity (see this volume). **Jackson (2003) deconstructed voice.** Scheurich deconstructed validity (1993) and the interview (1995). Pillow (2003) deconstructed reflexivity. I deconstructed data (St. Pierre, 1997a) and the field (1997b)." Jackson 2003 is "Rhizovocality", already read.

**On narrative, she sets poststructuralism and the randomised-trial advocates side by side as both critics, and marks the difference.** She prints Shavelson et al's questions against narrative and answers each in square brackets: "To what extent can rival narrative accounts of the same action be ruled out? [Why should rival accounts be ruled out – to silence disagreement?] . . . To what extent does the narrative generalize to other times and places? [Why is generalizability privileged?] . . . [But isn't science itself simply another narrative?]"

Then her own critique, which is different in kind: "poststructuralists are suspicious of what must be excluded to create the equilibrium, totality, unity, and pleasing closure that mark narrative."

**The charge against the field, stated as a mechanism rather than as a complaint.** "Most often, however, it seems that qualitative researchers 'find' stories in their data and call that work analysis. I have read too many research reports prefaced by a solid review of the theoretical literature, which presumably will be used in data analysis, only to find that theory abandoned and the 'analysis' section an unreflexive description of participants followed by a collection of stories." With a structural explanation: "I suspect that the organization of the typical qualitative research report (borrowed from the natural sciences) may encourage the tendency to suspend theory because theory and data are separated into different sections of the paper. **Theory, once reviewed, is simply forgotten by the time the author presents data, voices.**"

**On the gap between what is written theoretically and what is done**: "Even though we write theoretically about fractured, shifting subjects, participants in our reports retain the characteristics of humanist subjects – we organize them under proper names, 'pseudonyms,' and we write rich, thick descriptions of their appearances, personalities, and experiences embedded in stories. We continue to serve them up as whole as possible for our readers."

**The practical demonstration, which is the most immediately usable thing in the chapter.** Writing up a study of how expert readers read texts too hard to read, she found she had put interview material **inside the literature review**: "I had refused the primacy of voice – of interview data – by including data from participant interviews in the literature review, the review of theory. I did not write a separate literature review section in which I quoted only published (written) scholars followed at some point by an interpretation or data section." She prints a specimen paragraph where participants' words, in quotes and italics, sit among Eagleton and Sommer. Her reason: "Participants' comments were no more present, no more foundational, no more authentic, no truer than the comments of Eagleton or Sommer." And her note on how it happened: "Writing up my study in this way was not deliberate but rather an unintended effect of many years of attempting to refuse presence. **Deconstruction just happens.**"

**Her categories of data that are not interview transcripts**, named and dated: "**dream data, sensual data, emotional data, response data** (St. Pierre, 1997a), and **memory data** (St. Pierre, 1995). One must, following Rorty, give up the conventional description of data to think these (always already) data. Some escape the human/non-human binary. Some escape humanism's measured, chronological time."

**And the claim that follows from it**, which is the broadest position on data in any of the five: "**Foucault's words are data just as much as the 'voices of participants'** – and should be treated as such."

**Her three-point charge against the field's rigour**, printed as a list: "(1) its obsession with the voices of participants as the primary, most authentic data (evidence), which results in (2) the eclipse and disappearance of other data (evidence) that is surely unacknowledged and unaccounted for in a study and, thus (3) **weak analysis and the recycling of old ideas**."

**The passage she says changed her**, from Rorty, quoted at length and introduced by a sentence that is unusually personal: "Until I read those words, I thought I was essentially real. It had never occurred to me that I was a very real effect of a description . . . I found this idea terrifically freeing, though, of course, I soon understood that escaping the 'I' is impossible."

**Her warning about renaming**, which bears on the whole post-qualitative enterprise and on Mazzei's own "new vocabulary": Spivak's "to make a new word is to run the risk of forgetting the problem or believing it solved", applied to the substitution of "trustworthiness" for "validity", "because both words refer to legitimation or truth."

**And the question she leaves hanging over the field**: "If, in a research project, one were able to deconstruct even one, much less several, of the humanist concepts that organize the coherence of conventional qualitative inquiry . . . how would one then describe that project? Would it be qualitative? Would it be research?" With her own answer deferred: "**Whether we will call this work 'qualitative' remains to be seen.**"

She also places the chapter in its policy moment, and names the official: Grover J. Whitehurst, "Director of the U.S. Institute of Education Sciences and the creator of its What Works Clearinghouse", said at AERA 2003 "that we need less theory and more of what works and, further, that postmodern methods, in particular, will not help us learn what works." Her reply: "they might justifiably fear that science is being deconstructed, that it is increasingly unrecognizable, which means, of course, that the power they maintain within the old structure is likely to be diminished. **That could be good news indeed for the rest of us.**"

# PART THREE: RELATIONS AND FAULTS

## 9. Where these five sit beside the rest of the folder

**The voice thread is now complete in its own terms.** Jackson 2003 (rhizovocality), St. Pierre 2009 (the afterword to the book Jackson and Mazzei edited), Mazzei 2010 (where "voice without organs" is first asked as a question), Mazzei 2013 (where it becomes a concept), Jackson 2013 (the adjacent article on the same dataset through Pickering), and Jackson and Mazzei 2024 (where "data" is retired in favour of "performative accounts"). Six texts, one line, all read.

**The inversion recorded in the earlier file holds, and now has a third term.** Jackson 2003 wanted to know not what voice means "but what it does"; Mazzei 2013 asks "how does it work?"; and this batch confirms it twice more, in Mazzei's "how it works, who it works for, and what it does" and Jackson's "The point in analyzing mangled practices is not what they are but what they do." Shildrick's PITH team chose the opposite: "we were less interested in the descriptive question 'how?' than to ask the question 'why?'". Three post-qualitative researchers choosing "what it does" and one choosing "why". Still not adjudicated.

**Manning, now a three-way link.** Jackson and Mazzei read *Always More Than One* together and Manning entered the final chapter of their book; Gibson et al 2025 run on all three Manning books; and *Always More Than One* is already read in this folder. Those three reads can now be checked against each other.

**Butler, and a correction to the picture.** The earlier file recorded that *Bodies That Matter* is absent from Shildrick's bibliography and barely present in Mazzei 2013. It is central here: the retirement of "data" came from re-reading *Gender Trouble* and *Bodies That Matter*, and St. Pierre quotes Butler five times, including the sentence that lets her keep using "I": "My position is mine to the extent that 'I' – and I do not shirk from the pronoun – replay and resignify the theoretical positions that have constituted me." So Butler is load-bearing in this lineage and not in Shildrick's, which sharpens rather than softens the difference already recorded.

**Kotzé.** Her method chapter runs on *Thinking with Theory*, and this batch supplies its provenance: the book came out of a "long preparation" the two authors date to their doctoral training in the mid-1990s, and the cheerleader backflip her chapter refers to is Jackson's, first written up in "Deleuze and the girl" (2010) for Mazzei and McCoy's special issue. Her chapter also runs on an edition of the book that still used "data"; the 2023 edition does not.

**Shannon.** Mazzei and McCoy's "against metaphor" position and Shannon's double denial ("Barad is not writing metaphorically"; artworks "are not metaphors . . . they event concepts") are the same move, fourteen years apart, in the same journal. Neither cites the other. Shannon also cites Springgay and Truman for his "(in)tension", and Gibson et al cite the same paper, so three of the texts in this folder share that one source.

**The metaphor thread, revised.** Seven positions now, with Mazzei's corrected and moved to 2010.
- **Neimanis.** Figures that are "always more than metaphorical", working by "contiguity or continuity (rather than analogy)".
- **McKnight and Whitburn.** Entailments arrive whether intended or not; the test is affective; a ban is "beyond us as authors".
- **Betterton.** A figure of creativity applied to pregnancy erases the agency of the person it is applied to; the problem is deferred to her book.
- **Jackson.** Prefer "figuration" to "metaphor" because figuration keeps contradiction and disjunction (endnote 1, "Rhizovocality", 2003).
- **Mazzei, with McCoy.** Neither decorative nor literal: refuse "metaphors that were never intended as metaphors", and refuse equally the attempt "to conceive of a literal body without organs" (2010).
- **Shannon.** The word appears only in denials.
- **Shildrick.** No statement; a practice. Metaphor names what other people's language is doing to them, applied to herself once, as a withdrawal.
- *(Donnarumma, Murray and Gibson et al use the word only in passing or not at all, which stays an absence rather than a position.)*

## 10. Citation faults and things to check before citing

- Claire Parnet's surname is printed **"Parnat"** both in the body ("Deleuze and Parnat 1987/2002") and in the reference list ("Deleuze, G., and C. Parnat. 2002. *Dialogues II*").
- Lenz Taguchi's opening quotation is a personal communication with no date.

**Mazzei 2010.**
- Katharine Hepburn is printed **"Kathryn Hepburn"**.
- The acknowledgements list three conference presentations (BERA 2008, Stockholm 2009, AERA 2009) with no paper titles.

**Jackson 2013.**
- The reference list has a Descartes entry dated **"1998/1637"** with the translation year first, against the journal's own convention elsewhere of original/translation.
- Pickering is cited in three forms across two adjacent articles in the same issue. See section 3.

**Jackson and Mazzei 2024.**
- **Volume and issue are reversed in two entries**: "Jackson, A. Y. (2016). An ontology of a backflip. *Cultural Studies<->Critical Methodologies*, **2(16)**, 183-192" and "Mazzei, L. A. (2016). Voice without a subject. *Cultural Studies<->Critical Methodologies*, **2(16)**, 151-161". Both should be 16(2).
- **"Thinking without method" is cited two ways in the same article.** The body gives "'Thinking Without Method' (Jackson & Mazzei, 2017)" in one place and "'Thinking without Method' (Jackson, 2017)" in another, while the reference list carries both a Jackson 2017 solo article in *Qualitative Inquiry* 23(9), 666-674, and a Jackson and Mazzei 2017 handbook chapter. Two different items; check which is meant.
- Four entries put the volume number in parentheses, as "(23)(5)" and "(37)(4)", which is not the journal's style and makes the volume hard to read.
- Foucault 2000 is given as "*Power: Essential works of Foucault* **1954-1985**"; the series is conventionally 1954-1984.
- Grosz 2011 is cited as both *Becoming Undone* generally and "Chapter 5 of her book" with no chapter title.

**St. Pierre 2009.**
- **An apparent attribution slip**: "after studying poststructural theories of language (e.g., **Deleuze, 1993/1994, 1967/1974**; Foucault, 1971/1972)". The date pair 1967/1974 is *Of Grammatology*, which is Derrida, and she cites "Derrida, 1967/1974" correctly elsewhere in the same chapter. Read as Derrida unless the reference list says otherwise.
- The Carnap entry gives the same year for the original and the reprint: "(1966) . . . (Original work published 1966 by Basic Books under the title *Philosophical foundations of physics*)".
- Foucault's "So is it important to think?" is cited in the body as both "(Foucault, 2000, p. 456)" and "Foucault (1981)", the latter in a note, for the same interview.
- Spivak 1974 is the translator's preface to *Of Grammatology*, cited by its own year throughout without naming the host text.
