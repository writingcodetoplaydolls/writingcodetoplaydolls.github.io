# PART ONE: MURRAY

## 1. The title, the corpus and the method he claims

**Where the phrase comes from, and he says so in his first line.** "The phrase 'testbed of futurity' appears in William Gibson's 1996 novel *Idoru*", in a scene where the protagonist Colin Laney demands "What is all this about?" and Kuwayama answers: "It's about futurity . . . We have never developed a sinister view of technology, Mr. Laney. It is an aspect of the natural, of oneness. Through our efforts, oneness perfects itself. . . . And popular culture […] is the testbed of our futurity."

**The corpus**, all North American and all published in the seven years before the article: Emily St John Mandel's *Station Eleven* (2014), Jeff VanderMeer's *Borne* (2017), Cherie Dimaline's *The Marrow Thieves* (2017), Ling Ma's *Severance* (2018), Peng Shepherd's *The Book of M* (2018), Thea Lim's *An Ocean of Minutes* (2018) and Larissa Lai's *The Tiger Flu* (2018). "While my argument is drawn from my reading of all these, the central focus is on *The Marrow Thieves*, *Borne*, and *The Tiger Flu*."

**He states the method's limit before the argument**, which is worth noting as practice: "Because of word limitation, my method is designed necessarily to be exploratory, with an aim to point towards future work that can unpack the complexities raised here in more detail."

**The abstract's formula, repeated almost verbatim in the body, and it is the article's thesis:** "Bodies remain material, but they also become metamorphized and messy; they hold charged manifestations of personhood, but also leak these conceptions of 'person;' they are recognizably human, but also patterned as posthuman."

**Why speculative and not science fiction**, and the distinction is sharp. Ria Cheyne reads disability in science fiction through the genre's "varieties of wonder", and Murray says his novels do the opposite: "Embodiment or cognitive difference in these texts is far more likely to exist in spaces of contested belonging, frustrated incomprehension, and jaded indifference than awe, admiration, or curiosity." And the second difference: "such difference in speculative fiction slides away from an immediately recognizable sense of the disabled subject as disabled . . . the most thoughtful contemporary speculative fictions of disaster and social breakdown place **all bodies under pressure**, and that which might be conventionally read as disabled (in terms of standard notions of absence, lack, or loss) becomes overturned."

**The two terms he borrows and the guard he puts on one of them.** Sami Schalk's *Bodyminds Reimagined* supplies the phrase and the claim that this "changes the rules of interpretation"; Murray's guard: "to re-think interpretation in the ways that Schalk encourages is not to negate disabled agency and presence; rather it heralds a critical process that places innovative disability-led embodiment at the centre of futurity narratives, championing the often-radical potential of the disabled body." And Mitchell and Snyder supply "antinormative novels of embodiment", fictions that "reverse the usual line of diagnosis imposed upon disabled bodies" and treat "disability as revelatory of variation's potential for innovation", a "**capacity of incapacity**".

## 2. The readings, including the one that fails the argument

**Station Eleven is his negative case, and he does not soften it.** As the Georgia Flu spreads across Toronto, Jeevan visits his wheelchair-using brother Frank, and Frank decides to kill himself, to "leave first", because he could not "survive" outside. Murray: "For the brothers and indeed the narrative itself, this appears as an irrefutable logic. **For Jeevan to take part in the new mobility created by the pandemic, Frank has to die.**"

Then the second move, which is the reading that makes the section. The novel's theme of beauty in ruin, "there is still such beauty", runs even through the suicide scene, where Jeevan comments on the "stark and unexpected beauty" of the "silent metropolis" outside, a place of "no movement". Murray: "The novel asserts that, ultimately, the new world will be a place where literature, music, and beauty are still possible, and where new forms of cooperation may lead to just societies, **but not for a character such as Frank**."

**His endnote 3 is the sharpest sentence in the article**, and it is the one most worth lifting: "It is an irony that in the kind of hectic, 24/7 pre-pandemic world where people with disabilities are frequently judged to be too slow to keep up (in terms of 'efficiency' or 'productivity'), here they are rendered immobile and unfit for life in societies that are forced to slow down. **No matter how fast or slow society is, people with disabilities are always portrayed as slower: if the contemporary world is configured as speed, disability is slow; in slow post-pandemic societies, disability is static.**"

**Borne**, and this is where Shildrick enters. The city is wrecked by a company's escaped biotechnology: mobile modified plant life, giant lizards, "beetles that can be inserted into ears to produce fictional memories of pre-apocalypse safety", and a flying bear called Mord. Rachel is attacked by five augmented feral children, quoted: "four had traded their eyes for green-gold wasps that curled into their sockets and compounded their vision. Claws graced their hands like sharp commas. Scales at their throats burned red when they breathed."

Murray's insistence is that the children's inhumanity does not make Rachel human by contrast: "the children's inhumanity is not countered by any presumption of Rachel's humanism or bodily integrity", and he quotes her: "I was alive, and from past experience I knew in time that I would forget enough to again pretend that we could someday be free. . . . I don't know if that was hope. Maybe it was just stubborn inertia."

The pivotal scene is Rachel climbing inside Borne's body as he becomes a rock, which she experiences as a split: "In one world, I was cocooned inside a living organism that still defied explanation . . . In the other world, I was inside a cave trying to hide from a wild animal." Afterwards Borne is damaged beyond his own shape-shifting repair: "his left side was fissured and purpling-black and the ring of eyes, darkly luminous, circled his body in a haphazard way, like a rotting carnival ride one loose bolt away from spinning off into the crowd."

**And then the Shildrick sentence:** "In this moment, both Borne and Rachel exemplify the kind of **leaky bodies and visceral prostheses** explored by Margrit Shildrick in her work on the complexities of the microbiome. As Shildrick explores, difference within the organic body articulates a challenge to conventional understandings of the human ('Body Shock'; 'Matters of the Heart'). It recasts bodies as full of matter, physical states that display transcorporeality as a norm and not exception."

His conclusion on the novel, with the genre label refused: "This is not, it should be stressed, a dystopian vision; the novel's imagining does not subscribe to such a term", ending on Rachel's "The real reality is something we create every moment of every day […] So we set to work."

**The Tiger Flu**, which is the clearest case in the article of disability as the community's survival mechanism. A group of "genetically modified parthenogenic women (the Grist sisters)" exiled from Saltwater City, with "a mode of community reproduction based around organ transplantation and physical re-growth (characters are designated 'grooms,' 'doublers,' and 'starfish,' capable of the organic replacement and restitution of body parts)". Kirilow, quoted: "We split, we slit, we heal, we groom, self-mutated beyond the know-how of the clone company Jemini that spawned us . . . Only our starfish can save us, by regrowing whatever grooms like me cut out of them."

The ending he describes: 150 years later Kora "has evolved into a 'Starfish Tree,' from which human organs can be harvested for the perpetuation of the women of the community: a transformation from human to plant form that presents her selfhood as part of a dispersed assemblage and network of the organic and inorganic", while Kirilow, "now an 'ancient groom elder'", holds "a 'wrinkled, blackened bit of matter' that turns out to be a severed hand" kept for a century and a half.

**And the second Shildrick citation, this one on transplantation:** "That these are matters of disability can be seen in the novel's enaction of the kind of work on disability and organ transplantation by Shildrick, Donna McCormack, and others ('Messy Entanglements'; 'The Transplant Imaginary')."

**The Marrow Thieves**, read for what the harmed body does *not* signify. The premise: a Canadian indigenous community is "'harvested' for bone marrow that will potentially cure the 'dreamless' state of the non-indigenous majority population", leaving "missing eyes, lower limb amputation, wounds from invasive operations". His claim: "In Dimaline's novel, it is precisely the point that **the harmed body does not translate into the 'broken' community**. An ableist language of historical conquest is, it is made clear, an inadequate account of what a future society might be." And on the novel's ending: "marked bodies—in spite of their connections to histories of violence—eschew ideas of personal, racial, and indigenous vulnerability and instead speak of a future moving towards complex affiliations of presence."

He takes "becoming" from the novel's own practice rather than from theory, and says so: "My use of the word becoming above is informed directly by terms of indigenous reconceptualization, a direct reference to the individual 'coming-to' stories that each central character in *The Marrow Thieves* recounts to a communal audience, part of tribal traditions." Braidotti's becoming-with is brought in afterwards, as corroboration. Endnote 4 points to Gross's "post-apocalypse stress syndrome" and Larsen's claim that indigenous North Americans are "the postapocalyptic people", and records that Dimaline's novel "is working with indigenous traditions built on ideas of relationality, animism, and networked being, so its ideas of futurity articulate a continuum of past and present."

**Siebers supplies the key term.** "serious consideration of the disabled body exposes that our current theories of reality are not as sophisticated as we would like to think . . . More often than not, these theories are driven by ethical concerns rather than the desire to represent what happens to bodies in the world"; and "The body is, first and foremost, a biological agent **teeming** with vital and often unruly forces." Murray's use of the word is his own addition: "I am also drawn toward Siebers's use of *teeming*, because its sense of an overflow, a 'too-muchness,' fits not only the complexities of the disabled body, but also the cluttered, unregulated, and decaying environments central to much near-future speculative fiction."

## 3. His caveats, which are a model and should be kept whole

The conclusion opens by withdrawing generality from the whole corpus, in four steps.

"There are important caveats: the near futures envisaged in the fiction discussed here are **all North American** and as such share crucial commonalities."

"Most are fundamentally **suburban narratives** in which (despite cross-cutting issues of embodiment, material difference, race, and gender) it is an idea of the 'comfortable' that is thrown into crisis."

"**Nearly all the novels I analyse pitch middle-class characters into crumbling middle-class/professional worlds**, whether the arts in *Station Eleven*, the corporate world in *Severance*, post-graduate privilege in *The Book of M*, or biotechnology in *Borne*. Arguably, only *The Marrow Thieves* and *The Tiger Flu* genuinely begin with communities already outside of the structures of power and acceptance, where post-settlement and exile are precursors to post-apocalypse."

"it is important to note that other cultures and societies will find different localities to express their imagined futures." Plus endnote 5: "I do not have space here to investigate the complexities in the writing locations of the various authors discussed, especially those who are Asian North American."

And the sentence that survives the caveats, which is the article's positive claim: "**Disability disrupts the capacity inherent in comfort.**"

He closes on Kafer: "the futures we imagine reveal the biases of the present; it seems entirely possible that imagining different futures and temporalities might help us see, and do, the present differently", with his own last line admitting the present it was written in: "there are productive possibilities for re-visioning the present, even during what—on our worst days—we fear as an unimaginable crisis."

# PART TWO: GIBSON ET AL

## 4. What the study was, with its numbers

**The design.** "an exploratory participatory design and multiple methods". **Five youth partners, aged 16 to 27, all of whom identified as disabled**, as members of the research team rather than as subjects. Setting: "a Canadian city during the Covid19 pandemic."

**What was produced.** Over three weeks the youth partners "used a secure interactive app (ethOS™) to independently create and post images, videos and stories of how they moved through their worlds in their everyday activities", communicating with a research assistant through the app. Each also did "an individual dialogue (one-to-one interview) and a group dialogue workshop with the other partners". "Engagement in the various activities and dialogues occurred over the course of **14 months** wherein partners created/shared and discussed **48 photos, 32 videos** and various other poems, academic writings/performances and internet postings."

**Who the people were, as the article states them.** "Our academic team members included six researchers who claimed diverse spaces of belonging in terms of race, sexuality, disability and more and intersecting roles including artist, activist, professor, student, clinician and ally. All of us live in close relation with disability, either identifying as disabled and/or dwelling in and contributing to disability community and culture. Study youth partners included those who identified as LGBTQ+, as racialized and as nonbinary. **Four of the five were wheelchair users** and all had a range of impairments and medical diagnoses." Followed immediately by the limit on that account: "we also recognize that these are unstable and partial descriptions and do not begin to capture all the sociomaterial forces, objects, places, positionalities and inclinations that choreographed the study."

**The data availability statement is a position, not boilerplate, and is worth quoting as practice.** "Data for this study is not available in order to maintain the anonymity of the five youth partner participants. Data includes identifiable details in visual, auditory and written form that cannot be de-identified without significant redaction, compromising anonymity and/or narrative integrity."

**What they refuse to claim.** "we have not set out to present a 'valid interpretation' of 'findings,' but to rigorously explore relational potentials of the data as part of a knowledge-producing assemblage." And on the decisions behind the analysis: "research decisions were not intentional in the standard sense, but emergent tendencies that collided, vibrated and coalesced to move thought in new directions." A short film came out of it, at revisioncentre.ca.

## 5. The concept they add: choreography

"We approached our research as choreography, where choreography is understood as a co-mingling of ideas, people and things in/across certain time-spaces", so that "Choreographies generate, translate and transmit knowledge; they enact something new through a coming together that does not assume or rely on the intentions of human choreographers."

The term for what they then did with it, taken from Manning: data moments were treated as "choreographic objects" acting as "propositions or points of inflection", and choreography "cleaves an occasion, activating its relational potential". Dokumacı supplies the formula for where an affordance lives: it resides "neither in the environment nor in the body but in the inherent coupling of the two". And Garland-Thomson supplies the capacity claim: "Acquiring or being born with the traits we call disabilities fosters an adaptability and resourcefulness that often is underdeveloped in those whose bodies fit smoothly into the prevailing, sustaining environment."

**Their definition of movement, quoted from Kelly et al and adopted whole**, which is much wider than kinematics: movement as "encompassing the sensory, perceptual, affective, emotive, communicative, cognitive, physical, psychic, spiritual and all other actual and potential doings of bodies that ripple out into the world to have formative and transformative effects."

**Their precise definition of "affirmative"**, which they repeat twice because it is easily misread: "'affirmative' movement where the term connotes a disruptive change that is open-ended and unpredictable, rather than predetermined as either 'positive' or 'negative'."

And the hierarchy they name in rehabilitation, which is the thing being contested: "there are 'normal' and 'pathological' movements, and there are moral hierarchies of mobilities wherein, for example, independent ambulation is ordered above wheeling."

## 6. The three experiments

**Energy: Miles.** A theatre studies student, actor and dancer, who describes his power wheelchair as "a moveable island" and "a repository of energy". His own words: "My wheelchair actually works to an advantage because it provides more sensation that I can bounce off of to be in touch with my body from a physical standpoint. Living with a disability and being a power wheelchair-user feels like you're accessing the world from a moveable island." And: "I often think about how my wheelchair has become a repository of energy because almost every major event in my life, whether it's positive or negative, has happened from this chair. . . . even when you're out of the wheelchair, you cannot be very far away from it. For better or for worse, it is bound to you, and you are tethered to your equipment. . . **Forgive the very spiritual metaphor**, but it's almost as if your soul is tethered to more things. And the more things your soul is tethered to, the more you need to bring along. Which makes your access needs more difficult because society isn't built for those access needs."

This is where they cite Shildrick: "**Prosthetic bodies (Shildrick, 2022)** come into relation with forces, lines of articulation (Miles: 'what society isn't built for'), which limit potential AND spur innovation."

**Allison's poem**, "Beyond The Wheelchair", quoted at length with its line breaks marked by pipes: "This wheelchair doesn't define me |This wheelchair says nothing about me | You see this is the problem, so many people see my chair before they see me |They see the thing that gets me around and assume my brain doesn't work so they talk to whoever is with me |No| I am a human being! |I can talk for myself! |Yes, I have a physical disability |But it's not who I am . . . |People think because I'm in this wheelchair that I'm special |They want a high five even though they barely know me | You think it's cool that I'm living my life despite my disability? |No! |Why can't you see me as just another human |I don't need or want special treatment |I just want acceptance!" And from her interview: "That's the point of that poem—I am a human like you."

**They do not harmonise Miles and Allison, and they say so.** "While the ideas in the poem differ significantly from those shared by Miles, both can be read in terms of re-worlding disability. We read both as enfoldings: moving away and back at once." Allison's claim is read through the second Shildrick citation, Shildrick 2000: "Difference is configured not as other but a claim to 'difference as non-difference' or more specifically, as the irreducible differences connecting all humans."

**Active passivities: Jack.** This experiment contains the article's best piece of self-examination, and it is about their own instrument. Jack posted: "I don't go outside much because I prefer being inside. I like to make music on my computer and play video games." But "almost all the posts that Jack shared for the study reflected activities outside his family home", and for a "typical day" he posted an amusement park outing. Their reading is of the study, not of Jack: "This suggested to us that Jack had understood the kind of content we sought for the study in a particular way, i.e., in terms of doing something 'active' or dynamic that involved physical movement . . . This is understandable given how the study was presented as focused on mobilities and perhaps more so considering the normative characterizations of activities as 'active' or 'passive' that pervade health care and the social imaginary (e.g., 'sitting is the new smoking,' active lifestyles, the presumed perils of screen time). . . . **Jack assembled a subject that worked to satisfy what the research and perhaps society, seemed to be demanding: Active-Jack.**"

The reframing, via Manning: "that a body moves without displacing itself means that rest becomes an instance of absolute movement. Rest becomes an activity of rhythm." Their conclusion: "there can be no passive/active distinction and more profoundly, no stable movement/stillness distinction."

**The chair passage**, which is the most concrete thinking in the article: "Consider these two different chairs. The chair with wheels (wheel-chair), is usually seen as a mobility chair but it does other things. It is also something people sit in, like other chairs. Wheeling is both a position of rest and a position of dynamic activity." Then the roller coaster: "The roller coaster chair is designed for fun and safety, for safe fun. . . . One size fits all! (which of course it doesn't—as a universal chair it does not 'accommodate' all morphologies). . . . It is not about physical displacement, even though displacement occurs. Getting to another location is not the point, there is no other 'there' to go to, only a re-turn. It plays another game: movement for movement's sake, for pleasure's sake." Ending: "**Jack becomes through stillness and speed.**"

**Conveyance: Neil and Linda.** Neil "communicated through a variety of methods including verbalizations, two electronic communication devices and/or communication partners", the partners including family members and Linda, his assistant. The interview was on Zoom.

**They quote the research assistant's own clinical note in full and then hold it up for inspection**, which is the most transferable methodological move in either article. The note: "Neil has cerebral palsy and severe dystonia which cause him to have involuntary movements and postures which significantly affect his gross motor, fine motor, and oral motor coordination. He uses a power wheelchair and requires a full-time personal support worker with him to assist with activities of daily living. Neil does attempt verbalizations but has difficulty with functional speech."

Their reading: "The research assistant draws on her familiarity with clinical logics and terminology to convey to the research team something about Neil. Its descriptive and use-full for the clinicians on the team, **while also jarring in its use of medical jargon**. Something is conveyed: we picture a young man whose body is unruly, whose speech may be difficult to understand. But in picturing Neil, we also reflect that what gets produced in biomedicine which is centred around lack and differences that are marked in a negative register. 'Involuntary movements,' 'difficulties,' 'requires help,' 'severe dystonia' all suggest difference as deficit, captured as a medicalized problem. **But other cuts, other 'Neils,' are possible** that do not position his movements as lack or problem but as forms of conveyance that have equal moral standing with so-called 'functional speech'."

The transcript they print, with Linda's own framing of her role: "Neil tries with his words. We have a Proloquo2Go [a communication app] as well. He can pick his words, and he has his eye gaze, but his eye gaze isn't up right now. We have his iPad and his [app]. . . but I'm also here to help decipher if you don't understand what he is saying."

Their reading of "decipher": "Neil as communicator is inscribed as a code, a cipher, that is, a conglomeration of non-normative symbols, cues, utterances and movements that come together to convey something. Linda reads the codes as an intricate part of an assembled conveyance machine. . . . **Together they produce ciphers and decipherers.** The communication app also de-ciphers. It transforms body talk into electro-talk."

**And their definition of voice**, which is a one-sentence materialist account: "'Voice' is always a conglomeration of physical mechanics—air, muscle, glottis, lips, tongue—semiotics (language), positionings (class, gender, race) and agentic expression (perspectives, claims)."

Plus one observation that generalises: Neil's whole body moves when he speaks, which "suggest[s] that speaking is effortful, but also remind[s] us that **communication is a whole-body affair for all bodies**."

They also note what the method had to give up: "Neil-Linda-research assistant's communication did not conform to typical interview temporalities or 'one-to-one' conventions of research interviewing methods."

## 7. Their self-flags

Four, and the last two are unusually direct for a published paper.

On what they are not doing: "we do not ignore or minimize disabled people's oppression and suffering, or dismiss the importance of political struggles to create enabling worlds."

On what the paper is for: "**Our aim was to produce problems rather than solve them**, or more accurately, to mobilize new ways of thinking."

On the risk the whole method carries: "The risk in posthuman experimentation is a minimization of the pain and suffering of minoritized people. . . . **We approach this work with deep humility and trepidation, knowing that we may inadvertently be contributing to harms that we are hoping to address.**"

On what they left out, named specifically: "Choreographing the field of affects that act on/to/with bodies inevitably attunes to some forces and not others. Specifically, **our analyses say little about the entangled lines of articulation that materialize minoritized differences along lines of, e.g., race, gender, sexuality and coloniality**. Moreover, Puar (2017) reminds us that the material conditions for positive re-envisionings of disability are not readily available to all. Our intent is not to obscure these formations and the suffering they evoke. We approach these cautions by inviting critique and further scholarship that disrupts differences along other lines."

Ahmed supplies their formula for micro-activism: "(a) failure to use something properly can be a refusal to use something properly", with her point that the resulting misfit "can be generative rather than necessarily catastrophic".

**They close on a youth partner's words, not their own.** Naomi: "Today, now, in this moment, I can confidently say as a young disabled, woman of colour, I desire the ugliness for the ways it 'dismantles' and 'disrupts' what it means to be beautiful. I desire this ugliness for the ways it contains a magnificence that breaks down the wall that once stood in the way of my disabled body's freedom. . . I respect my disabled body in all its magnificence for the ways it gives me strength and power and for how it pushes me to flourish and thrive. It gives me the liberty to give myself titles of lifesaver, survivor, mermaid and butterfly."

# PART THREE: THE RELATIONS

## 8. The three-way citing circle, now documented

This is the finding that justified reading these two next, and it checks out in all three directions.

**Shildrick to Gibson.** *Visceral Prostheses* chapter 1 builds its "low-key assemblages" section on Barbara Gibson's work: the ventilator study with young men, quoted as "a fluid body . . . a conglomeration of energies. He has replaceable parts. . . . His organs are here, there, and everywhere" (Gibson 2006), and the study of a twelve-year-old girl and her mother (Gibson, Carnevale and King 2012). Gibson's 2006 article is also cited in this 2025 paper's own last line of argument, "Refusing and re-fusing (Gibson, 2006)".

**Gibson to Shildrick.** Gibson et al 2025 cite *Visceral Prostheses* (2022) by name at the point where they describe Miles's wheelchair as a repository of energy: "Prosthetic bodies (Shildrick, 2022)". They also cite Shildrick 2000, "Becoming vulnerable: Contagious encounters and the ethics of risk", for "difference as non-difference". Two entries, two distinct uses.

**Shildrick to Murray.** *Visceral Prostheses* names his monograph as the field's single exception to a diagnosis: "scholarly analysis of the concept of crafting the body through substantive technological transformations has tended to become arrested at the stage of descriptive wonder. **Stuart Murray's recent monograph *Disability and the Posthuman* (2020) is a welcome exception**, but its insights are firmly situated – even as he pushes at the limits – within the field of disability studies." She also quotes him in a chapter 1 endnote, on transhumanism: "Its appeal to technological knowledge is simply an updated version of an old story that in its ultimate form leads to genocide and the characterisation of 'a life not worth living'."

**Murray to Shildrick.** Three works cited: "Body Shock: Unsettling the Biosciences Through Postconventional Materialities", *Somatechnics* 9.2-3 (2019); "Matters of the Heart: Temporality and Microchimeric Entanglements", in *Entangled Bodies: Art, Identity, Intercorporeality*, ed. Tammer El Sheikh (2020); and "Messy Entanglements: Research Assemblages in Heart Transplantation Discourses and Practices", with Andrew Carnie, Alexa Wright and others, *BMJ Medical Humanities* 44 (2018). Murray uses the first two to read *Borne* and the third to read *The Tiger Flu*.

**Two of these are not in the folder and are directly relevant.** "Matters of the Heart" sits in a collection edited by Tammer El Sheikh, who is the cultural theorist named on Shildrick's own PITH team in her endnote. And "Messy Entanglements" is co-authored with Andrew Carnie and Alexa Wright, the two PITH visual artists, which makes it the one published account of the PITH art collaboration. Neither is held.

## 9. Where these two sit beside the rest of the folder

**Manning, and this is worth acting on.** Gibson et al run almost entirely on Erin Manning: *Relationscapes* (2009) for preacceleration and sitability, *Always More Than One* (2013) for choreographic objects, and *The Minor Gesture* (2016) for the article's governing concept, quoted four times including "(T)he minor gesture can open the way for a different kind of knowing, a knowing in the event, in nonlinear event-time, a knowing that, while impossible to parse, delights in the force of conceptual invention." *Always More Than One* is already read in this folder, in two files. So Gibson et al is the clearest worked application of Manning to disability research available here, and the Manning reads can be checked against it.

**Shannon.** No citation either way, and the structural similarity is close enough to record. Both attach devices to bodies and then refuse the device's own framing: Shannon will not call the EDA gizmo a "sensor" because "it's actively doing something to the body(mind)"; Gibson et al will not let the wheelchair be only a mobility device, because "Wheeling is both a position of rest and a position of dynamic activity". Both treat holding a tension open as the method rather than as a failure, Shannon as "friction" between four incommensurate orientations and Gibson et al as reading Miles and Allison as "enfoldings: moving away and back at once" without harmonising them. Both build method from their own instrument misbehaving, Shannon from the gizmos glitching and Gibson et al from Jack posting the wrong kind of day. And both quote their participants' own words at length rather than paraphrasing. Where they differ: Shannon is a single-author account of his own art practice, Gibson et al is a seven-author study with the participants as team members.

**Donnarumma.** No citation either way, and one near-identical passage. Gibson et al quote S. Wilson (2017) on musical performance: "the instrumentalists' gestures become, through the instrument, expressive output; in a feed-back relation the instrumentalist also responds to the sound produced . . . such as to modify their bodily gestures and hence the sonic outcome once again." That is Donnarumma's attunement argument, almost clause for clause: "The body and the instrument enter a feedback relation: the body gives energy to the instrument and this returns energy back to the player." Two texts, one in *Health* and one in *Frontiers in Computer Science*, reaching the same formulation from different fields, neither citing the other. Recorded as a convergence, not as a shared position.

**Fries, Fahey, Bufano.** Murray's corpus is fiction, not art practice, so the link is to Shildrick's chapter 1 rather than directly. But Murray's claim that these novels place "disability-led bodily transformation at the heart of" creativity, and Siebers's "teeming" as "a 'too-muchness'", sit alongside the artists' refusals of illustration already recorded across the Fries, Fahey, Shannon and Donnarumma reads. Five texts now, none citing the others.

**Jackson, Mazzei, St. Pierre.** Gibson et al is squarely in that lineage and cites it at one remove: Springgay and Truman's "On the need for methods beyond proceduralism: Speculative middles, (in)tensions", which is the same paper Shannon uses for his "(in)tension" to method, and Fox and Alldred for the research assemblage. The language of agential cuts is explicit: "All those involved made particular agential cuts in the data (i.e., drew boundaries, foregrounded some ideas and not others), flowing into openings to enact certain potentials and not others, propagating rhizomatic shoots of analysis." So Barad's cut is doing work here that it does not do in *Visceral Prostheses*, where Barad appears four times with no alignment.

**The metaphor thread: two small additions, neither of them a position.**

A participant flags his own figure: Miles says "**Forgive the very spiritual metaphor**, but it's almost as if your soul is tethered to more things." That is a research participant doing to his own language exactly what McKnight and Whitburn ask researchers to do to theirs, unprompted, and Gibson et al quote it without comment.

And the authors note a field's saturation in passing: "In music where **movement metaphors abound**", before quoting Wilson.

Murray's one relevant instance is a citation, Watkins on women's apocalyptic fiction replacing "colonial and patriarchal narratives of paternity and conquest with **metaphors** of mother-daughter relationships", which he reports without comment.

So neither article states a position on figure. Both use the word the way Shildrick does, as a passing name for what someone else's language is doing. The six positions recorded in the *Visceral Prostheses* file stand unchanged.
