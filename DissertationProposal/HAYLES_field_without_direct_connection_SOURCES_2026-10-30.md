# Influence without a direct connection: the passages, and the Kundera question

30 Oct 2026. Source material only. The passages are set out verbatim with their page numbers so they can be worked from directly. Nothing here is a reading of them.

## 1. Hayles, *The Cosmic Web* (1984)

**p. 22.** The sentence that rules out the causal picture:

> That Saussure's proposals are remarkably similar in spirit to those occurring about the same time in physics and mathematics does not require that Saussure knew of Einstein's 1905 papers or read *Principia Mathematica*. **Indeed, to suppose that such parallels require direct lines of influence is to be wedded to the very notions of causality that a field model renders obsolete.**

**p. 23.** The relation is two-way, and neither side is prior:

> Such a history would insist that we not be misled by a causal perspective into thinking of correspondences between disciplines as one-way exchanges, for example, by asserting that the change in scientific paradigms caused a shift in literary form. **In a field model, the interactions are always mutual: the cultural matrix guides individual inquiry at the same time that the inquiry helps to form, or transform, the matrix.**

**p. 24.** Both available models of influence are rejected by name:

> Since I am assuming that these novels are affected by the shift toward the new paradigm, the reader may wonder whether I also mean to imply that the authors are thoroughly conversant with field models, or are trying to re-create it within their works. **In my opinion, both of these models of "influence" oversimplify the interaction between an author and his culture.** Most of the authors I am concerned with know little of science, and what little they do know is often colored by their idiosyncratic interpretations.

**p. 25.** What is left once both are rejected:

> With few exceptions, these authors are reacting **not to science as such, but to a more general set of ideas pervasive in the culture.**

> There can be no question of choosing the "right" texts, for the argument is that **the influence of the field concept is pervasive throughout the literature.**

> The selection is diverse enough to show that **a writer does not have to be post-modernist to be affected by the field concept**; authors as different in their literary techniques and philosophies as Lawrence and Nabokov, Pynchon and Pirsig, **are all affected**.

> It is interesting that few of Pirsig's sources come directly from physics; Einstein is merely mentioned, for example, and **Heisenberg appears not at all.** Consequently, this text also demonstrates that **a writer can be concerned with issues that have been brought into focus by the paradigm shift without necessarily being familiar with those sources that most directly brought it about.**

**p. 26.** Quoted by Beer (2025: 175) from this page, and not yet checked against the page image:

> Lawrence and Nabokov demonstrate **how writers who are relatively ignorant of the new science nevertheless participate in the cultural matrix and so, willy-nilly, encounter in some form the matrix's underlying paradigm.**

**[My note, which is a count and not a reading.]** Across these seven pages of the Introduction (printed pp. 21 to 27), the word **affect** and its forms occur 3 times and **influence** and its forms occur 4 times, and the occurrences of influence are mostly the word being held at arm's length, in quotation marks or under negation. Across the whole book, affect and its forms occur 9 times.

## 2. Hayles, *Chaos Bound* (1990)

**p. 3.** The islands:

> Suppose an island breaks through the surface of the water, then another and another, until the sea is dotted with islands. Each has its own ecology, terrain, and morphology. **One can recognize these distinctions and at the same time wonder whether they are all part of an emerging mountain range, connected both through substrata they share and through the larger forces that brought them into being.**

**p. 4.** The refusal, then the mechanism:

> **The connections I explore among contemporary literature, critical theory, and science are not generally explainable by direct influence.** Rather, they derive from the fact that writers, critics, and scientists, however specialized or esoteric their work, all share certain kinds of everyday experiences.

> An influence argument would look for direct connections to explain these convergences. Sometimes such connections exist. It is possible that Barthes listened to Cage, Cage studied Lorenz, Lorenz read Barthes. But it stands to reason that, **of all the interdisciplinary parallels one might notice, only a few will be connected by direct lines of influence**, which are usually conveyed through disciplinary traditions.

> Interdisciplinary parallels commonly operate according to a different dynamic. Here influence spreads out through a diffuse network of everyday experiences that range from reading *The New York Times* to using bank cards on automatic teller machines to watching MTV. **When enough of the implications in these activities point in the same direction, they create a cultural field within which certain questions or concepts become highly charged.**

**pp. 4 to 5.** The Eno passage, where the connection is allowed not to have happened at all:

> Perhaps, for example, Brian Eno might first learn about Roland Barthes through *Time* magazine. Intrigued, he might read one of Barthes's books. **Or he might not.** The brief article summarizing Barthes's ideas would then become **one of the elements in Eno's cultural field, available to be reinforced by other elements until a resonance built up** which was strong enough to be a contributing factor in his work.

**p. 23.** The field is not uniform:

> The examples illustrate how **the cultural field can energize different kinds of reevaluations, because the arrows in the field do not all point in the same direction.**

## 4. The Kundera reference: what was found and what was not

**Not found, and it should not be cited until it is.** No Kundera passage about the word **reason** having two meanings in German turned up, in *The Book of Laughter and Forgetting* or anywhere else. Nothing by Kundera is held anywhere in these folders. The corpus mentions him three times and none is about German: a thesis quoting the airbrushed-photograph opening of *The Book of Laughter and Forgetting* (Harper Collins, New York, 1999, p. 4), a bibliography entry, and an epigraph from *The Unbearable Lightness of Being* about Franz and music.

**The Kundera passage that has the shape described** is about **compassion**, not reason, and it is in ***The Unbearable Lightness of Being*, Part One, "Lightness and Weight"**, not *The Book of Laughter and Forgetting*. Latin-derived languages build the word from a prefix meaning "with" and a root meaning suffering, *com-passio*. Czech, Polish, German and Swedish build it instead from a prefix plus the word for feeling: *soucit*, *wspolczucie*, **Mitgefuhl**, *medkansla*. Kundera's point is that the Latin-derived word, and "pity" with it, carries condescension toward the sufferer, while the feeling-based word means the ability to live another's emotion, joy as well as sorrow. **This summary is from search results and from quotation sites, not from the book, and the wording has not been checked against any printing.**

**The untranslatable-word set piece in *The Book of Laughter and Forgetting*** is **litost**, in Part Five, where Kundera writes that he has looked in vain in other languages for an equivalent. That is the passage of that book most often cited for an untranslatable word. **Also quoted at second hand and unchecked.**

**The German double meaning usually called problematic for "reason"** is **Grund**, meaning ground or cause, against **Vernunft**, the faculty, with **Verstand** as a third term. Heidegger's *Der Satz vom Grund* is built on exactly that doubling, by way of Leibniz's principle of sufficient reason. **Whether Kundera writes anywhere about it has not been established.**

**What would settle it**: a copy of *The Unbearable Lightness of Being* and of *The Book of Laughter and Forgetting*, or a full-text search of *The Art of the Novel* (which contains "Sixty-three Words", his own dictionary) and *Testaments Betrayed* (which contains the long analysis of translating Kafka's German). Those are fetches.

## 5. Added later on 30 Oct: Hayles answering this exact question in 1989

`K:\OneDrive\UNM\Dissertation\todo October 26\ijls-2516-araas-vesely.pdf`, the interview with Suzanne Araas-Vesely conducted after *The Cosmic Web* and before *Chaos Bound*. The interviewer puts the question directly.

**The question asked:**

> Of course, that guiding role of literature might be a matrix type of role rather than, as you said, **depending on direct influence**. Do you think that the model of what you call a "cultural matrix" would require Whitehead's "climate of opinion" to be in place before, say, Einstein's and Saussure's independent efforts could both suggest a relation of field models? **Or can an individual apprehend a physical-cultural matrix?**

**Hayles's answer, which allows a node to work against the field and still be in it:**

> Individual creative acts spring from the interaction between an individual's sensibility and the cultural climate in which that individual works. **When an individual vision is very idiosyncratic or very strongly motivated internally, it's quite possible that a vision can emerge that works at cross-purposes with the prevailing cultural climate.** A good example, I think, would be William Blake.

The interviewer then says Blake "is still interacting with the cultural matrix around him", and Hayles continues:

> **Strong evidence for the effect of culture on all kinds of creative acts is demonstrated by the fact that important scientific discoveries are often made simultaneously, or nearly so, in widely separated sites.** And this happens between science and other areas as well. Art, literature, theology, philosophy, very often manifest important correlations with one another. **And the most compelling explanation for this phenomenon is cultural.**

**And the threshold condition, where an unreinforced idea stays real but inert:**

> similar concepts can and do occur to thinkers in any period. But that **if there are not enough other people thinking along the same lines, if the idea is not strongly reinforced in the culture, then it is not likely to enter into the cultural mainstream** and to become part of its tradition. **It exists, it may even endure because of individual genius, but it will not have the same kind of effect** as when a culture [the sentence continues past the page]

**And the limit she sets on her own first book:**

> You're touching here on an aspect of my first book that I try to go beyond. **In *The Cosmic Web* I hypothesized a cultural matrix without really looking at what it meant or how it works.** In my second book, *Chaos Bound*, I've tried to go more deeply into the questions and, at the same time, to pose a more complex model for how interactions between disciplinary inquiries and cultural institutions take place. You're quite right to suggest that **it's too simple to think of one cultural matrix in an age, because clearly culture is highly fissured and stratified, not by any means homogeneous or monolithic.**

### 6.2 Field theory to assemblage theory: what the one text held says

`atkinson-2023-field-theory-and-assemblage-theory-toward-a-constructive-dialogue.txt`, held in the project corpora:

**Will Atkinson, "Field Theory and Assemblage Theory: Toward a Constructive Dialogue", *Theory, Culture & Society* 41(1), 2024, 79-94** (published online 2023), DOI 10.1177/02632764231167774. From its abstract:

> This paper engages with Manuel DeLanda's Deleuze-inspired 'assemblage theory' from a perspective sympathetic to Pierre Bourdieu's field theory. It first outlines DeLanda's proposed new 'philosophy of society' ... and registers some scepticism as to its originality for sociology. It then introduces and responds to DeLanda's critique of Bourdieu. Rather than simply reject assemblage theory outright, however, **I draw on selected insights from DeLanda to push field theory in new directions. More specifically, I conceptualise the interplay of fields and assemblages and use notions of 'exteriority' and 'possibility space'** to help conceive individual plurality of social positioning and its effects for subjectivity and practice.

**One fact about it, stated without comment.** In its 56,617 characters: **Bourdieu 60, DeLanda 89, Deleuze 25, Hayles 0, Braidotti 0, transposition 0, cultural matrix 0.** The route from field theory to assemblage theory that exists in print runs from Bourdieu to DeLanda. It does not pass through Hayles.
