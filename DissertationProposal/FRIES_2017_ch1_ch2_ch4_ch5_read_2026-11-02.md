## 1. Chapter one: the term is Ingold's, and she says exactly what she added

**Ingold arrives here for the fourth time in three days, and this is the primary use of him in the folder.** Her source is a journal article rather than a book, which makes it the easiest of the four to get: **Ingold, T. (2012), "Toward an Ecology of Materials", *Annual Review of Anthropology* 41: 428, 429, 436.**

> "Matter-flow is how materiality always occurs in processes of flow and transformation, so art making that follows the matter-flow focuses on the '**active materials that compose the lifeworld**'. These processes of gathering materials in movement are '**a particular knotting together of the matter-flow … to bring the movements of our own being into close correspondence with those of its constituent materials**'."

> Ingold articulates the artist's ability to follow the materials as a distinct "**change of focus, away from the 'objectness' of things, to the material flows and formative processes wherein they come into being**" (436, which Ingold himself refers to Heidegger's "The Thing").

> "since matter is always in movement, in flux and variation, for artists to work with matter-flow means that **materials can only be followed**. Indeed, artists '**who follow the flow are, in effect, itinerants, guided by intuition in action**'."

**And then she states her own addition to the borrowed term, in one sentence, which is a model of terminological honesty:**

> "**My addition of the term sentience to Ingold's expression of matter-flow emphasises the aliveness and agency of materials always interacting with each other. This descriptor of sentience also implies a sense of empathy, compassion and intimacy.**"

**The sentence that answers the representation question from the materials side**, and it is a position on the same problem the metaphor cluster circles, moved from words to matter:

> "this acknowledges that **materials themselves are present: they exhibit themselves rather than representing something else**. That is to say the materials in these art processes are **not used as taciturn substrates fashioned to mirror abstract** [ideas]."

**The art history in this chapter is a real contribution and nothing in the plan carries it.** Her claim is a contrast between traditions:

> "In China and Japan, there is an ancient sculptural tradition of seeking nuanced understandings of the world's agency conveyed in matter itself, and humanity's humble place within these vast interconnections. **Following the material's lead, such sculptors explored the materiality of stone and rock, to reverently reveal its inner essence. In Europe the Greco-Roman art tradition was substantially different, predominantly used to depict the world as a mirror reflecting the human condition, rather than allowing materiality to speak for itself.**"

> "The most substantial shift away from this practice of imposing a separate human-centric narrative onto matter occurred in the 1960s with **Arte Povera** in Italy and **American-British Earthworks**. At the same time in Japan there was a '**raw material as art**' revival with **Mono-ha**. These art movements have influenced numerous contemporary ephemeral, site-responsive, **Bio Art and Eco Art** practices today."

**Arte Povera and Mono-ha are two movements the reading plan does not name**, and "raw material as art" is her phrase for the third. For a project with a historical-art lane and a 1960s problem this is a genealogy to take or to argue with.

## 2. Chapter two: co-creation, and three accounts of the artwork refused

The chapter opens by saying where the work does not come from, which is more precise than saying where it does:

> "the process of making an artwork is co-creative, which means **the artist does not make the artwork alone. Neither is the artwork already formed in the materials, nor pre-existing in the location, nor even conjured by the viewer. The co-creative engagement of art making is an entangled relationship connecting all of these into meshed layers of interbeing encountered in the sentient matter-flow of a particular place and time.**"

**Her new materialist ground is Coole and Frost, and the held text and the citation now line up**, since their introduction is the one recovered by OCR on 29 October. She quotes them twice from p. 1: "we experience its restlessness and intransigence even as we reconfigure and consume it", and "**our existence depends from one moment to the next on myriad micro-organisms and diverse higher species, on our own hazily understood bodily and cellular reactions and on pitiless cosmic motions, on the material artefacts and natural stuff that populate our environment**". And the target: the anthropocentric narrative "that posits **humans as the makers of the world and the world as a resource for human endeavours**".

With the consequence she draws from it: "**When all human activities, from breathing to using the internet and making art, are viewed as co-creations then they can no longer be simply interpreted as purely human processes.**"

**Interbeing is given with its translation, and the translation is "being in touch":**

> "Interbeing is a Buddhist term that describes this complex interconnected entanglement of the human with our material surroundings. **Thích Nhất Hạnh**, Vietnamese-French Buddhist monk and teacher, **coined the term 'interbeing' as a translation of *tiep hien*, which means being in touch with the reality of the world.** Interbeing means that there are many elements of the nonhuman combined to make the human."

**And his own sentence, which is the permeability claim in Buddhist terms:**

> "**you are made of non-you elements, and the non-you elements continue to be in you. If you remove** [them…]"

That sits directly beside the passage already pulled from *Meeting the Universe Halfway* at 393, "the other is not just in one's skin, but in one's bones, in one's belly, in one's heart, in one's nucleus, in one's past and future". **Two held texts, two traditions, the same claim.**

## 3. Chapter four: impermanence is present time, and *uji*

> "**The felt experience of touching, and being touched by, the vitality of existence is impermanence. Touch is an immediate sensation, a present time experience. It is more than just temporal; touch is impermanent.**"

> "**Our bodies are fluid, constantly touching, sensing, changing, breathing and leaking, interacting with sentient matter-flow. Understanding that our bodies themselves are present time**, as they are always already vital and changing, is central to comprehending that impermanence is time, and time is impermanence."

> "the present is always located in a particular place, and **place also has its own vibrations of time regardless of whether humans are there or not**."

**The source is Dōgen**, thirteenth-century Japan, read through **Joan Stambaugh** (p. 1) and placed by **Graham Parkes, "The Role of Rock in the Japanese Dry Landscape Garden", in *Reading Zen in the Rocks* (Chicago, 2000: 138)**, where Dōgen is ranked "with those of the greatest figures in the Western tradition, from Plato and Augustine to Hegel and Heidegger".

> Impermanence is "**being-time**", ***uji***, "expressed as the present moment of how we experience our embodied existence in the world". "All time is impermanent; whenever something happens, it '**is not in time, but is time itself. Time is the taking place of all beings**'."

**And the comparative claim, stated plainly:**

> "**In Buddhism eternity is right now and in this moment; it is not elsewhere or outside of existence. This understanding is a decisive contrast to the Abrahamic religions where eternity is always placed somewhere else, in the afterlife or heaven.**"

***Uji*, being-time, is a third account of time now held**, beside Barad's spacetimemattering and Hayles's Minkowski material, where "spacetime is the same for everyone". Three held texts, three accounts, none of them reducible to the others.

## 4. Chapter five: enchantment, and one sentence that contradicts Barad

The chapter runs on **Bennett, *The Enchantment of Modern Life: Attachments, Crossings, and Ethics*** (Princeton, p. 5).

> "**To be enchanted is to be drawn beyond one's human-centric attempts to control, to be touched by a sense of another time and space.**" Historically "enchantment relates to the return-to-nature passionate ideals of Romanticism and the persistent disenchantment of modernity."

> The occasions are ordinary and listed: "From the fascination of watching rivulets of water trickle away, to observing the strange ways that mushrooms grow, meditative walking, hugging trees, perceiving a plant sprout from concrete and **feeling the connective reality of breathing with the Earth**, art encounters focus and conjure enchantment as **both letting go and waking up**."

> "**It is this letting go of control, of analytical thinking structures, which is at the heart of touching of impermanence.**"

**The attentional description is practice writing and belongs with the skin passage from chapter three:**

> "Something is sensed in the atmosphere, almost subconsciously, as one approaches such an art encounter. **One's walking pace slows, breathing changes, eyes adjust to different light, ears strain to listen, the subtle smell and taste of the air alter. All the senses are holistically heightened, as the mind, body and spirit focus together in nonduality, synchronised in the present moment**, in the totality of being in 'the temporary suspension of chronological time and bodily movement … to participate in a **momentarily immobilizing encounter**'. One is transfixed and spellbound."

With Bennett: "**thoughts are brought to rest, even as the senses continue to operate, indeed, in high gear … The world comes alive as a collection of singularities.**"

**And then the sentence to notice, because it says the opposite of what the apparatus says:**

> "This way of being attentive within a state of awareness is sometimes termed **mindfulness**. It is important in co-creation, as **such exerted concentration brings everything together, and nothing is exiled.**"

**Barad's apparatus works the other way.** An agential cut is constitutive by exclusion: apparatuses "enact what matters and what is excluded from mattering", and the researcher is response-able for those constitutive exclusions. **Fries says concentration exiles nothing.** Two held texts, two accounts of what attention does with what it leaves out, and the difference is not a mistake in either: hers is a claim about the state of the maker, Barad's is a claim about the apparatus. Recorded here as it stands.

**One more line worth having, on where the work comes from:**

> "**The most creative impulses do not come just from your mind or well established tropes of logic, but rather stem from the beyond, from the unknown. Peak experiences of enchantment are accidents; you cannot order them to happen, merely be open to them and put yourself in their way.**"

## 5. The conclusion

> "**Touching impermanence cannot be grasped or held still; it can only occur in physical embodied momentary encounters.**"

> "**These mysterious links can be accessed through phenomenological touch as multisensory correlations, and this results in haptic thinking, the unity of mind-body attentiveness in present time.**"

> "Although such felt enchantments with sentient matter-flow do occur in the everyday, **art uniquely focuses one's sensory attention through direct engagement with materiality, time and place. This attests to art's being the primary process by which humans generate understandings about our commonalities, differences, diversities and our actual being in the world.**"

The Anthropocene frame is explicit and the diagnosis is short: "**Apathy and consumption are two of the main causes of the Anthropocene** as a perilous and escalating situation, so the need for individuals to develop **empathy, compassion and reconnection** is vital." And the new materialisms are placed as "one of many responses", taking "on the challenge of **de-centering the human**".
