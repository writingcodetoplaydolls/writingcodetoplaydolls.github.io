## 1. What the book is, as a whole

Not a collection of applications of new materialism. **A collection that argues with new materialism**, from inside it. Three claims run through all eleven chapters:

1. **There is no outside of Nature** (Kirby 2011: x, restated in ch. 1). Culture, language, mathematics and representation are not added to matter; they are what matter does. The title is a question the book answers yes to.
2. **The cut is internal.** What look like two domains in communication (biology and the social, gene and environment, organism and antigen, nature and culture) are one system differentiating itself. Barad's **intra-action** and **"cutting together-apart"** do the work, Kirby's **"internal torsions"** name the result.
3. **The corrective repeats the error.** Each chapter finds a new materialist or posthumanist argument that reinstalls the divide it set out to dissolve. Ch. 2 on Bennett and Massumi, ch. 6 on Saldanha, ch. 7 on Butler, Ahmed and Puar, ch. 8 on animal-studies supplementarity, ch. 11 on Braidotti. Kirby's own ch. 1 says why: critique that tidies is "more intent on managing a threat than exploring a question".

The headline figures: **Barad 161, Braidotti 137, bodies 405, nature 273, hormones 141, epigenetics 75, plasticity 61, skin 59, entangle 82, intra-action 27, apparatus 33.** And **permeab\* 0, art practice 0, Whitehead 0, Manning 0.**

## 2. The eleven chapters

### 1. Vicki Kirby, "Matter out of Place: 'New Materialism' in Review", pp. 1-25

A critical review of the field by its most sceptical insider. Kirby reads Wigner's "The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics in the Natural Sciences" (1960), where a statistician's friend refuses to believe that pi belongs in a population table, and where Wigner concludes that mathematics is "a wonderful gift which we neither understand nor deserve" (1960: 14). Her point: a physicist admits representation is a genuine puzzle and then forbids the question.

She then walks the field: Alaimo and Hekman's *Material Feminisms* (2008), Hekman at 97-98 on the linguistic turn skewing toward words; Coole and Frost's *New Materialisms* (2010), "our material lives are always culturally mediated, but they are not only cultural" (2010: 27); Dolphijn and van der Tuin's *New Materialism: Interviews & Cartographies* (2012), where she reads the interview titles against each other (DeLanda at 38, Meillassoux at 71, Barad's "Matter feels, converses, suffers, desires, yearns and remembers" at 48, Braidotti at 16). Her verdict: DeLanda, Meillassoux and Butler all still grant a circumscribed interiority to the human, so the turn has not turned.

**The three passages to take.**

### 2. Ashley Barnwell, "Method Matters: The Ethics of Exclusion", pp. 26-47

**The methodology chapter, and the one that bears hardest on 2.2.** Barnwell asks what posthumanism has to exclude in order to be inclusive, and answers with two cases.

**The turn against critique**, assembled with citations:

- Barad: "I am not interested in critique. In my opinion, critique is over-rated, over-emphasized, and over-utilized, to the detriment of feminism" (in Dolphijn and van der Tuin 2012: 49)
- Latour, "Compositionist Manifesto" (2010: 4-5): critique "ran out of steam"; with a hammer "you cannot repair, take care, assemble, reassemble, stitch together"; "what can be critiqued cannot be composed"; "the suspension of the critical impulse"
- Best and Marcus, *Representations* (2009: 1-2) on symptomatic reading and the hermeneutics of suspicion
- Rita Felski, *Uses of Literature* (2008: 1): "a shape of thought has grown old"; "what virtue remains in unmasking when we know full well what lies beneath the mask?"

**Barnwell's counter**: the hammer builds too, and the Berlin Wall's demolition was the first step in stitching Europe together. Then Bennett's *Vibrant Matter* (2010), whose own words she uses against it: "elide the question of the human" (120), "strategically elide" (ix), "I will shift from the language of epistemology to that of ontology" (3), the "fetishisation of the subject, the image, the word" (19), and the caution that demystification "should be used with caution and sparingly" (xiv). Barnwell: Bennett's argument **is** a critique, and it relies on the method it sets aside. **"Method is not without agency."** Method cannot "be outgrown and shed like an old skin".

Then Massumi: affect as what emotion loses, "the expression event, in favour of structure" (2002: 27); "structure is the place where nothing ever happens" (2002: 27); affect "is not ownable or recognizable, and is thus resistant to critique" (2002: 28); "judgemental reason is an extremely weak form of thought, precisely because it is so sure of itself" (in Zournazi 2002: 220-1). And **Claire Hemmings's** reply, that we are left with affect as "something scientists can detect the loss of (in the anomaly), [and] social scientists and cultural critics cannot interpret, but philosophers can imagine" (2005: 563).

**Lauren Berlant** is the chapter's best ally: she "resist[s] idealising, even implicitly, any program of better thought or reading", and warns that "the overvaluation of reparative thought is both an occupational hazard and part of a larger overvaluation of a certain mode of virtuously intentional, self-reflective personhood" (2011: 124), against Sedgwick's paranoid/reparative distinction (1997).

**Note 2 is a reading-methods bibliography in one paragraph**, and it is the most useful single note in the book for 2.2: Bewes's "reading with the grain" (2010), Sedgwick's "reparative reading" (1997), Best and Marcus's "surface reading" (2009), Thrift's "non-representational theory" (2007), Warner's "uncritical reading" (2004), Ablow's "affective reading" (2010), Law's **"mess as method"** (2004), and **fictocriticism** (Taussig 2010; Stewart 2007; Muecke 2010).

### 3. Florence Chiew, "Sensory Substitution: The Plasticity of the Eye/I", pp. 48-69

Covered in full in `KIRBY_2017_collection_close_read.md`. In brief: Bach-y-Rita's TVSS, **a camera whose image is delivered to the skin** or to electrode arrays on the tongue; users report the sensation leaving the skin and relocating in front of them; the device enters the corporeal schema. Merleau-Ponty's chiasm, intercorporeity, and the phantom limb. The fourth reading of superposition, and the only refusal of it: "The two parts are total parts and yet are not superposable" (1968: 134), so Chiew substitutes **intra-modal** for cross-modal plasticity. The etymology of **deficiency** as *de* + *facere*, an unmaking rather than a lack.

Cites Kirby 2011 at **119**, and her own earlier paper, **Chiew, F. (2012), "Neuroplasticity as An Ecology of Mind", *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 19(11-12), 32-54**, at 40, 48 and 51, including the blind man's cane: "the cane is the hand, or that the cane is the eye, and even that the ground is the eye!"

### 4. Michelle Jamieson, "Allergy as the Puzzle of Causality", pp. 70-89

Two things here, and the first is new since the earlier read.

**The 1921 self-experiment.** Carl Prausnitz and Heinz Küstner used themselves as guinea pigs. Serum from Küstner, highly allergic to certain fish, was injected into Prausnitz's skin; a day later fish was administered to Prausnitz locally, and "a typical wheal and erythema hypersensitivity reaction" appeared at the site and persisted **over four weeks** (via Silverstein 1989: 226). They named the transferred agent **reagin**; Teruka and Kimishige Ishizaka identified it as IgE in 1966 (Silverstein 1989: 227).

**One body's sensitivity, carried into another body's skin, where it lived for a month.** For the permeability material this is the closest documented thing to a deliberate transcorporeal experiment, and it is the founding experiment of allergy.

**The skin prick test** as the grid printed on skin: inner forearm cleaned, a gridded stamp of ten to twenty squares imprinted, drops of glycerinated allergen extract at the centre of each, a prick through each drop with a lancet, saline as negative control and histamine as positive, ten to fifteen minutes, a wheal of 3mm or more read as positive (Walls 1997: 13). Jamieson's reading: the grid "rationally and numerically organises" the response, infers that each welt is a separate event, and **manufactures the circumstances it measures**, because every encounter with an allergen can itself sensitise. Clinicians already know the welts contaminate each other: "if there is insufficient space between injection sites, large reactions can reflexively cause positive reactions in adjacent sites" (Walls 1997: 13).

**The inheritance finding**, which matters for the microchimerism thread. Burrows, Martinez and colleagues found that parental **asthma**, not parental IgE, predicts asthma in offspring: "no statistically significant association between prevalence of asthma in children and serum IgE levels in their parents when the mother or the father did not have asthma" (Martinez 1997: S119-S120). Jamieson: "the offspring inherits and materially manifests a genetic predisposition that has already been (or will be) awoken at a different point in time, in a different body (or bodies)", so "any one instance of asthma must necessarily be caused by all other instances", and the asthmatic becomes "a biographical index". Also: antibody and antigen are "each a corporealised response to the call or provocation of the other, a call which has yet to take place."

Note 2 locates IgE's defence system "under the skin, under the epithelial surfaces of the airways ..., and in the submucosa of the gut" (Janeway et al. 2005: 521). The sites of entry.

### 5. Rebecca Oxley, "Pregnant Men: Paternal Postnatal Depression and a Culture of Hormones", pp. 90-109

Paternal postnatal depression runs at roughly the same incidence as maternal, about 10-13%, and yet is explained as psycho-social while the maternal version is explained hormonally. Oxley's move is to take fathers' endocrinology seriously.

| | |
|---|---|
| **The hormonal finding** | In fathers, testosterone falls and oestrogen rises during a partner's pregnancy and stays so for months after birth; vasopressin rises analogously to oxytocin in mothers (Kim and Swain 2007: 42). **Storey et al. 2000: 90**: men show significant pre-, peri- and postnatal changes in testosterone, cortisol and prolactin "with patterns of change paralleling those found in women", and "hormone correlations between partners suggest that communication within couples is related to the physiological changes the men experience" |
| **Couvade** | Csordas 1993: 146: the expectant father "experiences bodily sensations attuned to those of his pregnant mate", and the literature either exoticises it as "a primitive charade" or pathologises it as "psychosomatic overidentification". Fathers with marked expressions (growing breasts, enlarged stomach, labour pains) may have higher prolactin (Storey et al. 2000: 92) |
| **Her term** | Against Gail Weiss's **intercorporeality** (1995: 5), Oxley proposes **intra-corporeality**, "a mutual-constitution of entangled biographical corporealities", and **intra-corporeal synchrony**. Her conclusion: "the baby is as much inside the father as it is the mother", and "fathers, too, may be(come) pregnant" |
| **Etymology, again** | "etymologically hormone means to excite or provoke" (Roberts, cited in Irni 2013: 43) |
| **The law** | The NSW Infanticide Law (Crimes Act 22A) and the British Infanticide Act define infanticide by the biological mother suffering the effects of childbirth, so "there is little space for fathers to access the infanticide offence or defence". Moscucci 1993: 31 on the leniency as an application of the biological principle |

Also: Grosz 1994: 21 on "the hole in nature that allows cultural seepage"; Celia Roberts 2007: 21 on hormones as messengers, "if hormones are messengers of sex, what is the message? And to whom is the message sent?"; Sari Irni (2013) reading hormones through Barad; Csordas's **"somatic modes of attention"** (1993: 138) and "Speaking is a kind of sonorous touching; language is tissue in the flesh of the world" (2008: 118); Kirby 1997 at 4, 5, 59, 77 and Kirby 2008: 230, "nature reads, writes, articulates itself . . . For any first-person method about distress, Frank states the problem and Oxley states one way past it.

### 6. Noela Davis, "Material Culture: Epigenetics and the Molecularisation of the Social", pp. 110-133

**The chapter that connects the permeability material to inheritance, and the one with the most direct empirical claim in the book.**

Stigma is transmitted biologically across generations. Modin (2003; 2008; 2009) studied children and grandchildren of people born out of wedlock in early twentieth-century Sweden and found a correlation between the grandparents' illegitimacy and **increased risk of ischaemic heart disease in the subsequent two generations**. Lund et al. (2006) found the same for children born to unmarried mothers in Denmark in 1953, with a cumulative effect across non-married generations. Both teams argue the difference cannot be explained by material deprivation alone, and name **social stigma, moral condemnation and exclusion** as contributing factors (Modin 2003: 493, 496; Modin et al. 2008: 823-4; Lund et al. 2006: 499).

The mechanism, as Davis assembles it: stress hormones (adrenaline, noradrenaline, adrenal glucocorticoids) promote inflammatory pathways, insulin resistance and hypertension, which can manifest as diabetes and heart disease (Meaney 2001: 1163); the same elevated levels alter the methylation status of genes, reducing glucocorticoid receptor expression, raising hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal function, producing **heightened stress reactivity** often accompanied by **hypervigilance**, "a constant state of being on guard to avert the possibility of stigmatisation or discrimination" (McGowan et al. 2009: 342), with sustained change in the expression of stress-mediating genes (Meaney 2001: 1170). The literature calls the extra load **"minority stress"** (Meyer 2003: 675; Williams et al. 2010: 81).

Meaney calls being born into a stressful environment **"anticipatory development"** (2001: 1182): before individuals have experienced the environment, "they have in their inheritance messages from their environment already signalling their life conditions to them." Davis reads this with **Kirby's "mysterious clairvoyance"** (2011: 9), a preparedness to receive a message that has not yet arrived and has no addressee yet, and with **Barad's hauntology** (2010: 240), "cutting together-apart" (2012b: 32, 46), "spacetimemattering" (2012b: 32) and "differentiated indivisibility" (2010: 253).

Her target is Saldanha (2006: 12), who holds that language can circumscribe what a stigmatised person can do but "does not alter the biological expression of a phenotype". Davis's answer: the epigenetic evidence says it does.

**Note 4 is the line to keep**: epigeneticists' own phrasings, how the social environment "gets into the mind" (Toyokawa et al. 2012: 68) or "under the skin" (Galea et al. 2011: 400), which Davis reads as the division surviving inside the research that disproves it. Her formulation: "the social is always already 'under' the skin."

**Note 3 is the one for the bioelectricity thread**: epigenetic research suggests that physical forces including **electric fields, shear stresses, mechano-transduction and pressure**, which encompass sound pressure waves, can affect gene expression (Davies 2013a: 38-9; 2013b). Also Merleau-Ponty 2002 on empiricism as a "freezing of being" (60, 63), and that it "actually falsifies the world" (28).

### 7. Xin Liu, "Racialised Visual Encounters", pp. 134-152

**The first-person chapter.** Liu, a Chinese immigrant in Finland, writes her own body into the argument: learning the Finnish rolling r, the alveolar trill, by biting a pen crosswise and pushing her tongue against it, where "it was difficult to tell apart the fleshy soft surface of the tongue and the cold, hard plastic body of the pen". Then a classroom scene at an evening school in Turku: she is asked to produce the trill, feels the other students' attention, succeeds by surprise, and the teacher says "Chinese cannot pronounce that, which can be sometimes very problematic for others to understand."

She reads it three ways in sequence, through Butler, then Ahmed, then Puar, and finds each insufficient, then through Chiew's sensory substitution: "the tongue sees and hears through tonguing and being tongued". **"Can the tongue see the approaching of the teacher and the judgemental gaze of other students?"**

### 8. Jacqueline Dalziell, "Microbiology as Sociology: The Strange Sociality of Slime", pp. 153-178

**The practice-and-evidence chapter, and the one with original interview material.**

*Physarum polycephalum*, an acellular slime mould, unicellular and multicellular at once, the "many headed slime". Cut a plasmodium in half and you get two organisms that behave differently; put them back together and they fuse. "one could argue that there has only ever been one slime mould: spatially plural, yet ontologically singular." Its life cycle has no beginning.

Two experiments, read closely:

- **Latty and Beekman 2011**: comparative valuation. Offered 3% oatmeal in darkness and 5% in bright light, Physarum, being photophobic, showed no preference. Add a clearly inferior third option, 1% in shadow, and it switched to overwhelmingly choosing the 3%. The decoy effect, as in Tversky 1969. "it is remarkable that *P. polycephalum*, which belongs to an entirely different kingdom of life and lacks a central nervous system, uses the same comparative decision-making processes as do neurologically sophisticated organisms" (2011: 312)
- **Reid et al. 2012** (PNAS 17490-17492): the U-shaped trap. Physarum escaped within 120 hours using its own extracellular slime trails in 96% of cases; on agar pre-coated with slime its success fell to **33%** and the time spent re-traversing explored areas rose almost tenfold. So the slime is an **"externalized spatial memory system"**, and Physarum "constructs a map of its environment before constructing a solution"

**The terminology finding.** Across this literature "programming" replaces cognition, "signalling" replaces language, "anticipation" replaces forethought; "simple", "primitive" and "lower" recur; every intelligence word sits in inverted commas. Adamatzky: Physarum's intelligence "is not higher than [the] intelligence of a stone rolling down a hill".

**Tanya Latty's interview (pers. comm., 14 September 2012)** is the most useful primary material in the book:

- "Like I never use the word 'cognition' . . . we even had debates about 'problem solving' and 'decision making' . . . We've had arguments about whether you can have decision making without a brain."
- "I would never say they were intelligent on paper, and part of that is because I know it wouldn't work. But the other part is I don't know what I really think."
- On publishing: getting into *Nature*, *Science* or *Cell* "usually tends to help if you can have something in there . . . that says 'why is this so important beyond just being kind of cool'."
- **"I'm not sure that they are or that they're not. I don't know that anybody is."**
- And on how her own view changed: "I think you have to work with the things to kind of get to that point . . . I even think . . . plants have behaviour and I remember thinking, 'Plant behaviour, that's stupid'. You just get a different opinion when you start working with them and when you are actually the one setting up the experiment . . . I know how they behaved, I know that I didn't lie, I watched them go in one direction or another, I watched them do it."

**That last passage is a practice-as-research claim made by a working biologist**: the knowledge came from setting up the experiment and watching, not from reading, and it changed what she thought was possible. For the practice-as-research sections this is a better witness than most of the methodology literature, because she is not arguing for the position, she is reporting it reluctantly.

Dalziell then reads Durkheim beside the slime: *Rules of Sociological Method* on social facts as "external to the individual" and "endowed with coercive power" (1996: 2, 4, 9); "How can we belong entirely to ourselves, and entirely to others at one and the same time?" (1973: 152); "something else in us besides ourselves stimulates us to act" (1973: 153); **"each individual contains a part, but the whole is found in no one"** (1953: 26); and the footnote that undoes it, "we do not believe that there is a precise point where the individual ends and the social begins" (1897/2006: 347).

The sting in the tail: the maze-solving passage at the end, "gliding smoothly through the labyrinth at speeds of up to 10mm per second", is **a single drop of oil** (Lagzi et al. 2010: 1198), not Physarum. "If slime can cognise, can a drop of oil? Can a particle of light?"

Note 4 is a small history worth having: **"cell sociology"**, coined by Alexis Carrel in 1931, developed by the embryologist Rosine Chandebois in the 1970s, "development is a 'social' phenomenon", and "cells are ontologically 'transformed by their social interactions'" (Reynolds, forthcoming: 7, 18). Also "cell sociobiology", "socio-microbiology", "socio-bacteriological".

Note 9 gives **Barad, "Nature's Queer Performativity", *Kvinder, Køn og forskning / Women, Gender and Research* 1-2 (2012): 25-53**, on the animate/inanimate distinction as "the most routinely undisputed distinction within the humanities", and on refusing to begin analysis with that cut.

### 9. Astrida Neimanis, "Nature Represents Itself: Bibliophilia in a Changing Climate", pp. 179-198

**The art chapter, and the one that opens in Albuquerque.**

Its first scene: "At dusk in January, along the muddy-red waters on the Rio Grande near Albuquerque, New Mexico, a daughter wades in a river, fishing with her father. A piece of ice floats by." The father says it looks like an open book. The daughter decides the seed script in it is "a language of the land, one that the birds can read from their cottonwood perches" (Irland 2011).

**Basia Irland's ice books.** A US-based eco-artist freezes water with local communities, carves it into book shapes, open or closed, some paperback-sized and some for lecterns, embeds them with native seeds, seed pods or krill, and launches them into the rivers to reseed riparian zones. The essay is called "Receding/Reseeding". "The seeds transcribe an international ecological text." The books melt: "Instead of dust to dust, here we have water to water."

**Roni Horn's *Vatnasafn / Library of Water*** (Stykkishólmur, Iceland, in a former library, in the town where Iceland's weather was first recorded in 1845): twenty-four floor-to-ceiling glass columns of water collected by hand from Icelandic glaciers; a rubber floor engraved with Icelandic and English weather-feeling words; the book projects "The Weather Reports You" and "You Are the Weather"; a window onto the bay. Horn calls a repository of water "completely absurd" but "hideously, painfully apt" (2007: 66), and asks "When you talk of the water, are you talking of yourself, or the weather?" (2013). Bloom and Glasberg (2012: 131-134) note the columns mimic **ice core samples**, "the major form of glaciological research", and that they create "an affective possibility for the data", letting visitors "recognise themselves in the data, or, as data".

Her two coinages:

- **"situated literacy"**, explicitly after Haraway's "situated knowledges" (1988), which is held in this folder. "No translation is adequate, no text is perfectly captured, but some books and some libraries are particularly resistant to our human reading skills . . . the limits I invoke here are not ontological, but epistemological and ethical."
- **"we are all bodies of water"**, and the **hydrocommons**: "every living body on earth is at least 50 per cent water"; "our saltwater blood, our intercellular fluids, our humours and biles and various other lubrications"; "the promiscuous scribbling of our bodily fluids, or the protective imprint of an amniotic bath, these rivers write us, too, in a slow-moving cursive script."

Barad on representationalism, with pages: 46, 48, 49 (Joseph Rouse on the Cartesian hangover), 50 ("realism without representationalism"), 56 ("realness does not necessarily involve 'thingness'"), and 393 twice on ethics: "Ethics is therefore not about right response to a radically exterior/ised other, but about responsibility and accountability for the lively relationalities of becoming of which we are a part."

And the line that matters for divergence as method, via Sandilands (1999: 180) and Alaimo (2010: 23): the perpetual failure of representation **"is not a problem to be overcome, but a crucial part of a radical politics whose promise lies in its very unfinishedness."**

### 10. Will Johncock, "Climate Change, Socially Synchronised: Are We Really Running out of Time?", pp. 199-222

Takes the phrase "running out of time" in climate discourse literally and asks what theory of time it assumes. Traces it through Denis Hayes, Donald Brown (2010), Hansen's *Storms of My Grandchildren* (2009: xi), and back to the geoscientist Albert Engel's "Time and the Earth" (*American Scientist*, 1969: 460), "man, if not the Earth, is running out of time".

**Barbara Adam** supplies the diagnosis: "while the spatial dimension has been brought to the fore in a number of disciplines, the temporal equivalent has stayed implicit" (1993: 399); "we are running out, not of resources, but of time" (1993: 401); "time is everywhere, yet it eludes us. It is so deeply implicated in our existences that it is almost invisible" (1990: 9). Elizabeth Grosz: time as a "silent accompaniment, a shadowy implication" (1999: 1). Johncock's point: we treat space as alterable and time as not, which is Newton's "true, absolute time" that "flows equably without relation to anything external" (1978: 77).

Against it he uses **George Mead, *The Philosophy of the Present*** (2002): the past is "revocable" (36) and is remade as "a different past" (35) by each new present; "it is 'what it was' of the past 'that changes'" (37); against "an ultimate unchangeable past spread behind us in its entirety" (58).

**Two passages for the permeability thread.**

- **Merleau-Ponty**, the double touch: touching one hand with the other, the subject touches and is touched at once, so "the subject is a part of the world it touches at the same time as the touched world is a part of the subject" (1968: 133); perception as "formed in the midst of the world and as it were in the things" (134); "I do not see it from the depths of nothingness, but from the midst of itself" (113); subjectivity as a "chiasmic" production (130-155)
- **Heidegger, *Zollikon Seminars*** (2001: 86): the body is not "a thing which stops with the skin", and "when pointing with my finger . . . I [as body] do not end at my fingertips. Where then is the limit of the body? 'Each body is my body'. As such, the proposition [bodily limit] is nonsensical." Sartre had complained that Heidegger wrote "only six lines on the body in the whole of *Being and Time*" (2003: 231, 405), and Heidegger answers him here

### 11. Peta Hinton, "A Sociality of Death: Towards a New Materialist Politics and Ethics of Life Itself", pp. 223-247

**A sustained critique of Braidotti.** Relevant because the proposal uses Braidotti 80 times, and because Hinton co-edited the *Rhizomes* 30 Barad issue already in `QUANTUM_PERMEABILITY_lens.md` with Karin Sellberg.

Hinton works through Braidotti on Zoe and Bios, against Agamben: Zoe as what sovereign power reduces the subject to; the "naturalised other (earth, plants and animals)" alongside "the sexual other (woman), the ethnic other (the native)" (2006: 138). Death as a "zero institution" in Lévi-Strauss's sense, "the empty shape of all possible time" (2006: 147). "In and through many deaths, bios-zoe lives on" (2006: 150), on an "and/and/and" scheme rather than "either/or". From *The Posthuman* (2013): "one's view on death depends on one's assumptions about Life" (131); "death as a constitutive event is behind us" (132); it is "the event that has always already taken place at the level of consciousness" (133); "the inhuman within us, which frees us into life" (134).

**And from *Nomadic Subjects* (1994)**, which is the book needed for "Mothers, Monsters, and Machines": the body as "raw material, destined to be socialised into purposeful productivity" (45), bound into "the commerce of living bodies" (52); the bioscientist as "the very prototype of the instrumental intellectual" (47), under whose gaze living organisms "lose all reference to the human shape and to the specific temporality of the human being" (47); "All reference to death disappears in the discourse about 'biopower'" (47). Hinton's gloss: biopower denies death in order to master life, and so produces "a sanitised, technologised and alien relationship with bodies, for example, with reproduction and maternity". **That is the monster-and-mother thread, from the same book, with page numbers.**

**Hinton's objection**: Braidotti's affirmative reading privileges an "ontology of presence" (2010: 202) and displaces rather than reworks negativity; and in locating the recognition of death "at the level of consciousness", she reinstates a materiality/ideation split and returns the human as the proper ethical agent.

Her alternative is **Barad on nothingness**:

- **Barad, K. (2012a), *What is the Measure of Nothingness? Infinity, Virtuality, Justice*, dOCUMENTA 13, Hatje Cantz.** Indeterminacy as "key not only to the existence of matter, but also to its non-existence, or rather it is the key to the play of non/existence" (15); "un-ending dynamism" (11); "no matter" (12); "radical openness" at "the core of mattering" (18); finitude and infinite possibility "infinitely threaded through one another" (19); **"the possibilities for justice-to-come reside in every morsel of finitude"** (19)
- **Barad, K. (2012b), "On Touching, The Inhuman That Therefore I Am", *differences*.** The long passage at 216: "But what if the point is not to widen the bounds of inclusion to let everyone and everything in? What if it takes sensing the abyss, the edges of the limits of 'inclusion' and 'exclusion' before the binary of inside/outside . . . can be seriously troubled?" And "responsibility extends to the insensible as well as the sensible" (217); the inhuman defined at 222 as "that which holds open the space of the liveliness of indeterminacies that bleed through the cuts"

**The clearest statement of the Heisenberg/Bohr distinction in the folder** is here, from Barad 2007: 18-20: for Heisenberg the observer cannot know the behaviour or position of matter prior to experimentation; for Bohr "the nature of matter is itself indeterminate and dynamically contingent", and "there are no pre-measurable properties of matter that precede their experimentation."

Also **Kirby, V. (2009), "Tracing Life: La Vie La Mort", *CR: The New Centennial Review* 9(1), 107-26**: "différance is Life Itself" (118); "an epigenesis of infinite mutation" (118); "death would be internal to the very possibility of an entity's being itself, not simply at its birth, but throughout its ongoing re-production/othering of itself" (120); and **"a superposition of states that does not divide into the either/or of mortality/immortality"** (121), "the torsional differential that is becoming" (121).

And **Kirby, V. (1999), "Human Nature", *Australian Feminist Studies* 14(29), 19-29**, on error as *errans / errare*, to wander, where an error can "always infect its correction" (28).

Plus Derrida 1991: 26-7 via Cary Wolfe's *What is Posthumanism?* (2010: xxi-xxii): "the living present springs forth out of its nonidentity with itself"; and Claire Colebrook, *Death of the Posthuman* (2014: 13), on Agamben's **impotentiality**, "our essential capacity not to actualize that which would distinguish us as human".
