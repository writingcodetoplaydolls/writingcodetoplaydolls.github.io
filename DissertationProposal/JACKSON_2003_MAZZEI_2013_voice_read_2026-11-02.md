# PART ONE: JACKSON 2003

## 1. What kind of text it says it is

She states the form before the argument. "The challenge of this mapping lies in the limits of the linear space of graphic writing, for voice in feminist qualitative research does not have a lineal, hierarchical history with simple and striated originating, culminating, or terminating points."

So the five sections are "irruptions", not stages, and she instructs the reader directly: "Imagine, then, as you read, that this is a flat, spatial text, spreading out and forming a rhizome. Read the rhizomatic 'irruptions' of multiple conceptions of voice in feminist qualitative research as 'lines of flight,' as things popping up in the middle, not preceding but exceeding." She says the first irruption is not an origin and the last is not a terminus, and closes the introduction: "though you may enter it wherever you like."

**Her method, in one sentence.** "Therefore, the mapping I offer in this paper does not seek to reveal what voice means in feminist qualitative research but what it does, how voice functions, what enables it to function in certain ways, and what it produces."

**The constraint she puts on her own invented term**, which is the most careful thing in the article: "rhizovocality is only possible within the territory it deterritorializes. New lines of flight are always connected to what produced them." She takes the principle from Derrida: "Working the limits, in this Derridean sense, means to transgress in the middle of things and reconfigure them, not to transcend or be beyond them. 'One never lives elsewhere,' Derrida (1981/1972, p. 12) writes."

## 2. Irruption one: emancipatory feminist research

The position she is mapping, not holding. Harding: we live in a culture which "systematically silences and devalues the voices of women". Reinharz on the founding awareness that women had "the right to criticize the accepted body of knowledge and the right to create knowledge". DeVault: "women's voices hold meanings worth discovering". Reinharz again: feminist ethnographers use interviews to "make women's voices audible". Mies on group interviews helping women "overcome their structural isolation in their families and to understand that their individual sufferings have social causes". Gilligan treating voice as "the core of the self . . . a powerful psychological instrument and channel, connecting inner and outer worlds". Munhall's collection: "Using many women's voices . . . the many narratives are authentic and faithful to the experience of women."

The epistemological claim she identifies in it: "According to this view, women's standpoints are not equally true to men's but, by virtue of their sexual difference, are more true."

**And then the sentence that matters for the metaphor thread in this folder.** She names the whole vocabulary as metaphor: "Metaphors of voice and silence permeate these texts which signify women's exclusion from the production of knowledge and the absence of women's experiences in social, cultural, and political spheres: 'finding a voice,' 'coming to voice,' 'creating safe spaces for voice,' 'centering on voice,' 'voicing resistance,' 'losing voice,' 'giving up voice,' 'bring voices to the center.'"

That is one of only two places the word metaphor appears in the article. The other is endnote 1, in section 6 below, where she refuses the term for her own practice.

## 3. Irruption two: the dilemmas of power

Wolf doubts that co-authoring and polyvocality change anything, "since the academic feminist maintains control over the entire research process, from the topic selection to the data representation", and researchers "may remain transparent in their own silent presence and power". Behar: even voices presented "in the raw" become "a translation of power". Lather: researchers "describe and inscribe when representing data".

Fine's critique is quoted at most length, and it is the one closest to a usable test. Some feminist ethnographic texts appear to let the Other speak while the researcher hides "just under the covers of those marginal, if now 'liberated' voices", and appear "as if they were constructed without . . . as if researchers were simply vehicles for transmission, with no voices of their own". Fine's charge is not that voices should not be used: "she argues that participants' voices offer a decoy when feminist researchers rely on them as innocent, monolithic, or singular, as if the voices say it all." And Jackson's gloss: "Denying the complex, contradictory 'hard-to-code' voices makes trouble for creating borders around conclusive arguments."

Spivak on the mechanism: researchers use the Other as "simply a name that provides the alibi for erasing the investigator's intervention into the construction and representation of the narrative."

**Alcoff's four interrogatory practices**, which Jackson lists in italics and which are the article's only actual procedure. Questioning our desire to master and dominate as the speaker. Interrogating our positionalities and how that bears on what we say, "going beyond mere autobiographical disclaimers". Being accountable and responsible for what we say, "which involves being open and attentive to criticism". And analysing the discursive and material effects of our words, looking at "where the speech goes and what it does there".

## 4. Irruption three: talking back

hooks, quoted at length, on what speech about the Other does: "no need to hear your voice when I can talk about you better than you can speak about yourself. No need to hear your voice. Only tell me about your pain. I want to know your story. And then I will tell it back to you in a new way. Tell it back to you in such a way that it has become mine, my own. Re-writing you, I write myself anew. I am still author, authority. I am still the colonizer, the speaking subject, and you are now at the center of my talk."

Lugones and Spelman: the demand for women's voices to be heard "is a call for a particular type of woman's voice", and white feminist researchers who claim to speak for women of colour "can only produce violently incomplete representations". Their condition for hearing is quoted exactly, and Jackson calls it "hard listening": it requires "to come to terms with having [their] world thoroughly criticized and scrutinized from the point of view of those who have been harmed by it, having important concepts central to it dismissed, being seen as of no consequence [to us] except as an object of mistrust." Jackson's gloss: "This hard listening (which resonates with Alcoff's call) demands an unlearning of privilege, authority, and knowledge, an openness to and acceptance of severe criticism, and a profound self-questioning and circumspection."

**Collins revising herself across two editions**, which is the clearest instance in either article of a scholar withdrawing a position. In the 1990 preface: "This book reflects one stage in my ongoing struggle to regain my voice. . . . So the voice that I now seek is both individual and collective, personal and political." By 2000, Jackson records, "Collins aims for more fluidity in her definitions of knowledge, consciousness, empowerment, and voice. She emphasizes power relations in her analysis and is less preoccupied with coming to voice, since, as a result of power relations, voice is unstable and contingent." Collins's new sentence: "My concern now lies in finding effective ways to use the voice I have claimed while I have it."

Trinh on being spoken for: "In trying to tell something, a woman is told. . . . You who understand the dehumanization of forced removal-relocation-reeducation-redefinition, the humiliation of having to falsify your own reality, your voice, you know. And often cannot say it. You try and keep on trying to unsay it, for if you don't, they will not fail to fill in the blanks on your behalf, and you will be said." And on the demand for authenticity: Third World women are expected to paint themselves "thick with authenticity" and offer something different and exotic, "but not so different that it defies what White women have made Third World women to be." Inauthenticity is "condemned as a loss of origins".

Spivak: the answer to "Can the subaltern speak?" is "an unequivocal 'no'", because "the colonized subaltern subject is irretrievably heterogeneous" and the desire for a homogeneous Other keeps the "subaltern woman as mute as ever". Her instruction, which Jackson italicises: white feminists must "learn to speak to (rather than listen to or speak for) the historically muted subject of the subaltern woman."

On American Indian research specifically she cites Jojola, Thornton and Swisher, the last advocating "a separatist view" and calling "for an end to Whites researching Indians". hooks's phrase for the alternative impulse is "eating the other", a commodification offering differences "as new dishes to enhance the white palate".

## 5. Irruptions four and five: Derrida, then Lather and Britzman

**Four.** Derrida against phonocentrism and the metaphysics of presence. "Metaphysics privileges voice and therefore the speaking, fully conscious subject who utters pure and full meaning." The drive she names is Derrida's "absolute will-to-hear-oneself-speak". Against it: différance, the trace, "the hinge (discontinuity) of the sign marks the impossibility of full speech, consciousness, and meaning". The image she keeps is Derrida's: voice as a labyrinth "which includes in itself its own exits. . . . Contrary to what phenomenology has tried to make us believe: the thing itself always escapes."

The parallel move on experience, via Scott, de Lauretis and Fuss. Scott rejects experience "as uncontestable evidence and as an originary point of explanation", and wants it redefined as "at once always already an interpretation and something that needs to be interpreted. What counts as experience is neither self-evident nor straightforward; it is always contested, and always therefore political." De Lauretis: "subjectivity is an ongoing construction, not a fixed point of departure or arrival . . . it is the effect of experience." Fuss: "experience is never as unified, as knowable, as universal, and as stable as we presume it to be."

Her summary, and the question she takes out of it: "In poststructuralism, there is no prelinguistic experience or meaning that is 'out there' waiting to be expressed by our innocent voices." So: "If there cannot be an essentialist search for lost origins, giving voice to women, how can research be 'made to mean?'"

**Five.** Her two worked examples. Lather and Smithies's *Troubling the Angels* (1997): "our focus was on voice as limit", a "theory of deferral", a split-text format that "refuses to smother the women's voices with the researcher's authorial presence", stitching together "seemingly disconnected narrative worlds, angelology, email and journal entries, letters, poems, interview transcripts, academic talk about theory and method, and autobiography". Lather's formula for what translation is: "a knowing disruption", an acknowledgement that "faithful reproduction is false". And the instruction Jackson quotes at length: "Assume narrator as both unreliable and bearer of knowledge in a way that attends to the price subjects pay to tell the truth about themselves."

Britzman's critical voice: not negation of voice but the view that voices "only partially tell stories and express meaning since they are bound by the 'exigencies of what can and cannot be told. . . . Narratives of lived experience are always selective, partial, and in tension.'" And the disclaimer Britzman attaches, which Jackson keeps: this is not "to assert a dreary relativism that all meanings are equal, accurate, just, or empowering, or that communication is either impossible or a mere matter of individual thought". Britzman's longer passage: "In poststructuralist versions, meaning is unruly. Despite our best authorial intentions, language cannot deliver what it promises: unmediated access to 'the real.' . . . This is not, however, to assert that 'the real' does not exist. Rather, the real must be continually imagined and articulated."

**Silence, treated as agency rather than as lack.** "Rather than giving in to the humanist impulse to fill up silence with voices, feminist qualitative researchers can resist this urge and point to the silence, critique it, and expose how discourses govern silence." Visweswaran "goes as far as to refuse polyvocality as a remedy", and her term is "decisive silence", "a refusal to speak that goes beyond 'what cannot be said'", where "a refusal to speak is resisting the power relations between feminist researchers and women subjects".

**Haraway's Trickster**, and the position on failure. Interviewers "give up mastery but keep searching for fidelity, knowing all the while that [they] will be hoodwinked . . . [this] makes room for surprises and ironies at the heart of all knowledge production". Visweswaran's Trickster "trips on, but is not tripped up by, the seductions of a feminism that promises what it may never fully deliver". And Jackson's own sentence: "Recognizing failure from the start is not paralysis. Visweswaran defends failure as possibility."

**Ellsworth's objection to the easy fix**, which is the argument for needing a new term at all: simply pluralising voices "oftentimes fails to consider the rich texture of vocality. To only pluralize voice remains focused on units of voice rather than dimensions of voice."

## 6. What rhizovocality is, in her own definition

Two halves, each sourced. "*Rhizo*, a prefix I borrow from Deleuze and Guattari's (1987) image of the rhizome, captures the heterogeneity of vocality in a spatial figuration, accentuating its connection to other things through its very diversity. *Vocality*, in music theory, emphasizes the performative dimension of voice, its expressive power, its tensions of dissonant counterpoint, and its variations on thematic connections; it challenges our attention and demands deep concentration if we are to hear its nuances."

What it is for: "offers a vision of performative utterances that consist of unfolding and irrupting threads", compelling researchers "to listen for texture and subtlety within and among discordant, muted, and harmonious voices, including their own."

Why it cannot claim authenticity: "Without origins, rhizomes cannot claim authenticity; neither do voices, which, as adventitious themselves, rupture into lines of flight, deterritorializing any demand for coherence or stability." And it does not make the voices better: "In their becoming, vocalizations are not reaching for a more full, complete, coherent status; rather, they are opening up territory, spreading out."

The last line of the article is not hers: "The question is not: is it true? But: does it work. (Massumi, 1987, p. xv)"

**Endnote 1, which is the sharpest statement on terminology in this folder.** "I prefer the use of *figuration*, as opposed to *metaphor*, since figurations foreground contradiction and disjunction and are useful as 'tools of rigorous confusion that jettison clarity in favor of the unintelligible' (St. Pierre, 1997a, p. 281)."

Endnote 3 gives hooks's own definition of talking back: "speaking as an equal to an authority figure . . . daring to disagree . . . having an opinion", used "to signal a movement from object to subject". Endnote 4 records Spivak's comparison between white feminist researchers giving voice to subaltern women and "Freud's giving voice to the hysteric, transforming her into the subject of hysteria", where "an object of research is reconfigured into a subject of the dominator's making."

# PART TWO: MAZZEI 2013

## 7. The concept and where it comes from

Stated in the abstract: "I am thinking with Deleuze and Guattari's concept, the Body without Organs, one they use to enact thinking without a subject and to liberate thought from overcoded images in order to confront a reliance on objects or material representations to understand and explain. Using this concept, I theorize a Voice without Organs (VwO) as a voice that does not emanate from a singular subject but is produced in an enactment among research-data-participants-theory-analysis."

The reason for the detour is given in two sentences: "Deleuze and Guattari are not interested in producing meaning but instead want to know how things work and what they produce." And: "Voice in humanist qualitative inquiry must be present, spoken, heard, recorded, and transcribed into words in an interview transcript."

Her own prior position, which this extends: in work with white teachers she "theorized that interview data (and/or voice) is not bound by speech but can be found in inhabited silence, both purpose full and meaning full" (printed as two words each, deliberately). The lesson she draws from that work: "limiting interview data to spoken words and not attending to words that seemed present in their absence limits knowledge production."

The question she poses for herself: "what kind of voice, what kind of human being can be thought once voice no longer has to be present, emanating from a unique, essentialist subject conscious to itself?"

## 8. The three agencies

She sorts the field by what agency is, and the three descriptions are worth keeping separate.

**Humanist.** "agency is an innate characteristic of the essentialist, intentional, free subject that enables him to act on and in the world", presupposing subjects "who speak for themselves; subjects capable of knowing others; and subjects in charge of their desires and identifications" (Lather 2009).

**Poststructural.** Agency "seems to lie in the subject's ability to decode and recode its identity within discursive formations and cultural practices" (St. Pierre 2000). But: "the humanist subject creeps back into methodological practices when researchers assert a reflexive stance that assumes a posture of knowing in an attempt at greater self-accounting and authenticity."

**Posthumanist.** Agency "is distributed in a way that avoids hanging on to the vestiges of a knowing humanist subject that lingers in some poststructuralist analysis." The warrant is Haraway, quoted via Barad: "Reflexivity has been recommended as a critical practice, but my suspicion is that reflexivity, like reflection, only displaces the same elsewhere, setting up worries about . . . the search for the authentic and really real." And intentionality becomes "attributable to a complex network of human and nonhuman agents, including historically specific sets of material conditions that exceed the traditional notion of the individual."

MacLure supplies the negative: in Deleuzo-Guattarian ontology "there is no present, conscious, coherent individual who 'knows who she is, says what she means and means what she says.'"

## 9. The data, and what she does with it

The study: ten first-generation women faculty and administrators, interviewed with Alecia Jackson for *Thinking with Theory in Qualitative Research* (2012). She states her own and her co-researcher's position in the assemblage: "one of us a first-generation college student; the other, the daughter of parents from Appalachia whose father was the son of immigrants and who attended college on the GI Bill."

The one excerpt in the article is Fran's answer to "What prompted you to first get your master's degree and then your PhD?": "I had been through a really bad breakup, I was the girl's basketball coach, who dated the boy's basketball coach, . . . everybody in the town assumed we were gonna get married and have these little basketball babies and be there forever. . . . I had some friends living in Knoxville and decided I was gonna go to Knoxville for the summer and take some master's classes just to get away for three months . . . and never went back."

**The two readings she sets side by side.** "From a humanist perspective, I could read the above as Fran's construction of herself as a conscious, rational subject, spoken, heard, recorded, and transcribed into words in an interview transcript. But, if I think those words as produced by a VwO, an assemblage, an entanglement, a knot of forces and intensities that operate on a plane of immanence, then I think of them as an enactment among researcher-data-participants-theory-analysis."

What the second reading yields: "That portion of the interview transcript is no longer, for me, a story of Fran, but a collision of forces, a machinic assemblage of becomings of women from small towns who became women in the academy, unexpected becomings." And the flat statement of what is given up: "Agency is not attached to Fran, nor meaning to her words or experience."

**She puts herself in it, and does so concretely.** "That VwO includes my own leaving of a small town that I continue to leave. It also includes my mother and grandmother and aunts and friends, some who left, some who did not, some who could not. It includes the becoming of my leaving as I always negotiate that leaving with students, nieces, geography, partner, parents, family, exes."

On her own control, with Barad's term: "I made agential 'cuts' (see Barad, 2011, p. 123) as an agent, but not as an agent in full control of outcomes and becomings. I set things in motion without knowing what might result."

On what is centred, in a sentence of six negations: "Nothing is centered or stabilized in such an approach to interviewing: not voice, not the subject, not the interviewer or interviewee, not experience, not analysis."

**The different questions she says the concept produces** are listed, and the list is the practical content of the article: "they are questions, not about what our participants mean, but about the forces of desire that are acting through and with our research participants"; questions "about what results (or what is produced) from such interactions"; "questions about how desire is functioning to keep/maintain/produce smooth social, familial, and professional relations"; "questions about the forces/drives/intensities that produce new dreams and desires". Her summary of the shift: "These are questions that prompt me to ask not what is happening but how our participants and our researcher selves are becoming."

## 10. What she says becomes of interviewing

She asks it directly: "Are these relics of humanist qualitative inquiry, or can one still interview and analyze data if the subject is no more?"

Her answer keeps the method and demotes it. "Theorizing with Deleuze and Guattari's concept BwO, and analyzing with VwO does not mean that interviewing must be abandoned as a method, although some might say that it should. However, it certainly demands a de-centering and a de-privileging of the interview in social science research." And more specifically: "Interviewing then and interview data transcribed into transcripts would be only one agential force in the assemblage that one plugs into as one does something called 'research.' Certainly, it would not be the only method nor the primary method of data collection."

Three concrete consequences she names. Attention shifts to "the where of the interview, and the when of the interview, and the if of the interview". Analysis needs "a way to account for the material and discursive simultaneously, the bodies of those whom we interview, our own bodies". And: "perhaps we must think practices that disavow an over-reliance on words as the primary source of meaning."

The substitution she offers for access she does not have, which is the most practical sentence in the article: "Maybe I cannot visit Fran's small town, but I can visit my small town, or other small towns, or reinsert myself into the data in a plugging into the assemblage of human and nonhuman forces. This would not be in an attempt to re-center a description of the small town as primary, but how the small town acts with an agential force and produces its own set of material and discursive constructions."

The symmetry claim she takes from Barad: "neither the discursive practices nor material phenomena are ontologically or epistemologically prior. . . . Neither can be explained in terms of the other. Neither is reducible to the other."

Her warning to her own camp: "The challenge for posthumanist researchers using a Deleuzian ontology of entanglement and assemblage is to attend to how being, doing, and living are different so as not to reproduce the same methods with a different language."

Her acknowledgements name who read it: "the editors; Patti Lather and Bettie St. Pierre, and my readers; Alecia Jackson, Phillip Prince, and Jerry Rosiek."

# PART THREE: THE TWO TOGETHER, AND THIS FOLDER

## 11. What the two articles share

The same journal, ten years apart. The same two authors' collaboration: Mazzei's data are from the study she and Jackson ran together, and Jackson read Mazzei's manuscript.

**The same Deleuze and Guattari passage, in both articles, as the pivot.** Jackson: "the tripartite division between a field of reality (the world) and a field of representation (the book) and a field of subjectivity (the author). Rather, an assemblage establishes connections between certain multiplicities drawn from each of these orders, so that a book has no sequel nor the world its object nor one or several authors as its subject." Mazzei quotes the first two sentences of the same passage, applying it to the interview: "If the interview is also to be thought as an assemblage, there can no longer be a division between a field of reality (what we ask, what our participants tell us, and the places we inhabit), a field of representation (research narratives constructed after the interview), and a field of subjectivity (participants and researchers)."

The two cite the passage to **different page numbers**: Jackson gives *A Thousand Plateaus* pp. 22-23, Mazzei gives p. 25. Both quote the same translation (Massumi). Which is right is not resolvable from these two files and would need the book.

Both refuse the authentic, essentialist speaking subject. Both treat silence as doing something rather than as absence. Both end on production rather than meaning: Jackson with Massumi's "The question is not: is it true? But: does it work", Mazzei with the Deleuzian question she poses as a section heading, "How does it work?".

## 12. Where they differ, recorded as a difference and not settled

**The theorists.** Jackson 2003 runs on Derrida first and Deleuze and Guattari second, with the deconstruction of presence carrying the weight. Mazzei 2013 runs on Deleuze and Guattari plus Barad, and Derrida does not appear in her 14 references.

**Who may speak for whom.** This is the substantive difference. Jackson's three middle irruptions are almost entirely about that question: hooks, Lugones and Spelman, Collins, Mohanty, Trinh, Spivak, Abu-Lughod, Suleri, Jojola, Thornton, Swisher, Alcoff, Visweswaran. None of those names appears in Mazzei's reference list. Mazzei's question is ontological, what kind of voice can be thought, and the question of the researcher's authority over someone else's words is not posed in her article, though she does insert her own position into the assemblage at length.

**What the researcher owes.** Jackson's article contains an actual procedure, Alcoff's four interrogatory practices, and keeps Lugones and Spelman's "hard listening" and Spivak's "unlearning" as obligations. Mazzei's article replaces the obligation with a different question set, the four kinds of question in section 9 above, and treats the reflexive stance itself as a residue of humanism, quoting Haraway that reflexivity "only displaces the same elsewhere".

Both are in this folder. Neither addresses the other on these points. Nothing here decides between them.

## 13. The metaphor thread: four positions now, none settled

Jackson's endnote 1 is a direct statement on the word itself: "I prefer the use of *figuration*, as opposed to *metaphor*, since figurations foreground contradiction and disjunction." And in her body text, the only other use of the word names the emancipatory vocabulary as metaphor: "Metaphors of voice and silence permeate these texts." So in her article "metaphor" is the term for what someone else's language does and "figuration" is the term for what hers does. Mazzei uses neither word, not once in 5,171 words.

The four positions in this folder, as of this read:

- **Neimanis.** Her water figures are "always more than metaphorical", working by "contiguity or continuity (rather than analogy)", and "concepts are always also material".
- **McKnight and Whitburn.** A figure's entailments arrive whether the writer intends them or not, the test is affective ("attend to the wince"), and the ban on metaphor is "a task beyond us as authors".
- **Betterton.** A particular figure of creativity, applied to pregnancy, erases the agency of the person it is applied to; she quotes Davis on feminist theory's "tendency to privilege the body as metaphor" and defers the problem to her book.
- **Jackson.** The word metaphor should be given up for figuration, because figuration keeps contradiction and disjunction where metaphor resolves them.

Four positions. Recorded. Not adjudicated.

## 14. Kotzé, and the rest of the folder

**Kotzé.** This is the pair her method chapter runs on. Her chapter uses Jackson and Mazzei's *Thinking with Theory*, and these are the two primary articles behind it: Jackson's rhizovocality for the data that "glow" and the lines of flight, Mazzei's plugging-in for the thinking-with procedure. Two things to hold rather than reconcile. Kotzé gives her reasons for using neither interviews nor questionnaires; Mazzei's article is a defence of keeping the interview while de-privileging it, written from inside an interview study. And Kotzé's balcony passage defines her method through her own presence in the scene, which is Mazzei's move with the small town and not Jackson's procedure of accountability to the person spoken about. Not adjudicated.

**Betterton.** The link is her Kuhn quotation, on finding "not the voice of the monstrous singular ego but one that, summoning the resources of the place we come from, can speak with eloquence of, and for, that place". Jackson's whole middle section is an argument that speaking "for that place" is the thing that cannot be done innocently; Mazzei's material is literally the place she comes from, used as an agential force rather than as a ground to speak from. Three uses of place, three different claims. Recorded.

**Butler.** Absent from both. Zero occurrences in Jackson's article and references; absent from Mazzei's 14 references. Jackson's performativity language ("performative utterances", "the performative dimension of voice") comes from music theory and Deleuze, not from Butler, and she says so. Given that *Bodies That Matter* was read in full in this folder, that absence is worth noting and is not a fault to fix.

**Shildrick, Fries, Fahey, Romero.** No contact in citation with either article. Barad is the only overlap: central to Mazzei, absent from Jackson, cited twice by McKnight and Whitburn as an already-tried alternative, and the subject of the physics-side question in Romero's chapter. None of those settles any of the others.

## 15. Citation faults and things to check before citing

**Mazzei's reference list drops two words from Barad's subtitle.** She prints "Barad, K. (2007). *Meeting the universe halfway: Quantum physics and the entanglement of meaning*."
