## 2. The climate of opinion, p. 22

The full sentence, with what it is doing around it:

> "That Saussure's proposals are remarkably similar in spirit to those occurring about the same time in physics and mathematics does not require that Saussure knew of Einstein's 1905 papers or read *Principia Mathematica*. Indeed, **to suppose that such parallels require direct lines of influence is to be wedded to the very notions of causality that a field model renders obsolete.** A more accurate and appropriate model for such parallel developments would be a field notion of culture, a societal matrix which consists (in Whitehead's phrase) of a **'climate of opinion' that makes some questions interesting to pursue and renders others uninteresting or irrelevant.** Such a field theory of culture has yet to be definitively articulated, and is beyond the scope of this study."

And immediately after, p. 22 into p. 23:

> "It would, for example, define more fully how a 'climate of opinion' is established, and demonstrate that **it is this climate, rather than direct borrowing or transmission, that is the underlying force guiding intellectual inquiry.** This climate would be, of course, as capable of influencing scientific inquiry as it is of guiding any other conceptualization. Such a history would insist that we not be misled by a causal perspective into thinking of correspondences between disciplines as one-way exchanges, for example, by asserting that the change in scientific paradigms caused a shift in literary form. **In a field model, the interactions are always mutual: the cultural matrix guides individual inquiry at the same time that the inquiry helps to form, or transform, the matrix.**"

Three things about this passage, as findings rather than as reading.

**First, the phrase has no source note.** Hayles writes "(in Whitehead's phrase)" and gives no footnote, although this Introduction footnotes Whorf, Weyl, Eichner and Capra within a few pages. The References list contains exactly one Whitehead entry, and it is **Whitehead and Russell, *Principia Mathematica*, 3 vols., 2nd ed. (Cambridge, 1925-27)**, which is not where the phrase comes from. Whitehead appears three times in the whole book: this sentence, that reference-list entry, and the index line pointing back to p. 22. So the attribution is unsourced, and tracing it to a Whitehead page is a chase item (section 8, item 1).

**Second, the passage is a methodological licence, and it is the strongest one in the book.** It says that parallels between disciplines do not need lines of influence, and that requiring them is itself the old causal thinking. Anything in a dissertation that puts an artwork beside a physics paper, or Barad beside a stim, has to answer the question of what licenses the comparison. This paragraph is an answer with a 1984 date on it, from inside literary studies, and it names the alternative: a field notion of culture.

**Third, Hayles says the theory does not exist.** "Such a field theory of culture has yet to be definitively articulated, and is beyond the scope of this study." She then sketches what it would have to do in four moves (define how a climate is established; show the climate rather than borrowing is what guides inquiry; apply equally to science; refuse one-way causal stories). That is an opening left standing in 1984 and, as of this reading, not closed by anything in these folders. **Whether it has been closed since is worth one search before any claim is built on its being open** (section 8, item 2).

Note what the passage does **not** say. It does not say art and science live inside the climate; it says Saussure's linguistics and Einstein's physics do, and then generalises to "intellectual inquiry" and "any other conceptualization." Art enters the book as literature, and in the Introduction only as the novel. The Heisenberg epigraph to chapter 1 is the place where **art** as such is named, and that is a physicist talking, not Hayles:

> ". . . are the different styles of art an arbitrary product of the human mind? Here again we must not be misled by the Cartesian partition. **The style arises out of the interplay between the world and ourselves, or more specifically, between the spirit of the time and the artist.** The spirit of a time is probably a fact as objective as any fact in natural science, and this spirit brings out certain features in the world . . . . The artist tries in his work to make these features understandable, and in this attempt he is led to the forms of the style in which he works."
> (Werner Heisenberg, *Physics and Philosophy*, as Hayles's epigraph to chapter 1, p. 31)

So the book has two names for the same thing in two registers: Whitehead's **climate of opinion** on p. 22, Heisenberg's **spirit of the time** on p. 31. Heisenberg's version is explicitly about art and the artist and about style; Hayles's version is about questions and inquiry. If the ontology layer wants art and science inside one climate, the Heisenberg epigraph is the sentence that says so, and Hayles put it at the head of the chapter without discussing it.

## 3. No unambiguous time-line for spatially separated events, p. 22

The sentence, in the paragraph that lists the paradoxes of the field concept:

> "Another assumption that becomes paradoxical in a field model is the premise that **it is possible to establish an unambiguous time-line for spatially separated events**, a conception whose unraveling is as important to relativity theory as it is to Nabokov's *Ada*."

It is one sentence in the Introduction, and the argument for it is in chapter 1, pp. 42-49, where it arrives twice.

**The classical assumption, stated so it can be withdrawn, p. 42:**

> "In classical mechanics, the physical world was considered to be composed of isolated objects separated from one another in an empty space that was rigid and unchanging, with **a universal 'now' pervading all space at any given moment**. Because time was handled as though it consisted of a succession of universal moments, **there was never any ambiguity about the order of events**. Hence causality could be unidirectional and absolute."

**The withdrawal, pp. 46-47,** through Minkowski rather than through Einstein:

> Minkowski, before an assembly of colleagues: "Henceforth space by itself, and time by itself, are doomed to fade away into mere shadows, and only a kind of union of the two will preserve an independent reality."

> "In the Minkowski interpretation, time and space are combined into the four-dimensional matrix of 'spacetime.' **It is when this four-dimensional matrix is projected into the three dimensions of traditional Cartesian space that different observers can disagree about what happened.** If, however, time is added as a 'fourth dimension,' the resulting (four-dimensional) description will be the same for all observers."

> Taylor and Wheeler, quoted at p. 47: "Space is different for different observers. Time is different for different observers. **Spacetime is the same for everyone**."

Two cautions that matter for using this, and both are Hayles's own.

**Relativity does not mean everything is relative.** p. 45: "Relativity does not imply that 'everything is relative.' Indeed, before he settled on 'relativity,' Einstein had considered calling his hypothesis the 'Theory of invariance.'" And p. 46: "**Paradoxically, these quantities are made relative so that others may become absolute.**" The loss of a single time-line is the price of an invariance, not the arrival of a free-for-all.

**The ambiguity is not perceptual.** Footnote 26, p. 46: "It should be emphasized that the relativity of these quantities is not merely a perceptual ambiguity in the observer. The most sensitive instruments (for example, nuclear decay clocks) will record a time that is not absolute, but relative to the reference frame to which they are attached." The same point is made at p. 18 against Bergson: for Einstein "time is relativistic not because the universe is infused with life, but because the motion of the observer affects the language of description," and "the same result would be obtained if the measurement were made by a nuclear decay clock rather than a person."

What relativity does leave, p. 49, in two numbered implications:

> "first, that the world is an interconnected whole, so that the dichotomies of space and time, matter and energy, gravity and inertia, become nothing more than different aspects of the same phenomena; and second, that **there is no such thing as observing this interactive whole from a frame of reference removed from it**. Relativity implies that we cannot observe the universe from an Olympian perspective. **Necessarily and irrevocably we are within it, part of the cosmic web.**"

## 4. The holistic field and the linear flow of words

Hayles asks this twice, both times as a question, and both times it is the question of the whole book rather than a claim of hers.

p. 25 into p. 26, of Pirsig:

> "From this popular treatment of the field concept emerges **a question that haunts all of the writers in this study: can the representation of a holistic field be accomplished within the linear flow of words, or is the attempt inherently limited by the fragmentation of the medium?**"

p. 27, of Pynchon:

> "At the center of Pynchon's *Gravity's Rainbow* is the question with which we began **- can a holistic field be represented in a linear flow of words? -** and his treatment of it is formidably complex."

She never answers it in the Introduction. Structurally, though, the book has already answered it in the negative twice, and both answers are stronger than the question.

**The cosmic web passage, p. 21, is the first answer.** The whole of it, because the last three sentences are the argument:

> "A central metaphor in this study, the cosmic web has connotations worth exploring. Readers who desire a visual image to go along with the metaphor may imagine it as a network of strands coextensive with space. Note that the web is not space itself, nor does it 'contain' space. Rather **it is an artifact, a created object whose artificiality corresponds to the conceptualization of the field models it signifies**; what we are concerned with in these models is not reality as such, but conceptualizations that may or may not correspond with whatever we call reality. Imagine further that the web is composed of articulated joints, much as a spider's web is. These joinings will serve as a convenient reminder that the verbal models we shall be examining are also **articulated, in the double sense of being utterances and of being composed of discrete units joined together.** Once the web is constructed, these joinings may stand for, or gesture toward, a seamless whole; but **this evocation can be attempted only through a medium that is itself linear, sequential, and articulated. The prey the cosmic web is designed to entrap is the dynamic, holistic reality implied by the field concept. But the prey always escapes, precisely because the web is articulated**; as we shall see, **to speak is to create, or presuppose, the separation between subject and object that the reality would deny. What is captured by the cosmic web is thus not the elusive whole, but the observer who would speak that whole.** Hence the cosmic web is inherently paradoxical, deriving its deepest meaning from a whole that it can neither contain nor express. Its history can be told as the history of certain paradoxes."

**The close of chapter 1, p. 59, is the second answer**, and it generalises the failure past natural language:

> "What our survey of the field concept in various scientific models has shown is that **the problem of articulation is intrinsic to this view of reality**, whether the language involved is the binary sequence of computer programs, the 'wave-packet' equations of quantum mechanics, or one of the syntactically linear natural languages in which scientists attempt to come to grips with the philosophical implications of their models. **Because the task of articulation requires that a vision of a dynamic, mutually interacting field be represented through a medium that is inherently linear, fragmented, and unidirectional**, the novelist's concern with language will have much in common with these scientific concerns."

And the two themes she states explicitly at p. 54, having kept them implicit through the chapter:

> "One is **the extraordinary vision of unity inherent in the field concept of reality**; the second is **the extreme difficulty of translating this intuitive vision into an articulated model.**"

So in Hayles the impossibility is a property of **articulation**: anything made of joined discrete units, prose and equations and binary alike, cannot hold a field. Two further supports for the same point, from the Introduction's kaleidoscope passage, pp. 19-20: the synchronic cut ("by stopping the kaleidoscope we have lost the dynamic essence of the dance, for the static 'patterns' never in fact existed as discrete entities. **The problem is endemic to synchronic analyses; any finite, slice-of-time model will encounter the same problem**"), and the missing standpoint ("**there will always be one place we can never see at all - the spot we are standing on.** Like the figure in a painting who wishes to gesture toward the picture that contains him, we can never arrive at a complete and unambiguous description of this reality because we are involved in what we would describe").

Bohr's position, given at pp. 53-55, is the one place in the book where the limit is made workable rather than mourned:

> "To speak is to speak from a position that is defined as separate and distinct from that which is spoken about. Language thus implies a viewpoint, a specific place at which the subject-object split is made. But because of the Uncertainty Relation, this viewpoint will always result in an incomplete and partial description. To complete the description, another viewpoint is necessary which makes the subject-object split in a different place. **But these viewpoints will be mutually exclusive**, because the subject-object split can only be made in one place at a time."

> Bohr, quoted p. 53: "we must, in general, be prepared to accept the fact that **a complete elucidation of one and the same object may require diverse points of view which defy a unique description**."

> p. 54-55: "according to Bohr, we progressively refine our viewpoints not by attempting the impossible, that is, observing without a viewpoint, but by **recognizing the ways in which our description of reality depends on the viewpoint we have chosen** . . . **Thus progress is made not by ignoring or underplaying limitations of viewpoint, but by systematically examining and exploiting them.**"

And Bohr's own line, which Hayles uses as an Introduction epigraph and returns to at p. 52 through Aage Petersen: **"we are suspended in language."**

Trinh's own version of the kernel refusal, *Woman, Native, Other*, in the chapter "The Language of Nativism," at the page the text marks 49:

> "Since there is '**no hidden unity to be grasped**,' **no secret meaning to discover behind the package, to look for it is to throw the package away.**"

And immediately before it, the sentence that gives the refusal a body:

> "**Seeking to perforate meaning by forcing my entry or breaking it open to dissipate what is thought to be its secrets seems to me as crippled an act as verifying the sex of an unborn child by ripping open the mother's womb.** It is typical of a mentality that proves incapable of touching the living thing without crushing its delicateness. I undeniably prefer the heterogeneity of free play in a dice game to the unity and uniformity of dissection, classification, and synthesis toward a higher truth."

### Clarity

The section is titled "Vertically imposed language: on clarity, craftsmanship, and She who steals language," beginning at p. 15. The sentence, at pp. 16-17:

> "To use the language well, says the voice of literacy, cherish its classic form. Do not choose the offbeat at the cost of clarity. Obscurity is an imposition on the reader. True, but beware when you cross railroad tracks for one train may hide another train. **Clarity is a means of subjection, a quality both of official, taught language and of correct writing, two old mates of power: together they flow, together they flower, vertically, to impose an order.**"

With the two sentences that set up what clarity is for:

> "**Clarity as a purely rhetorical attribute serves the purpose of a classical feature in language, namely, its instrumentality.** To write is to communicate, express, witness, impose, instruct, redeem, or save - at any rate **to mean and to send out an unambiguous message**. Writing thus reduced to a mere vehicle of thought may be used to orient toward a goal or to sustain an act, but it does not constitute an act in itself."

And on who gets to be unclear, same chapter: Taoist and Zen language, "perfectly accessible but rife with paradox," does not qualify as clear, "for paradox is 'illogical' and 'nonsensical' to many Westerners"; nor does vernacular speech, "which is not acquired through institutions."

On objectivity and the willed meaning, "The Language of Nativism":

> "Not his language, which he wishes to render transparent, believing he could erase himself in his writing while clinging to the author's mastership. **The claim to objectivity subjects words to a willed meaning-intention.** Do I make sense? **Meaningful language is confined to 'expression'** and what appears significant to him is its reduction to pure instrumentality . . . **Words are solicited only for their effacement from the page. Their materiality, their glaring bodies must somehow sink and disappear from the field of visibility**, to yield ground to the . . ."

### Truth

From the Chen interview, *Visual Anthropology Review* 8.1 (Spring 1992), at the page the text marks 86:

> "**Truth never yields itself in anything said or shown. One cannot just point a camera at it to catch it: the very effort to do so will kill it.** It is worth quoting here again Walter Benjamin for whom, 'nothing is poorer than a truth expressed as it was thought.' **Truth can only be approached indirectly if one does not want to lose it.**"

In *Woman, Native, Other* the same refusal is structural: the whole of chapter IV is "Grandma's Story," and its first section, at p. 119, is titled "**Truth and fact: story and history**." Its anthropologist is "**the purveyor of 'truth'**" who "has moved from the absolute to the relative and now assumes the role of purveyor of 'certain truths,'" and "**The purveyor of Truth or certain anthropomorphic truths can equally be called the purveyor of Error or certain anthropomorphic errors.**"

### Meaning, and the plurality of it

> "**The more ears I am able to hear with, the farther I see the plurality of meaning and the less I lend myself to the illusion of a single message.**"

> "**Words empty out with age. Die and rise again, accordingly invested with new meanings, and always equipped with a secondhand memory.**" (the opening lines of chapter I, after the epigraphs)

> "the necessity of **a practice of language which remains, through its signifying operations, a process constantly unsettling the identity of meaning and speaking/writing subject**"

### Speaking nearby

The definition, Chen interview, p. 86, given in answer to a question about indirect language. The source text is a two-column scan whose columns interleave in extraction, so this is the passage reassembled and it must be checked against the printed page before it is quoted:

> "The link is nicely done; especially between '**speaking nearby**' and indirect language. In other words, **a speaking that does not objectify, does not point to an object as if it is distant from the speaking subject or absent from the speaking place. A speaking that reflects on itself and can come very close to a subject without, however, seizing or claiming it. A speaking in brief, whose closures are only moments of transition opening up to other possible moments of transition** - these are forms of indirectness well understood by anyone in tune with poetic language."

And why it is not a technique:

> "**To say therefore that one prefers not to speak about but rather to speak nearby, is a great challenge. Because actually, this is not just a technique or a statement to be made verbally. It is an attitude in life, a way of positioning oneself in relation to the world.** Thus, the challenge is to materialize it in all aspects of the film - verbally, musically, visually."

In *Woman, Native, Other* the phrase arrives in the Difference chapter without the film around it:

> "The process of differentiation, however, continues, and **speaking nearby or together with certainly differs from speaking for and about. The latter aims at the finite and dwells in the realm of fixed oppositions** (subject/object difference; man/woman sexual difference), tending thereby to valorize the privileged father-daughter relationship."

And the sentence nearest to the luminous haze, from the end of the Nativism chapter, on what a different relation of words to things would be:

> "**it holds words out in the direction of things or lays them down nearby things so as to call them and breathe new life into them. Not to capture, to chain them up, nor to mean.** Not to instruct nor to discipline."

That last sentence is the one to set directly against Hayles's web. Hayles's web is built to **entrap** the whole and fails ("the prey always escapes"). Trinh's words are laid **nearby** things and are explicitly not for capture. The verb is the same; the intention is reversed.

### Where the two accounts go apart (recorded, not adjudicated)

Both refuse the kernel. They do not refuse it for the same reason, and the difference is not a disagreement to be settled.

- **Hayles's limit is formal.** It follows from the medium: anything articulated (prose, equations, binary) cannot hold a field, so the attempt at representation is defeated by the instrument. Her question stays a question, and in the Introduction the writers in her study are arranged by how much they resist the answer.
- **Trinh's limit is a politics and then a practice.** Clarity is not a neutral property that happens to fail; it is "a means of subjection," an instrumentality serving an order, and the demand to "mean and to send out an unambiguous message" is what is being refused. What follows is not defeat but a way of working: indirection, opacity respected, speaking nearby, closures that are "only moments of transition."
- **One consequence of the difference.** In Hayles the observer is what gets caught by the web ("What is captured by the cosmic web is thus not the elusive whole, but the observer who would speak that whole"). In Trinh the speaker is not caught but repositioned, and the position is the method ("an attitude in life, a way of positioning oneself in relation to the world").
- **The haze itself reads differently in each.** For Hayles the haze is where the meaning of a text turns out to be once the field concept is admitted, a finding about *Heart of Darkness*. For Trinh an equivalent haze is not a finding but a condition to be worked in and protected, and the attempt to clear it is what kills the thing.

## 6. Chapter 3, the body chapter

"Evasion: The Field of the Unconscious in D. H. Lawrence," pp. 85-110. Epigraph from Evelyn Keller, "Cognitive Repression in Contemporary Physics."

**What is in it.** Lawrence's "subjective science," whose "basic premise . . . was that it is possible to apprehend reality directly from a set of symmetrically arranged 'centers' in the body, without mediation from the conscious mind" (p. 85). Verification is by other people's intuition, "appealing to the intuition of others who will verify statements from their own unconscious centers." When two sets of centres meet they become "polarities."

**The body as an actual map, pp. 104-105.** This is the most concrete body material in the book, and it is a scheme of paired organs:

| stage | centre | where | what it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| first | solar plexus | front, upper abdomen | the infant's response **toward** the parents |
| second | lumbar ganglion | lower back | the infant's reaction **away** from the parents |
| third | cardiac plexus | chest | toward, in an "objective" mode, seeking direct knowledge of the object |
| fourth | thoracic ganglion | shoulders | volition and autonomy, same mode |
| at puberty | four more | | |
| later in life | four more again | | |

Two axes come out of it: "the polarity between the spiritual and sensual in the vertical plane; and the polarity between union and autonomy on the horizontal plane." And the detail Hayles says matters most: "**the genital center has its symmetrical counterpart in the upper center of the throat.**"

**What Hayles does with the throat, pp. 106-107.** Lawrence's "spiritual incest" is located "not in the genital centers, but in the upper throat centers," quoting *Fantasia* p. 158: mothers "establish a dynamic connection between the two centres, the centres of the throat, the centres of the higher dynamic sympathy and cognition. They establish that circuit. And break it if you can. Very often not even death can break it." Then the turn:

> "**The displacement of genital bonding by bonding through the throat is appropriate (and even necessary) as a reinforcement to the artist-becoming-the-mother, for the throat is, of course, the place from which speech issues.**"

Which rests on the labour analogy from p. 97 and p. 107: Lawrence's style, the "continual, slightly modified repetition," is "a movement of speech analogous to the contractions of labor, a rhythmic and tense pulsation capable of propelling one into a new existence," and "**By imaging his speech as the act of birth, the quintessential moment when one becomes a mother, Lawrence implies that through his art he can metaphorically become the mother.**"

**The sentence that carries the chapter, p. 95:**

> "It evolves from Lawrence's belief that real knowledge is always sensual and immediate rather than mental, and his simultaneous endeavor to make us apprehend this through the verbal abstractions and stylizations of art-speech. Simply put, **the paradox is this: to know is not to be able to say, and to say is to move from the reality of unmediated knowledge into abstraction.** It is a dilemma that Bohr also recognized as fundamental, for to speak is to enter into the 'either-or' conceptualizations that quantum theory, like Lawrence, was trying to escape."

**And a formal answer to the linear-words question, from inside the writing**, pp. 97-98. Lawrence's *Women in Love* foreword: "fault is often found with the continual, slightly modified repetition. The only answer is that it is natural to the author; and that every natural crisis in emotion or passion or understanding comes from this pulsing, frictional to-and-fro which works up to culmination." Hayles's gloss: "**Through the 'continual, slightly modified repetition,' the linear flow of language is partially checked.** The result is not straightforward linearity but the 'frictional to-and-fro' of repetitive clauses that build through a series of periodic sentences up to the culmination of the single, short declarative sentence which, if the style works, is the point of breakthrough." Later, of the Ursula and Anton passage, p. 98: "**the language achieves what the characters cannot**, an inner tension that can come to climax without being condemned to linearity as a result."

That is the one place in the three chapters read where a writer's formal device **partially defeats** linearity rather than losing to it. Hayles grants it twice and calls it a partial check, not a solution.

**Why Hayles calls it evasion.** Three strands. (i) The dialectic is unstable because one polarity is always valued over the other, so it either collapses into "rigidity" or has to be subsumed into a larger term, and "**its continuation implies that its putative goal, the breakthrough into the unconscious, is never achieved**" (p. 89). (ii) The undifferentiated field is the same state as the infant's non-differentiation from the mother, which Lawrence "regards with horror" and associates with "the 'ghoul' of repressed incest desire," so the goal is also the thing most feared (pp. 89-90). (iii) The closing move, p. 110: Lawrence and the quantum physicists share "an early attachment to the mother as primary caretaker, and a process of gender differentiation in which the mother is posited as quintessentially different from the self," so "for both artist and scientist a subconscious equation is made between the autonomy of the self and the objectification of the other." The scientist's version becomes the objectification of Mother Nature, by way of Keller.

**The Keller argument, pp. 87-88, is the chapter's engine and it is a feminist one.** Keller on the physicists: the Copenhagen interpretation is "an umbrella term 'under which a host of different, often contradictory positions co-reside'," and the confusions are "**de facto evidence of defense and evasion**." What is evaded is "the recognition that the self exists neither in isolation from the world nor in mysterious sympathy with it." And: "the capacity for objective thought and perception is not inborn, but rather . . . acquired as part of the long and painful struggle for psychic autonomy, a state never entirely free from ambiguity and tension. **The internal pressure to delineate self from other . . . leaves us acutely vulnerable to anxiety about wishes or experiences which might threaten that delineation.**"

**What to know before using this chapter for a body thread (mine, not hers).** The body in it is Lawrence's scheme and Lawrence's anxiety, read psychoanalytically, and the chapter's verdict is that the route through the body fails. The usable material is not the plexus map but three structural claims: that the demand for a rigid self/other boundary is itself what produces the objectifying stance (Keller), that the throat and the genital centre being one axis makes **speech a bodily organ of the same system** as sex and birth, and that a formal device in writing can partially check linearity. Nothing in the chapter is about an artwork made with a body, and nothing in it concerns transcorporeality in Alaimo's sense: no toxins, no permeability, no environment. Its body is a bounded, symmetrically mapped interior with a mother outside it.
