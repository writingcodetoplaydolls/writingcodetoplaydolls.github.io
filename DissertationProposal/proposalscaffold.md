## What a proposal is for

A proposal is a contract. It tells your committee what you will make, why it matters, how you will do it, and how they will judge it. It is written in future tense ("I will"), and its chapters usually become Chapters 1–3 of the dissertation.

It has to answer five questions:

1. What will you make or do?
2. Why does it matter, and to whom? In LLSS, that includes why the work belongs in a language, literacy, and sociocultural studies department.
3. How will you do it? Theory, then methodology, then methods: the same split your comps takes from Sullivan.
4. How will the committee know it is done, and how will they judge it?
5. What will they receive at the defense, and how will it be archived?

A written dissertation answers questions 4 and 5 by default, so Lim's proposal never has to address them. When the final artifact is not writing, those two questions carry the most weight. Rajabali spends four framing sections (On Process, On Engaging, On Song, On Method and Metaphor) answering them before any of the work appears.

The proposal itself is still writing, even when the dissertation isn't.

LLSS logistics: a four-member committee with at least one external member, a proposal hearing scheduled through your advisor, and an IRB protocol if the study involves human subjects. The department recommends taking LLSS 650 (Dissertation Seminar) while you develop the proposal ([LLSS PhD page](https://coehs.unm.edu/departments-programs/llss-alt/language-literacy-sociocultural-studies/phd.html)).

## Framing the research questions

Build the questions in five layers, from a premise down to sub-questions, then state the contribution. Each layer narrows what the artifact has to do.

1. **Premise.** "Given that \[a claim about the world, from your theory\], and if \[a claim about what practice does\], then the central concern of this study is not whether \[the conventional question\], but how \[the generative question\]."
2. **Purpose statement** (Lim's form). "The purpose of this \[art practice as research\] study is to \[verb\] \[phenomenon\] through the making of \[artifact\], in order to \[contribution\]."
3. **Two main questions.** One about enactment: "How has/does my \[practice\] produce \[phenomenon\]?" One about implication: "What does \[phenomenon\] reveal about \[larger concern\] for \[audience\]?"
4. **Three dimensions** (the fugitive-geographies model): how the practice is enacted, how it has shaped you, and what it offers others.
5. **Sub-questions, one per named concept.** The concepts become the organizing structure of Chapter 3, and later of the dissertation.
6. **Contribution and claims** (Andersen's form). "My contribution is \[the apparatus / method / body of work\] and \[what it makes knowable\]. I will propose that \[claim\], \[claim\], and \[claim\]."

Checks before committing:

- Ask "how" and "what," never "whether" or "does."
- Each question can be answered through making, with the artifact as evidence.
- Each sub-question points to the traces that will answer it in Chapter 3.
- The scope is bounded: which works, which years, which apparatus.
- First person is expected in self-study. The fugitive-geographies questions and Rajabali both use "my."

If your questions grew out of the work, say so. Andersen writes: "Instead of starting with a research question per se, my inquiry started as a practical concern," one that arose in her teaching. A premise can name that origin.

### Worked sketch from your Maximalist Assemblage notes

This shows the mechanics. The wording is a starting point to rewrite.

**Premise.** Given that knowing and being are inseparable, and the researcher is part of the apparatus that produces what she knows (Barad, 2007), and if art practice is one of the milieus where knowledge is made rather than a later application of theory, then the central concern of this study is not whether my art practice counts as research, but how it frames singular phenomena on a continuous trajectory of becoming, and what those framings make knowable.

**Main questions.**

1. How have the nodes of my nomadic subjectivities (artist, educator, researcher, parent, neurodiverse person) shown up in my art practice as research?
2. How does a specific apparatus frame a singular time/space/phenomenon without exhaustive capture, and what does that framing offer \[the students I walk alongside / art education / literacy studies\]?

**Sub-questions keyed to your triad.**

- **Apparatus:** What arrangements have I built (camera, 3D scan, code, installation, text), and what do their cuts include and exclude?
- **Transposition:** How has theory/abstraction always moved through my making? The past works folded into the corpus are the evidence.
- **Diffraction:** What becomes perceptible when my works are read through one another and through Braidotti, Trinh, Bennett, Barad, Alaimo, and Deleuze?

Your third draft question ("How does theory/abstraction/my nomadic subjectivities create with/through an explicit/specific apparatus …") already holds all three concepts. The sketch pulls them apart so each can be answered separately.

## Framing the task

The task statement says what you will make, how, and what the committee will receive. Fill each bracket and most of Chapter 3 follows from it.

"I will make \[artifact\] using \[media / apparatus\] at \[site\] between \[start\] and \[end\]. Making it will involve \[methods\]. The making will leave these traces: \[documentation\]. The work will be encountered by \[who\] at \[where / when\]. The written component will \[its job\] and run about \[length\]. At the defense the committee will receive \[what\], and it will be deposited as \[format\]."

- [x] What is the artifact: new work, a re-presentation of the 2016–2019 works, or both? Both: new work, a generative, interactive installation in and out of digital space, with a website as its corpus. The 2016–2019 works fold into that corpus as evidence that theory and abstraction have always been your diffraction grating.
- [ ] Is the artifact the answer to the questions, or the site where answers are produced? This is Frayling's "research for" (thinking embodied in the artifact) versus "research through" (comps, Part Three §1.2).
- [ ] What is the written component's job: an exegesis that contextualizes the work, a parallel artwork in maximalist assemblage writing (as Rajabali's prose is), or both?
- [ ] Who encounters the work, and does their encounter become data? Contributed images or audience responses, as in Sunsets for Mike and Carmen, likely mean an IRB protocol.
- [ ] How will a time-based, interactive, or generative work be deposited? A piece with an endless running time needs a documentation plan: video capture, code, stills. - [ ] Which past works are in the corpus, and which are out?
- [ ] Will the dissertation end with something others can use, as Andersen ends with a workshop recipe? A classroom score or protocol drawn from your practice could carry the LLSS contribution.
- [ ] Ask the Graduate Studies manuscript reviewer whether media files can be deposited with the dissertation. The [format page](https://grad.unm.edu/resources/start-to-finish-unm/phd/format-submit-dissertation.html) doesn't say.

## Assessing the work: a crosswalk

Lim's four trustworthiness criteria have rough counterparts in art practice as research. Naming them shows an LLSS committee how to judge the work on its own terms. They are analogies, not equivalences.

| Lim's criterion | What it asks | Counterpart in your field | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Credibility | Do participants' perceptions match the researcher's portrayal? | Accountability to the cut: specify the arrangement that produced the knowledge and what it excluded | Barad (2007); Comps Conclusion |
| Dependability | Is the research process documented transparently? | Traceability: the artist-researcher makes the work's consequences traceable and communicable | Kirkkopelto (2015); Comps Part Three §1.2 |
| Confirmability | Has researcher bias been accounted for? | Situated location stated and apparatus disclosed; the researcher is inside the apparatus, not outside it | Comps Introduction; Part Three §1.4 |
| Transferability | Can findings move to similar contexts? | Generativity: the work "is done when someone else picks it up and makes something of it that the apparatus could not predict" | Barone & Eisner (2012), via Rajabali; Comps Conclusion |

Rajabali adds Barone and Eisner's evocation and illumination as assessment criteria, and points to Gouzouasis (2008) on assessment and validity in arts-based research. Andersen adds two moves. She distributes validation by leaving interpretation to the participants, and her conclusion states plainly what her technique cannot do. A limits paragraph like hers reads as rigor, not weakness. Sanders invokes Eisner's (1976) educational connoisseurship, which gives a College of Education committee a familiar name for judging craft.
