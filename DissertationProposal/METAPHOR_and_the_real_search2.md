## 2. Haraway, *Modest_Witness*, p. 11. The source the draft cites, and it is held.

**Haraway, D. J. (1997), *Modest_Witness@Second_Millennium.FemaleMan©_Meets_OncoMouse™*, Routledge.** `K:\OneDrive\UNM\Comps\compsnotused\Haraway_Donna_J_Modest_Witness_Second_Millennium_1997.pdf`. **The reading plan does not list this book as held.** `Draft v37` cites it at pp. 11 and 16; p. 11 is the figuration passage, and it is the most direct statement of the claim found in any held text.

> "topics and tropes are both spatial concepts. **The 'figure' is the French term for the face**, a meaning kept in English in the notion of the lineaments of a story. **'To figure' means to count or calculate and also to be in a story, to have a role. A figure is also a drawing.** Figures pertain to graphic representation and visual forms in general, a matter of no small importance in visually saturated technoscientific culture. **Figures do not have to be representational and mimetic, but they do have to be tropic; that is, they cannot be literal and self-identical.** Figures must involve at least some kind of displacement that can trouble identifications and certainties. **Figurations are performative images that can be inhabited.** Verbal or visual, figurations can be condensed maps of contestable worlds. **All language, including mathematics, is figurative, that is, made of tropes, constituted by bumps that make us swerve from literal-mindedness.** I emphasize figuration to make explicit and inescapable **the tropic quality of all material-semiotic processes**, especially in technoscience."

Three things in it. "**All language, including mathematics, is figurative**" removes the literal baseline against which a figure could be measured as lesser. "**They cannot be literal and self-identical**" makes the non-identity a requirement rather than a defect. And "**performative images that can be inhabited**" makes the figure a place rather than a copy.

**It also carries a triple etymology in two sentences**, which is a tenth precedent for the 1.5 row and an unusual one, because the three senses it gathers are the three things this project does: ***figure*** as the French for face; "**to figure**" as both to count or calculate and to be in a story; and "**a figure is also a drawing**." Counting, narrative and drawing in one word.

The p. 16 passage the draft also cites is the reflexivity one, which turned up independently today inside Barad's book: "Reflexivity, like reflection, only displaces the same elsewhere, setting up the worries about copy and original and the search for the authentic and really real", against which "**diffract the rays of technoscience so that we get more promising interference patterns on the recording films of our lives and bodies**".

## 3. Leonardo, 1981: a review that is an argument about exactly this

Found in the Leonardo spoke, which is her own catalogued corpus. **Leonardo 14(3), 1981, pp. 248-249.** The item is a book review:

**Turbayne, C. M. (1970), *The Myth of Metaphor*, University of South Carolina Press, 241 pp., reviewed by Carl R. Hausman** (Department of Philosophy, Pennsylvania State University; the Leonardo index spells him "Hausmann"). Text at `K:\ResearchRepository\Dissertation_Hub\Leonardo_Spoke\text\599735.txt`.

**Turbayne's position, as the review reports it.** Metaphor is "**sort-crossing**" in which there is a "**pretense**" that facts of one category belong to another, in words ("Man is a wolf") and in non-verbal media including painting and sculpture. Then:

> "**Awareness that such sort-crossing is make-believe is crucial. One is used by metaphors when one forgets that they treat something as if it were something else and instead mistake 'the model for the thing modeled'.** One tends to overlook how much one's language is shot through with expressions that were once metaphorical but that are now taken as literal."

Descartes and Newton are his cases: "Each was 'victimized' by his metaphors, because each thought his deductive procedures constituted necessary relations in the process that the procedures were intended to explain." And: "**The use of metaphor is inevitable. Thus, one should treat hypotheses and theories only as if they give us access to reality. But some metaphors are better than others**", better meaning "more economical and aids prediction and application of the metaphor to the processes of nature".

**Hausman's objections are the claim being looked for.** Two of them:

> "He admits that he is committed to denying that one can know facts independently of metaphors. Yet he suggests that he has some access to facts, **how else would he know that metaphors are not identical with reality?** and he does refer to independent processes in nature and to observed relationships among events. He cannot be sure what the facts are, but he somehow knows that there are facts and that there is a 'world' with features to be selected by metaphors. **What kind of Kantianism is this?**"

> "**Finally, he defines metaphor so that it excludes the possibility that metaphors generate novelty. His metaphors are really analogies. They are selective rather than generative**, and, paradoxically, they are selective from a world that remains unknown in its own terms."

And the self-application problem, in one line: "one wonders if **Turbayne views his own metaphor thesis as metaphorical**, and, if it is, **could one find a better metaphor to substitute for it?**"

So Hausman holds that a metaphor **generates** rather than selects, and he challenges the claim that metaphors are not identical with reality by asking what vantage point could establish it. **Carl R. Hausman went on to write *Metaphor and Art: Interactionism and Reference in the Verbal and Nonverbal Arts* (Cambridge, 1989)**, which is the book-length version and is the thing to get if this is the lane.

## 4. Leonardo, 1978: "real metaphor" is a defined term in this journal, coined by an artist with a camera

This is the find that is most particular to her corpus, and it is a term with a stated criterion.

**Muscutt, K. (1978), "Projected Kinetic Displays and Photomicrographs Based on the Use of Polarized Light", *Leonardo* 11(2), 97-102**, 3,739 words. The author makes kinetic displays and photomicrographs by manipulating birefringent materials under polarized light. In section IV, writing in the first person about his own work:

> "I have been intrigued by the range of visual metaphors available particularly in the photomicrographs. Although the metaphors that interest me are more ambiguous and less familiar than direct associations with everyday objects, they are none-the-less founded largely on the recognition of recurrent patterns in nature. **I have found it helpful to distinguish between two kinds of visual metaphor: real metaphor and imaginary metaphor. I use these terms for their relative convenience rather than to make absolute distinctions.** Thus, **real metaphors are resemblances that are based on some factual connection** between a photomicrograph and something more familiar that it resembles. For instance, a dendritic formation of a slide matrix may look similar to an aerial view of a river system. **Both patterns are governed by physical principles of nature and the connection between them is real, despite differences of scale.**"

> "**Imaginary metaphors point to resemblances between patterns that share little or no factual connection.** An example of an imaginary metaphor is the impression of depth sometimes apparent in photomicrographs. Since the specimen is of negligible thickness, the impression of depth is a visual illusion. **It results from the mimicry of depictions presented in perspective.**"

> "**Colour furnishes examples of both real and imaginary metaphor.**"

**And the term entered the journal's own glossary.** In the "Terminology" instalment at *Leonardo* 11 (1978), `text/599066.txt`:

> "**885. Real metaphor (in visual art)** — A kind of visual metaphor that refers to a resemblance between, say, a pattern seen in a material under a microscope and a pattern of something more familiar seen on a much larger scale in nature (for example an aerial view of a river system) **when the two patterns have a factual connection (for example they were formed by the same or related physical or chemical forces)**. (11, 100) (cf. Term 880: imaginary metaphor)"

Four things make this worth more than its length. **The criterion is causal, not visual**: a metaphor is "real" when the same or related physical forces produced both patterns. **It is a claim about scale**, "despite differences of scale", which is the scale question arriving from the art side rather than from Barad. **It is a camera argument**, made by an artist working through a microscope in polarized light, which puts it in 1.1. And **it is an art term with a formal definition in an art-and-science journal's terminology**, cross-referenced to its opposite at term 880, so it can be cited as a term and not only as an opinion.

## 5. Three more from Leonardo, shorter

**The *Ways of Worldmaking* review**, *Leonardo* 14(3), 1981, 247-248, on Nelson Goodman, in which the versions are not lesser than the world: "'**We must distinguish falsehood and fiction from truth and fact; but we cannot . . . do it on the grounds that fiction is fabricated and fact is found**.' Rather, one must consider the versions that one has of the facts '**for facts, after all, are obviously factitious**'. . . . Thus, **worlds are made through symbols** that are used to provide versions of it . . . **For Goodman, the correctness of fit is of greater concern than the truth.** Since there is not a world uncolored by one's traditions and conceptions, 'knowing and understanding [are] seen as ranging beyond the acquiring of true beliefs to the discovery and devising of fits of all sorts'." ## 9. The full-corpus sweep, finished, and what it found

**1,242 PDFs attempted, 886 extracted, 482 tier-1 windows.** The extractions are cached, so any further search of this corpus is now instant.

**The first thing it found is a word.** The claim being looked for has a technical name in rhetoric: **catachresis**, a figure used where no literal term exists, so that it cannot be a substitute for a literal expression and cannot be measured against one. **107 windows across eight texts**, and two of those texts are held and substantial.

### Butler, *Bodies That Matter* (1993), 24 windows. Held.

`K:\OneDrive\UNM\Comps\compsnotused\Butler_Judith_1993_Bodies_That_Matter_On_the_Discursive_Limits_of_Sex.pdf`. Butler's definition, given through Irigaray:

> "the feminine appears for Irigaray only in catachresis, that is, **in those figures that function improperly, as an improper transfer of sense, the use of a proper name to describe that which does not properly belong to it**, and that return to haunt and coopt the very language from which the feminine is excluded."

**The passage that is directly about the figure and the real** is her reading of Zizek's rock: "the rock thus **figures the unfigurable**, and so emerges not only as a catachresis, but as one that is supposed to **secure the borders between what he will call sometimes symbolization and sometimes 'discourse,' on the one hand, and the 'real,' on the other**, where the latter is designated as that for which no symbolization is possible."

**And the passage on naming, which is the mechanism**: "by virtue of the very reiterability of the name, the necessity that the name be reiterated in order to name, to fix its referent, **this risk of catachresis is continually reproduced. Hence, the very iterability of the name produces the catachrestic divergence from the chain that the referent is meant to forestall.** And this raises the further and consequential question of **whether the permanent risk of catachresis does not 'unfix' the referent**", and "whether the referent is itself always only tenuously fixed by this regulation of its use".

Also: Plato "**proceeds to name what cannot be properly named, invoking a catachresis**" for the receptacle; Spivak's "**subaltern feminine, understood as a catachresis, whose exclusion from representation has become the condition of representation itself**"; matter in two modalities, the second "**as an ungrounded figure, worrisomely speculative and catachrestic**"; and Kripke's aardvark, where the improper use of a proper name "**inheres in the possibility of the proper usage**".

The plan's 2.5 cluster already cites Butler 1993 at p. 30 for signification producing the body it claims to discover. **This is the same book, and the catachresis material in it has not been used.**

### Carter, F. (2022), "'Work at the language-face'", PhD, University of Edinburgh. 36 windows. Held twice.

`K:\OneDrive\UNM\Dissertation\todo October 26\historical art and black mountain new mat posthum\CarterFred_2022_WorkAttheLanguageFace.pdf`, and the same thesis again under the filename `122_Belletto_Carter_Davidson_Gaydos_Gillott_Hume.pdf` in the same folder, which is a mis-named duplicate. Subtitle: *Linguistically innovative poetry in and against the Anthropocene*.

This is a **poetics** use rather than an ontology-of-metaphor one, but it is the most sustained treatment of catachresis in the corpus and it names its source:

> "In their essay '"Queer, Wonderful Misunderstandings": Catachresis as Aesthetic in Contemporary Poetry,' Gardner offers the most explicit account of this poetics of erring or citational failure. For Gardner, the rhetorical device of catachresis, **defined as the intentional 'misapplication' of language or 'abuse of words'**, offers an '**anti-aesthetic**' through which innovative poetry fundamentally '**troubles the veridicality of language**'."

> "**catachresis goes against the grain of linguistic habit in order to undermine the normative conditions by which language-use succeeds or fails**", so that "**the misuse of words becomes part of a poetic practice, an aesthetic and political choice**".

With Sutherland on "wrong poetry", where catachresis is used to "**tax and stretch grammar, idiom, diction, propositions, echo, and utterance to a break point**", and Halberstam's "resistance through failure". **For the form-of-the-writing question (the stutter, echolalia built into the prose) this thesis is a precedent that is already in the folder and unread.**

### Deleuze and Guattari on Kafka: the strongest version of all, and it refuses the term

From a held item in `ScholarDownloads_Deleuze` ("Differential desiring practice: a path into a Deleuze inspired literary discourse"), quoting *Kafka: Toward a Minor Literature* and Kafka's diary of 1921, "**Metaphors are one of the things that make me despair of literature**":

> "**Kafka deliberately kills all metaphor, all symbolism, all signification, no less than all designation. Metamorphosis is the contrary of metaphor. There is no longer any proper sense or figurative sense, but only a distribution of states that is part of the range of the word.** The thing and other things are no longer anything but intensities."

That is not "the metaphor is as real as the thing". It is the abolition of the proper/figurative distinction altogether, which removes the ground on which a figure could be called lesser. It is also **the opposite pole from Haraway**, who keeps the trope and makes everything tropic; Deleuze and Guattari drop the trope and keep only states. **Two held texts, two opposite routes to the same refusal of hierarchy.**

And from a held item on Deleuze and Derrida in gardens, *A Thousand Plateaus* p. 305: "**that which one becomes becomes no less than the one that becomes**", with "**Writing is rhizomatic or a rhizome-book and connects with the world to the extent that it is not an image of the world.**"
