# PART ONE: WHAT THE BOOK ARGUES

## 1. The term, and what she means by it

**The redefinition.** "Where conventional conceptions of prostheses refer to devices that replace or augment impaired parts of the body for rehabilitation purposes, I broaden the scope to encompass both mechanical and organic prostheses, which, going further, will include **any material incorporated into the structure and performativity of the body** . . . that cannot be identified with what is understood as the substance of the biological self."

**Why "visceral".** "I use the term visceral prostheses to indicate both the embodied physicality and, more metaphorically, the 'stickiness' of prosthetic assemblies." That is one of only two places in 117,000 words where she flags a figure of her own as metaphorical. See section 10.

**The three domains she brings together**, which she says have been worked separately: disability, organ and tissue transplantation, and micro-biology, by which she means the microbiome and microchimerism. Her note 3 to the introduction explains the hyphen: "I am using the hyphenated term 'micro-biology' to distinguish it from the narrower discipline of microbiology which names the study of unicellular, multicellular or acellular micro-organisms. The latter includes the composition of the microbiome, but not microchimerism."

**What she claims is new.** "Perhaps the most innovative thrust of the enterprise concerns the least-known aspect of the problematic, specifically my development of the notion of microchimerism as a prosthetic process with implications for us all."

**The thesis in one sentence**, which is also the book's refrain: "In the twenty-first century, prosthetic embodiment can no longer be seen as the exceptional case but as the certain condition of us all."

**And the thing she immediately guards against**, which is the sentence to quote if only one is quoted: "Going well beyond the somewhat banal observation that many of us use spectacles, mobile phones or hearing aids as prosthetic enhancement, it speaks to domains where the relations of power might be undercut by the queering of corporeal norms."

## 2. The frame: Freud's dictum, and Haraway's question

She opens on Freud's "prosthetic god" (1961: 39) and reads the ambivalence out of it: the technology "had enabled the human race to acquire superhuman qualities that enhanced an already-exceptional status", but "did not, however, ensure happiness."

Then she sets transhumanism and posthumanism apart, and this distinction runs the whole book. Transhumanism seeks "an enhanced, even perfected, state of human being". Her alternative: "an embrace of a posthuman future that instead of seeking to supplement and transcend the existing order prefers to uncover the multifarious ways in which embodiment already exceeds its established boundaries and meshes with non-human entities."

**Haraway's question is quoted and dated.** "Donna Haraway's question 'Why should our bodies end at the skin?' (1991: 178) has become ever more relevant and has particular significance in the extended context of prostheses, which I understand as both exceeding the putatively 'natural' body and being located deep inside its very biology." So the question that titled her 2015 *Hypatia* article is cited here at p. 178 of *Simians, Cyborgs and Women*, and it is the hinge of this book too.

**A grammatical move she asks the reader to notice.** "And I emphasise 'what' here rather than 'who' to indicate that the posthuman no longer lines up human selfhood and human biology as the only facets that count."

## 3. The history she puts behind the term, with its numbers

"Prosthesis" entered English "in early-eighteenth-century medical texts, where it was used to denote the 'replacement of a missing part of the body with an artificial one'" (Wills 1995: 215). Earlier devices existed: wooden peg-legs, and "the discovery of prosthetic toes in ancient Egyptian tombs (Finch 2011)".

The modern surge she dates to the American Civil War, where "an estimated 60,000 men lost limbs alone", met by a US government programme named the "Great Civil War Benefaction", and publicised through "visual images fostered by glass-plate photography made available for public consumption."

The continuity she insists on: "To the present day, armed conflict and war have remained among the primary drivers of prosthetic technologies", naming DARPA's Revolutionizing Prosthetics initiative and the Neural Engineering System Design programme. And the distributional point: DARPA advances "percolate into the commercial market offering prosthetic relief, not so much to average people with disabilities, but to those able to afford cutting-edge technologies." Her summary: "The coming together of militarism, capitalism and consumerism in the history of prostheses is a recurring thread."

In her later chapters DARPA reappears twice: funding deep brain stimulation research "for deployment with battle-field soldiers, with use in the field of dementia being a secondary spin-off", and funding the 2021 frog-cell xenobots.

## 4. The theory, and the order she moves through it

She is explicit about the sequence and about abandoning each stage in turn, which is unusual and worth keeping.

**Merleau-Ponty first, then declared insufficient.** The cane passage is the hinge: the tip "is transformed into a sensitive zone, it increases the scope and the radius of the act of touching and has become analogous to a gaze" (1962: 178). She takes "the flesh of the world" (1968: 77) and "There is no inner man, man is in the world, and only in the world does he know himself" (1962: xii). Then the limit, with its critics named: "his rethinking of bodily difference is not as radical as his new slant seems to promise and remains rooted in the perspective of healthy and able-bodied adults" (Young 1984; Patterson and Hughes 1999; Reynolds 2017). And on her own research history: "much of the empirical data was examined initially . . . through the lens of an phenomenological analysis drawn from Merleau-Ponty . . . but over time has come to feel inadequate to the pointers to posthumanism that began to emerge strongly."

**Derrida second, for the supplement.** The quoted core: "The strange structure of the supplement appears here: by delayed reaction, a possibility produces that to which it is said to be added on" (1973: 89). Her application: "only something which is already inherently incomplete can be augmented . . . and technology constructs that which it purports to enhance." Peggy Kamuf's gloss, which she uses twice: a supplement is "at once something secondary, external, and compensatory, and something that substitutes, violates and usurps."

**And the sentence from this book that Donnarumma quotes**, here in its original context: "a prosthesis may both extend functional agency and radically destabilise specifically human agency as such." In this book it appears without the 2013 article's emphasis.

Derrida's hospitality carries the ethics: "the host thus becomes a retained hostage . . . responsible for and victim of the gift" (2000: 107) and "the guest becomes the host's host" (2000: 125), which she quotes three times across the book. And on technology, from *Points*: "technology has not simply added itself, from the outside or after the fact, as a foreign body . . . this foreign or dangerous supplement is 'originarily' at work and in place in the supposedly ideal interiority of the 'body and soul'. **It is indeed at the heart of the heart**" (1995: 244).

**Deleuze third, and she states exactly how much loyalty she gives him.** "I should stress, however, that I offer no loyalty to any canon, but simply rummage in the Deleuzian toolbox." The working concepts are assemblage, desiring machines, becoming, the body-without-organs, molar and molecular, and lines of flight. The quoted anchors: "a body populated by multiplicities" (1987: 30); the map "is open and connectable in all its dimensions; it is detachable, reversible, susceptible to constant modification" (1987: 12); Guattari's "(Desiring) machines arrange and connect flows. They do not recognize distinctions between persons, organs, material flows, and semiotic flows" (1996: 46), which she quotes twice; and the caution that subject effects persist, "you have to keep small rations of subjectivity . . . to enable you to respond to the dominant reality" (1987: 160).

**Somatechnics, defined, and credited with going further than Deleuze.** "I have found the scope of somatechnics – which goes where Deleuze never ventured – highly productive. At a basic level, somatechnics describes the irreducible interconnections and mutual influences of bodies, biologies and technologies, and indicates an approach to corporeality which considers it as always already bound up with a variety of technologies, techniques and technics."

**And her diagnosis of what body theory has not done**, which is the gap the book is written into: "scholarly analysis of the concept of crafting the body through substantive technological transformations has tended to become arrested at the stage of descriptive wonder. Stuart Murray's recent monograph *Disability and the Posthuman* (2020) is a welcome exception, but its insights are firmly situated – even as he pushes at the limits – within the field of disability studies."

# PART TWO: THE CHAPTERS

## 5. Chapter 1, disability and the prosthetic imaginary (pp. 23-43)

**What critical disability studies is, in her account**, as against the social model: CDS "has embraced postconventional thought and is more concerned with the phenomenology of the body and with contesting the modernist, and very Western-based, psycho-social imaginary that distinguishes between normative and non-normative categories of embodiment."

**The framing sentence she borrows and adapts from Sedgwick**, with the borrowing marked: disability is "far from being an unfortunate condition that can be ameliorated by mechanical aids, but rather the jump-off point for an adventure in applied posthumanism. This is not to belittle the discriminatory experiences of disabled people in mainstream society, but – as my adaptation of Eve Kosofsky Sedgwick's radical rethinking in *Tendencies* of the experience of her cancer as 'an adventure in applied deconstruction' (1994: 12) signals – to make a break with normative thinking."

**The reversal she makes on the wheelchair-user point**, which is the same argument as the 2015 article and is worth having in both versions. Here: "Although there is plentiful evidence of the desire to separate the embodied self from any technological aid – the mainstream disability community is careful to refer to a wheelchair user, for example – I would argue that that reflects a modernist mindset that pays scant attention to the phenomenology of embodied experience in which a prosthesis, whether high or low-tech, becomes integrated into selfhood."

**Her degree-not-kind formula, which recurs throughout the book**: "Present-day users of wearable technologies such as Fitbits or Smart Glasses may not see the connection to hearing aids or heart monitors but they are **different in degree of sophistication, not in kind**." Sobchack supplies the same form for her own amputation: "'Phantom' limbs, 'prosthetic' limbs, 'real' limbs: their difference is one of degree, not of kind – and less in function than in material substance" (2010: 63).

**Sobchack's three-part account** of her own post-amputation embodiment, which Shildrick numbers: "(1) the ongoing sense of her originary 'whole' corporeality as it was before amputation; (2) the absent presence of her 'phantom' limb – itself a psychic prosthesis; (3) the materiality of her 'real' manufactured prosthesis which can be forgotten, but may erupt into consciousness." And Sobchack's own line: "to some degree I am always aware of the different choreographies, bodily rhythms, and spaces that attach to and attend my use (or non-use) of my crutches or my prosthetic leg."

**The history of prosthetic normalisation, with its gender politics.** Fineman on the cartoonist Umbo: "Umbo reinvents the body as an assemblage of separate parts, tools, and devices, each corresponding to a particular and nonfungible function. His modern man, the prosthetic god, takes the form of a humanized Swiss army knife." Serlin on the Second World War in the United States: "the fiercely heterosexual culture of rehabilitation medicine, especially [in] its orthodox zeal to preserve the masculine status of disabled veterans." And Ott, that mid-century prosthetic success "was often measured in professional literature by the extent to which the wearer was enabled to engage in normal gender activities like dating, dancing and ultimately marriage."

**The artists, which is the part of this book closest to the art-practice reading in this folder.**

*Lisa Bufano*, American performer, "who underwent a bilateral below-the-knee and total finger-thumb amputation at twenty-one." Bufano in her own words: "My eye has always been drawn to abnormal forms . . . It's just that now my tool is my body. . . . being a performer with a deformity"; and "I find that there's a gut response in audiences, an attraction/repulsion aspect to it that can be compelling." Her brother after her death: "Lisa was easily engaged by seemingly futile efforts. She saw something beautiful in futility."

The work Shildrick reads at length is *Mentally Fine* (2010): Bufano in the window of an empty store-front in Boise, Idaho, "performing on her elongated prosthetic giraffe-like legs, which are shaped like those of Queen Anne chairs." Two women passers-by are filmed. One, having established Bufano's agency as an artist, "exclaims in relief 'mentally fine then?', implicitly making a conventional distinction and related assertion that cognitive disability is less acceptable than a physical impairment." Shildrick does not leave it there: "Good liberal viewers of the video will no doubt laugh, but the passer-by then goes on to mention that she was once faced with an amputation herself, indicating that perhaps her reduction of the performance to normative epistemologies masks a moment of communality."

Also: Graeae theatre company's 2017 *The House of Bernarda Alba*, which "seamlessly interwove deaf and hearing actors with sign language, surtitles and speech"; Claire Cunningham performing on crutches; the aerialist Erin Ball working "both with and without her prosthetic lower limbs"; and DV8 Physical Theatre's *The Cost of Living* (2004), with David Toole, "who has just two limbs, his arms", dancing with a non-disabled colleague. Her reading of the last: "What is surprising is not that Toole can move his body with as much grace and control as the other dancers, but that he is fully integrated – without explanation or apology – into their display. DV8 are no utopian idealists, however, and the performance also reminds us that the disabled man in his wheelchair is initially very much on the outside of artistic endeavour."

**Her claim about creativity**, which decouples it from the individual: "Creativity can no longer be thought as a quality of a singular body, but as the energy and affect that flows between multiple bodies, locations, resources and ideas."

**And the list that appears in the 2015 article, reused here in a different argument.** In the *Hypatia* piece it is the athlete-as-event list at the medal ceremony. Here it is the network behind a disabled artist's work: "healthcare professionals; modes of education; the designers of prostheses; **the specific national benefits system that impedes or supports embodied difference; the paid assistants who facilitate personal care**; the existing bio-imaginaries of the recipients of the art work; and many more." Same two clauses, verbatim in substance, moved from athletics to art. Recorded as a reuse, not as a fault.

**The limits she puts on her own argument in this chapter.** On how many disabled people would want it: "I am not arguing that all disabled people have a postmodernist view of embodiment . . . Most users are probably fairly conservative, but the more adventurous – and, to be pragmatic, the more privileged – have the option of creating new worlds for themselves." On geography, quoting Erevelles: "What does it mean to come to terms with the transgressive vagaries of . . . assemblage – precarious/partial/body-without-organs/liminal/affective/molecular – within political economic contexts imbricated in colonial/neocolonial practices of unrelenting social, economic, and militarized violence?" On what she is not claiming: "my contention is not that visceral prostheses are tools to deliver social justice, but rather to enable a new imaginary of embodied forms." And Wolbring's warning, which she endorses: "the introduction of every new technology has led to a new group of marginalised people and to new inequalities."

**The Jaipur Foot**, her one extended non-Western case: "invented in 1968 as a cheaply produced mobility device . . . It proved to be superior in flexibility and durability to the more sophisticated models and had the local advantage of allowing users to walk barefoot if that was their practice. It is still in widespread use and costs under $35." Her point is that it serves a different value: "As a rehabilitation technology, it undoubtedly aids the push for independence and personal autonomy, but those attributes are not the primary goals in Indian society. Even under neoliberalism, family and communality have a higher value, and that form of inclusion is what the Jaipur Foot enables."

**Spivak supplies the formula for the ambivalence she keeps**: "a persistent critique of what one cannot not want" (1993: 42). Her gloss: "Insofar as rights can bestow social value and help pull oppressed people everywhere out of dehumanising situations, they should be supported as well as contested."

**Gibson's ventilator studies** are her low-key cases. On one man: he is "a fluid body . . . a conglomeration of energies. He has replaceable parts. . . . His organs are here, there, and everywhere", and an "electrified body flowing through power lines connected to the hydroelectric dam, receiving power (desire) from the river, from gravity that motivates the river". On a twelve-year-old girl and her mother: "their selves connect and merge into assemblages and later disconnect and reconnect with others to form different assemblages. Within these assemblages there are no clear distinction between persons or between persons and technologies."

Her notation, which she explains: "I use the slash to indicate the inadequacy of the usual term", writing dis/abled.

## 6. Chapter 2, transplantation and the PITH project (pp. 45-69)

**This is the chapter with the empirical base, and the numbers matter.**

The PITH project (Process of Incorporating a Transplanted Heart), Toronto-based, REB file # 07-0822-BE. Endnote 7 names the whole team: Heather Ross (medical director of the transplant clinic), Susan Abbey (director of psychiatry), Jennifer Poole (mental health researcher), Patricia McKeever (medical sociologist), Shildrick herself (biophilosopher), two nurse-trained researchers Oliver Mauthner and Enza DeLuca, "several visual artists – Alexa Wright, Ingrid Bachman, Andrew Carnie, Dana del Po, Emily Jan – and cultural theorist Tammer El-Sheik." The follow-on project was GOLA (Gift of Life Analysis), with donor proxies.

**The PITH figures, all from this chapter.** 25 recipients interviewed, mixed age, gender and ethnicity, all one to nine years post-transplant, **all medically and psychologically stable**. Of the 25: **17 spoke of the heart through a machine model**; **20 expressed or displayed distress or dysphoria** about the donor, their own identity, or both; **19 of the 25 video records showed clear incongruities between spoken words and embodied expression**. That cohort was "well over 90 per cent of the eligible interviewees" in the area. And the comparison in endnote 10: "Using a self-report symptom checklist, Dew and DiMartini (2005) identified substantial distress in just 33 per cent of their sample of transplant recipients, but our results are significantly higher at near 80 per cent."

**Why the method matters**, and this is the methodological passage in the book most transferable to other work. Interviews were "not only audio-taped but also video-recorded to capture the bodies of both the participants and something of the environment in which the interview took place." Recipients chose the location, most choosing home. "In most interviews, then, the recording captures speech, posture and dress, bodily gestures, environmental artefacts and the embodied interaction between interviewer and interviewee." Heath supplies the warrant: "(t)hrough gesture, bodily comportment and talk, (respondents) render visible what would otherwise remain hidden and unavailable for inspection."

Endnote 15 classifies the incongruities: "upgrades where recipients displayed guarded body comportment or distressed expressions while voicing positive feelings ('I'm a 100 per cent satisfied' said in an assertive voice while the speaker held herself in a comforting hug), or downgrades in which body language was assessed as non-distressed and open, despite the markedly negative content of verbal communication (the laughing participant who spoke of the 'expiry date' of the transplanted organ)."

**Two scenes she reports in full, because the objects did the talking.** A woman three years post-transplant "chose to be interviewed under a prominent wall sign spelling out the word 'Hope'", and spoke "in a slow pained monotone, but by constantly banging a water bottle on her own lap, albeit the message she was attempting to convey to the interviewer was one of survival and hope." And a man "who was highly successful in deflecting the interviewer's questions . . . used the medium of taking apart a heart-sized and shaped Inuit puzzle sculpture to visually signal his fear that his own 'broken heart' could not be put together again."

**The recipients' own words she quotes.** "Somebody dies to make someone live, you know . . . You think about it all the time." "I don't know how to say thank you . . . it's not . . . not enough, it's not enough . . . I really owe them something." "there must be some, maybe a memory chip, in that heart that came from that person and came to me . . . it doesn't really match mine". The male recipient "who reported that he had acquired a preference for pink clothing and felt therefore that his donor must be a gay man." "The heart belongs to that person, so I'm not alive, he's alive, he's alive in the shape of me." And the one that names the contradiction: "the transplant program really tries hard to get us to objectify the organ. And treat it just as though, just as an organ", but at the same time "this girl whose heart I have, um, she's here in me".

**The LVAD finding, which runs against the chapter's own grain and which she flags as against expectation.** Recipients maintained on left ventricular assist devices "were largely unfazed by their hybrid embodiment, and in some cases jokingly saw it as conferring superpower status. The users still felt self-complete and the somatechnics of LVADs were not a source of ontological anxiety." One woman: "I'm going to have withdrawals when I do get the heart . . . it's my little friend, it's keeping me alive. . . . I think of it as a machine, but it's part of me." Another, anticipating an organic graft: "I'll have to give back the cape!" Her reading of the asymmetry, via Haraway: the cyborg "is lived as posthuman, whereas transplant and implant recipients are unsupported in the context of an imaginary that can recognise only the human."

**The gift, three ways.** Mauss: "to accept something from somebody is to accept some part of his spiritual essence, of his soul" (1990: 12) and "The objects are never completely separated from the men who exchange them" (1990: 31). Diprose's corporeal generosity: the gift "exceeds . . . contractual relations between individuals", and "intercorporeal generosity maintains alterity and ambiguity in the possibilities it opens . . . generosity is only possible if neither sameness nor unity is assumed as either the basis or the goal of an encounter with another" (2002: 90-1); Shildrick adds the caution that "Diprose is not proposing a rerun of early second wave feminist touchy-feely notions of mutuality." Derrida: "(f)or there to be a gift, there must be no reciprocity, return, exchange, counter-gift or debt" (1992: 12).

**Her own coinage**, used 16 times across the book: concorporeality. "The term intercorporeality moves in a posthumanist direction, but it nevertheless preserves an unhelpful sense of self and other." Her version: "a mode of concorporeality that would include the hybridity of the heart transplant recipient and donor organ." And she marks the grammar: "The embrace of concorporeality – and I deliberately leave the verb both active and passive – is open to all those who abandon the privilege of normative embodiment."

**Nancy's *L'Intrus*, read carefully and against the obvious reading.** The opening is not about the graft: "L'Intrus opens with a deliberation on the in-coming of the stranger, which to our surprise is not a metaphor for the grafted heart, but Nancy's own failing heart as it had become an element threatening the previously assumed integrity of his embodiment", now "an elsewhere 'in' me" (2002: 6). Nancy on the gift: "the whole dubious symbolism of the gift of the other – a secret, ghostly complicity or intimacy between the other and me – wears out very quickly" (2008: 166). And the list: "I am the cancerous cell and the grafted organ, I am the immuno-depressive agents and their palliatives, I am the bits of wire that hold together my sternum, and I am this injection site permanently stitched in below my clavicle, just as I was already these screws in my hip and this plate in my groin. . . . We are . . . the beginnings of a mutation: man recommences going infinitely beyond man" (2002: 13). The conclusion she takes: "the intrus is none other than me, my self."

**And she states her own position on the practice, which is not neutral.** "Faced with the evidence of the PITH and GOLA projects that both recipients and donor proxies are deeply disordered by their experiences, I have long felt highly sceptical towards claims that transplantation is an irreproachable moral good."

## 7. Chapter 3, micro-biologies (pp. 71-101)

**The chapter opens with an ethical self-objection she does not resolve**, which is worth noting because it is unusual in a theory book. On animal models: "I wonder if there is not an irresolvable contradiction in promoting the commensality of such life forms in a way that goes far beyond the simple biological data while at the same time referencing laboratory procedures that habitually use animal models." She states where she lands: "I am not – given my own rejection of binary solutions – a fully committed abolitionist but hold what the Nuffield Council Report (2005) calls the 'moral dilemma view'. It is not an ethically comfortable position." And: "I am not suggesting that experimental science alone has a case to answer – we are all complicit."

**Grosz supplies the argument for going into biology at all**: "feminist theory has protected and insulated itself from any incursions into biology through the fear, indeed paranoia, surrounding the question of essentialism, though biology is one of the few disciplines able to adequately contest essentialism." And Shildrick's own guard: "I am not suggesting that biology trumps other concerns: it is no less a political discourse than any other and – despite the illusions of many practitioners – is generative of partial truths at best, not an objective finality."

**Microbiome figures, with the revision she records rather than hiding.** Around 10,000 microbial species in the body, the largest proportion in the gut. "Early claims that microbes, measured by cell number, constitute approximately 90 per cent of human bodies (Gilbert, Sapp and Tauber 2012) are now considered unconvincing, and more recent estimates – based solely on bacterial presence – suggest a ratio of 1:1 for human to non-human cells (Gilbert et al 2018), or at most around 1:3 if all microbial elements are taken into account. **The revision is substantial but it makes little difference to the basic argument**: that we are not singular or bounded entities, but are materialised as genetically multiple." Less than 30 per cent of microbial components are found consistently across all healthy bodies of the same species. The Human Microbiome Project was funded by the NIH 2008-2016.

**Microchimerism, defined and quantified.** "a small but significant presence of so-called non-self human cells co-existing with a dominant population of self cells in the same body" carrying different DNA. "Strictly speaking, microchimerism indicates that no more than 1 in 1,000 cells is genetically distinct from the majority, but in some cases such cells may come to predominate in a particular organ or tissue as well as circulating in low numbers throughout the body."

The chimera/hybrid distinction she insists on, with the geep: "in a hybrid each cell consists in a combination of genes, while in a chimera each individual cell will contain genes from only one of the originating organisms." And the consequence: "the chimera alone escapes the reproductive dead-end of the hybrid."

The known routes, as she lists them: transplantation, pregnancy, non-irradiated blood transfusions, bone marrow transplants, the fusion of two zygotes (tetragametic chimerism), "with further suggestions that lactation and fluid sexual exchanges can also generate the phenomenon." Her own extension: an intergenerational cycle, worked out in the first person in chapter 6.

**The immunology she takes apart.** Medawar and Burnet's self/non-self model, with Medawar's own term quoted from 1960: "'immunological tolerance' may be described as a state of indifference or non-reactivity towards a substance that would normally be expected to excite an immunological response." Her historical point: "In formalising a theory of self/non-self antagonism, it was necessary to dismiss evidence of what is now called microchimerism", which Medawar called "a 'natural accident'" and "astonishing".

Two alternative models she presents fairly and does not pick between. Matzinger's danger model: "the emphasis is shifted from the trope of invasion to one of interruption in the smooth flow of internal communication." Pradeu's continuity model: a response is triggered by "the sudden appearance in the organism of antigenic patterns strongly different from those with which the immune system continuously (i.e., regularly) interacts", so "It is not the foreignness of an antigen that invokes a response but the discontinuity in forms of expression"; Pradeu's aim, quoted, is "to open up the immune system to its environment instead of viewing it as exclusively self-centred."

**Two cases.** H. pylori, "currently resident in around 50 per cent of the world's population, usually from childhood, and yet as few as one in ten people experience any ill-health as a result." Her reading: "It is not only a leap of faith to name H. pylori as the causative factor of gastric disorder, but indicative of a failure to consider what other changes have occurred to convert a hitherto harmless bacteria into something destructive."

And the Alexander case, an Australian emergency liver transplant in a nine-year-old girl "whose whole immunological response realigned itself with that of the male deceased donor, with her blood group switching from O-negative to O-positive." Immunosuppression was withdrawn after chimerism was found by chance at nine months; "an assay of 250 peripheral blood cells at post-transplantation day 492 showed 'all of these cells were male'." The clinical report, quoted in its own register: "(t)he patient remains well [five] years after transplantation. She has not received any immunosuppressive therapy for [four] years." Her comment on the field's response: "most subsequent studies have avoided upsetting orthodoxies by failing to pick up its radical implications."

**Starzl's "epistemologic collapse"**, quoted: the solid organ graft had been understood "as an island in a hostile sea in which the leukocytes were solely those of the recipient" (2007: 15).

**Her own original speculation, which she flags as unanswered.** The vagus nerve is cut in heart transplantation, and Fülling et al describe the microbiota influencing the brain "by hijacking vagus nerve signalling", with vagal ablation raising anxiety disorders. "What is surprising then is why there appears to be nothing published on the effects of cutting the vagus nerve as an inevitable by-product of heart transplantation. As that has been my own major area of research, I have asked many research scientists and clinicians for insights into the question, but without success." Her hypothesis, with its own hedge: "Unrestrained by such conventions I wonder if damage to the vagus offers a partial explanation of the deep distress experienced post-transplant by the majority of the PITH respondents. . . . I say that explanation is 'partial' advisedly as it would be misleading to suggest that experience can be measured by empirical data alone."

**Esposito, and the etymology that carries the chapter's ethics.** Immunity and community "have a common root in the Latin *munus* which means an obligation or even a gift. . . . The one who is immune is exonerated from reciprocal gift giving and stands as an autonomous individual." Esposito: "immunity, which is necessary for protecting our lives, if carried past a certain threshold, winds up negating it" (2013: 61); and the maternal-foetal relation as "the figure of a cohabitation with difference, an emblem for a different means of thinking about community . . . not just a defensive system, but a system of 'self-alteration'" (2011: 169).

Derrida on autoimmunity, which she sets against Esposito without deciding: "without autoimmunity . . . nothing would ever happen or arrive; we would no longer wait, await, or expect, no longer expect one another, or expect any event" (2003: 152). And Deutscher's summary, which she quotes and then declines to accept in that order: "It is because we are open to disaster that we are open to transformation. But the reverse also must hold. If we are open to transformation, we are open to disaster." Shildrick: "I whole heartedly agree but would want to reverse the emphasis of her equation."

**Endnote 32 records the reception of her own work**, and it is the sharpest thing in the notes: "My own first presentation on the implications of microchimerism was met with a dismissive denial by a biochemist of the very possibility of such a process. After I referred him to a plethora of bioscientific papers backing up my claims, his response was that **if it was ubiquitous, then there was nothing interesting to say about it**."

## 8. Chapters 4, 5 and 6, the three "ecosystems"

**Chapter 4, dementia (pp. 107-127).** WHO 2020 figures as she gives them: around 50 million people have dementia; 5 to 8 per cent of the population aged sixty and over; "nearly 60 per cent living in low- and middle-income countries."

Four categories of care robot: rehabilitation, service, telepresence, companion. **PARO**, the harp-seal robot, "currently estimated to be present in 80 per cent of Danish care institutions, despite its high cost of over $6000 for each unit." The Moyle Australian study ran PARO against a Plush Toy (disclosed as "actually PARO with all the functions disabled") and usual care, for ten weeks with a fifteen-week follow-up; over 50 per cent of residents with dementia in those facilities were reported to have aggression, agitation, vocal disruption or chronic mood disturbance. The Thodberg Danish study compared a dog, PARO and a soft toy cat across 124 residents in "twelve ten-minute visits". The Aarskog review covered fifteen studies.

Her objection is to the evaluative frame, not the devices: "The engagement and pleasure that PARO – and to a certain extent Plush Toy – evoked in residents were simply treated as a means to a definitively human-centred end, rather than as a demonstration of the constrictions of an anthropocentric outlook." And: "Had the human–machine sensory interactions been seen as valuable in their own right . . . then the abrupt withdrawal of PARO would surely constitute a serious ethical misstep."

**The touch argument**, which is where the chapter's own contribution lies: "residents with either moderate or severe dementia may become non-verbal, while touch – which is the very first perceptual sense to develop (Anzieu 1989) – remains relatively unimpaired." And: "unlike sight, touch crosses the boundaries of the proper rather than creating distance. It is precisely where the ontological separation of self and other; human and animal; animal and machine; living and non-living might be overcome."

**The cross-cultural material**, quoted from Calo and colleagues: "in Asian countries, Japan and Korea, people accepted Paro as a pet, but not for therapy. In European countries, the UK, Sweden, and Italy, people accepted Paro for therapy, but not as a pet. In the US and Brunei, people accepted Paro as a pet and for therapy." And the Japanese concept *boke*, via Traphagan: "a 'fundamentally antisocial' debility that is stigmatised not for the cognitive and physical failure it may accompany but for the moral failure it signifies", so that "What is at stake, then, is the failure to maintain the self as an intersubjective and irreducibly connected entity."

She is willing to be rude about a device: "For all the hype, PaPeRo is rigid, very slow to move or respond, and – if intended to mimic human behaviour – entirely unconvincing. I am not used to my companions randomly breaking into song or dance, and would be slightly alarmed should they do so." And then the serious version: "Either the conditions of dementia genuinely infantilise or that is the only way that those who provide care environments can make sense of the changed affects and capacities."

**And she corrects a cited author gently.** "DeFalco seems to have mistaken PARO for a dog, but it matters little when she writes that animal robots 'raise the spectre of queer, destabilizing intimacies that cast doubt on the very condition of the human'."

The micro-biology she brings to dementia, with the puzzle left open: "The unresolved puzzle is why Parkinson's disease appears to correlate with a higher than average incidence of brain microchimeras, while research into Alzheimer's disease shows the exact opposite that higher levels of foetal cells in the brain are found in those who do not have the disease (Chan et al 2012)." Plus Fox et al on pregnancy reducing Alzheimer's risk, and the Biagi finding that centenarians' microbiomes are "significantly different and more diverse."

**Chapter 5, stem cell transplantation (pp. 129-147).** The empirical base is the Lübeck project, and endnote 2 names the team and the number: "a sibling stem cell project which interviewed **seventeen families**", with thanks to Christina Schües, Christoph Reimann-Sutter, Martina Jürgensen and Madeleine Herzog at the University of Lübeck, whose edited collection the data belongs to. All recipients and most donors were children at the time; respondents were interviewed "up to twenty years after the transplant"; **two recipients out of sixteen died** in the early years after transplantation; **three donors** had undergone more than one extraction.

**The ethical core, stated flatly**: "The irreducible moral problem is that the extraction of stem cells has the potential to harm the donor without offering any personal therapeutic benefit. The recipient on the other hand may face death if what is a relatively risk-free medical procedure for the donor is not carried out."

The families' own words, which are the strongest material in the book after the PITH quotations. The Zucker mother: "It was clear to me, without us having a massive discussion with Zorro, that Zorro WILL donate whether he wants to or not." The Kunow father "ruefully admits that he thought of his donor son as a 'spare parts depot'", while the Wahl father refers to one child "as a construction site for the other." The Rohde donor: "now my sister is (laughs), yes, the same as me, how stupid, now I'm no longer unique or something." The Speidal mother: "it was like this, he [the recipient] gets his brother's blood, so he BECOMES his brother", and the recipient: "you've got your brother's blood actually IN you. Like and, mh, yes, it's FUNNY to describe. It's as if you were linked even more closely than you would be anyway." The Wahl male donor on the procedure: "like a birth or like a (..), like a sort of transfer of now- now- as if life is somehow flowing in right now or something and that COMES from ME." The Rohde father, the only direct mention of the term by any participant: "you know I find the concept so crazy, the doctors are talking about chimerism . . . it's from, comes from the Greek chimera."

**And the passage that is hardest to read**, a female recipient in the Kirstein family: "well [I was] also very depressed and then I tried to kill myself and um my brother could never approve of that like, because he said: I SAVED your life and now you just want to throw it away!" Shildrick's comment is one sentence and does not editorialise: "the putative altruism of the gift blocks open relationality and the recipient referred to here laments that her brother was unconcerned with her reasons for attempting suicide."

The Bahr mother and daughter exchange, in endnote 7: "D: I still think it was right, but for me as a child I was still aware: if I don't do this, I'm to blame. M: Exactly, if Björn dies, exactly."

**The argument about SCT that makes the chapter**: microchimerism is not incidental to bone marrow transplant, it is the point of it. "unlike the situation with solid organ transplants, where the dominant clinical narrative still insists that the DNA of the graft will remain in situ and play no part in recovery and future life . . . the biomedical point of stem cell transplants is exactly the opposite." And: "Microchimerism may not be named, but it is at the heart of the whole enterprise." Hence: "biomedicine has been successfully exploiting the phenomenon in bone marrow transplant for over fifty years", while "the most notable challengers to the orthodoxy – Donna Haraway (1989b), Polly Matzinger (2001) and Thomas Pradeu (2012) – offer no significant commentary as to the startling implications and ongoing viability of bone marrow transplants."

And the consent question she raises from it: "One might ask whether the omission of explanatory information about issues that may affect both future health and existential well-Being raises the question – at least in its conventional sense – of what constitutes informed consent, especially where children are the recipients and donors for whom proxy decisions are being made."

Wanner et al, quoted for a figure: "eleven of the twelve unrelated donors who were interviewed wondered if they shared characteristics other than HLA with their recipients and had become their 'genetic twins' following SCT."

**The family as assemblage, and the point where she says it is not one.** The Bahr mother: "something very special connects us, what we experienced together, what we endured and survived." The Kötter father: "a family is something . . . something priceless (…) because actually they're all part of oneself." Her reading, which withholds the term: "in that sense they satisfy the English language definition of assemblage as the fitting together of disparate parts to form a unified whole, but **not the full Deleuzian sense** in which the original word *agencement* indicates a loosely linked array of heterogeneous elements." And the paradox she names: "the implicit rejection of radical difference in the desire for kin stem cells at one level defines an immunitary unit at odds with the communitarian sharing that SCT implies."

Endnote 9 gives the German costs in 2018: over €100,000 for an autologous bone marrow transplant, over €200,000 for a related allogeneic transplant, more than €250,000 for an unrelated donor. Endnote 11 gives Pillay et al: 68 per cent of donors name emotional distress as the most challenging aspect, against 41 per cent naming physical discomfort, and she adds that "The authors' account of what constitutes emotional distress is, however, very thin."

**Chapter 6, genetic origins and surrogacy (pp. 149-167).** The Human Genome Project "officially got underway in the early 1990s", completed 2003, claiming "99.99 per cent of the genes of the human genome"; the ELSI programme "had a 5 per cent stake in the total budget and ran alongside the HGP for its duration, it was set up as a separate entity with an unclear mandate in terms of influence."

**The chimerism cases, in order, and they are the clearest narrative sequence in the book.**

*Mrs McK, 1953*, "the first human case being reported in 1953 (Dunsford et al: 81) when a Mrs McK donated blood that was found to be of two different types" — from an in utero exchange with a dizygotic twin brother. "Mrs McK was simply a scientific puzzle – 'an experiment of surpassing interest' according to those who investigated."

*Lydia Fairchild*, whose "maternity of her own 'natural' child could not be verified when she submitted a blood sample for genetic testing as part of a stringent welfare application. She was under suspicion of fraud, and even kidnapping", resolved when it emerged "that the DNA of her blood was quite different to the DNA of her reproductive organs."

*Karen Keegan*, whose three sons tested as two being "genetically unrelated to their mother, although they were genetic brothers."

*Sheets et al 2018*: repeated negative paternity results resolved by microarray, which "uncovered not the expected status of genetic father, but an avuncular (uncle/nephew) relationship", the man being tetragametic chimeric from an unborn male twin.

*The Alaska case*: semen from a sexual assault matched a man already in the police database who was in prison at the time; "his brother, who had been a sibling bone marrow donor for the original suspect shared an identical blood DNA profile – though a buccal swab showed up a different DNA profile – was the actual attacker."

**The practical point she draws, which is the chapter's most actionable claim**: "microchimerism is irregularly distributed throughout the body. Blood or buccal swab tests are relatively unobtrusive and cheap to administer, but the DNA obtained there may not match that of reproductive organs, for example, or other potential test sites." Applied to immigration: "DNA testing, which is increasingly becoming the technology of choice to settle contentious immigration claims in countries of the global North, is deployed not so much to legitimate claims to citizenship rights, as to facilitate exclusion and the denial of rights." And her limit: "I am not suggesting that doubt should be shed on all DNA identification, but where there is no corroborating evidence and strong denial, it should not be beyond the remit of the state to investigate the possibility of microchimerism." She calls Kaye's dismissal of such cases as exceptional "wilfully complacent."

**Her own intergenerational worked example, in the first person**: "any male DNA circulating in my own body may originate from a great uncle whose gestation period preceded that of his sister (my grandmother). She in turn passed on XY cells to any subsequent female foetuses of her own, including my mother, setting up an intergenerational cycle of microchimerism."

**The Irish case**, which is the chapter's legal centre. *R. v. An tArd Chláraitheoir* (2013), Dublin High Court, Abbott J. "At that time, and still as I write in 2020, Ireland had no specific laws relating to surrogacy so any judgement sets a precedent." Experts in epigenetic biology mostly "agreed that gestation conferred an unbreakable epigenetic link that justified the nomination of motherhood, even if that posited more than one mother." Abbott J. nonetheless found for the intending parents, ruling that "the influence of such epigenetic occurrences is not of such significance as to alter the overriding significance of chromosomal DNA for the purpose of determining identity and inherited characteristics", and adding that he thought it unlikely the "deterministic quality of chromosomal DNA" would ever be trumped. The expert witness Dr Molony said microchimerism "does not change the core DNA of the child", and Abbott J. quoted a concession that microchimeric cells were "'red herrings' in the genetic scenario". The Supreme Court reversed and reinstated the surrogate as legal mother, but "did not, however, cite the epigenetic – still less microchimeric – evidence as a reason for the reversal, simply saying that any change in the customary approach was a matter for the legislature not the courts."

Shildrick's answer to the testimony is one line: "Contemporary bioscience disagrees; both epigenetics and microchimerism have life-long and heritable genetic effects."

**The placenta material**, which is her strongest non-Western section: Young and Benyshek "investigated practices in over 170 different cultures – ranging across every habitable continent – and found that the dominant practices in traditional societies were highly concerned with following the correct rituals in the disposal of the placenta after birth", many believing "the organ was a child's kin – sometimes a twin, but also a mother or grandmother or some other form of protector". "In Maori culture the term for placenta is the same as that for earth – *whenua* – and burial was seen as an act of significance for the whole community." Against which: "In modern medicine postpartum placental material is treated as clinical waste, or even a biohazard."

**One claim she marks as dubious and quotes anyway**, from a US surrogacy agency's website telling prospective gestational mothers that surrogacy increases life expectancy: "Over 80% of women with the chromosome lived until the age of 80, while just over 65% of those without . . . lived an equal amount of time". Her comment: "The statistics may be dubious, but the message is clear: surrogacy is good for you because microchimerism confers individual benefit."

**And the sentence that names what surrogacy is in her frame, without softening it**: "It is effectively a case of one individual using the body of the other as a prosthetic aid." With the general version: "the transactions are rarely ones of equal exchange that benefit all, but the relegation of some bodies into visceral prostheses for others."

## 9. Chapters 7 and 8, death, and the ethics

**Chapter 7, temporalities of life and death (pp. 173-194).** The GOLA numbers: **22 donor proxies interviewed, all of whom had consented**, drawn from three Canadian provinces. The Donor Wall at the PITH hospital: "a display of photographs memorialising **sixty** previous donors", passed on the way into the post-transplant clinic. Shah, Kasper and Miller's review of **43** geographically diverse papers found that families cross-culturally "possess hope for a miracle or the belief that their family member may recover after brain death."

**The history of the definition of death, with its dates.** "Until the 1960s, death was defined as the failure of the cardio-respiratory system." The ventilator broke that, and in **1967** the definition shifted to brain death. Peter Singer, who approved it, is quoted against it: the new definition of death was "an ethical choice masquerading as a medical fact" (1995: 50). Shildrick's verdict: "the adoption of the brain dead criterion was always a fudge." In **2008** the US President's Commission amended it so that destruction of the brain constituted death because the person could no longer engage in "commerce with the surrounding world", of which she says: "It is far from self-evident what that phrase actually means."

Two anomalies she presses. The **Lazarus sign**, where a brain-dead body may raise its arms over the head and lower them onto the chest; the biomedical explanation is a spinal reflex arc. Her question: "we might pause to wonder at the justification of excluding such phenomena which though uncommon in 'death' are an intrinsic part of everyday living." And the octopus, which has "effectively no brain at all, but simply a dispersed network of neurons throughout the body", and yet "is thought to have the intelligence of a young human child", so: "Should the diverse evolutionary paths really lead us to suppose that human reflex has no connection to such higher functions?"

**Heart beating transfer**, which she notes will undo the clinical certainty: "new techniques – called heart beating transfer – now obviate the need to stop the heart at all. . . . In short, the graft continues beating throughout, and in such cases **cardiac death as such never occurs**. If for the clinical staff the putative crossing point of the life/death binary is what makes the whole procedure ethically acceptable, it is possible that the new technique will disrupt that certainty once it becomes more widely used."

**The donor proxy quotations, which are the book's rawest material.** Kathleen Fenton, quoted: "society as a whole is not completely comfortable with the idea that a warm, pink patient is actually a corpse." A mother: "I think he died in front of me", "only to be told that her son had already been dead for several hours." The 80-year-old retired professor: "I looked down at him and said that's not my husband . . . the body is just cells . . . they're not the person." Parents of a deceased daughter: "We were a little bit on pins and needles to make sure it [donation] happened." A mother on the staff: they "were very professional and personal at the same time", but "in the back of my mind I kept wondering was he really gone." Two bereaved sisters who had seen their brother's legs twitching and a tear fall from his face, on the consent process: "like a pushy used car salesman". A bereaved husband: "like seagulls circling". A mother still traumatised thirty-six months later, whose daughter "looked like she was just asleep": "I just wanted to punch them in the face . . . That's your child being carved up to be doled out to other people." Another: "you listen but you don't hear . . . it's gut wrenching", and "I was having nightmares . . . when they were removing his heart and eyes, he was screaming". And one more: "I felt some-one was surgically removing my heart".

On living on: "there's my sister's heart beating away in someone else, bringing them joy"; "I haven't lost him – I know exactly where he is . . . he's still giving"; "He lives on, he got to share himself with strangers"; and the parents of a woman killed in a light aircraft crash, "Ruth [the recipient] is a little piece of Lisa [their daughter] . . . if something happened to her, it would be another death for Lisa."

**The case she gives most space to**, and she handles the categories carefully. A young First Nations man, called Eric in the text, who had drowned and been temporarily revived; interviews with the estranged non-indigenous mother separately from the indigenous husband and daughter. Her caution first: "it is important to mark the non-Western context of the narrative, indigeneity is a somewhat uncomfortable blanket term that perpetuates a typically Western form of categorisation. In Canada, indigenous peoples may identify with the very different cultures of Métis, Inuit or First Nations, none of which should be seen as uniform in themselves." Endnote 13: "In the interests of anonymity, the name has been changed and the specific First Nations identification not given."

Eric "was himself in training to become a traditional elder". The father resisted consent, producing "an eight-day gap between the declaration of brain death and donation", and changed his mind after a drumming ceremony at the lake where Eric drowned: "two loons on the lake that spoke to the calmness of Eric's spirit, a crow that flew up overhead in response to a prayer for a sign from Mother Earth, and finally coming across a seemingly dead, but still sprouting tree which signalled the intertwining of life and death." The two beliefs he held at once: "his traditional First Nation beliefs were opposed to donation because the body is cherished (as a gift in itself), the self is transcendent, and not least because the deceased person's eyes were needed to show the way to the spirit world. On the other hand, he firmly believed that both Eric and the recipients would be enabled to live on through donation." Five lives, through the distribution of his organs. The father "emphasised how he would like to ask the heart recipient if he ever wanted to ride a horse or climb mountains as his son did." The mother: "I love talking about Eric . . . I hope he hears me."

**The sentence she takes as the chapter's turn**, from the retired professor despite her "cool rationalism": "It's no longer life, it's potential." Set beside Deleuze: "Death has an extreme and definite relation to me and my body and is grounded in me, but it also has no relation to me at all – it is incorporeal and infinitive, impersonal, grounded only in itself" (1990: 151).

**The anonymity argument.** Identifying text is "redacted at source"; "speaking of the letters reduced many of the interviewees to tears and marked a focal point of overt disturbance for givers and receivers alike", and endnote 9 to chapter 8 puts a number on it: "**Over 80 per cent of the PITH recipient cohort** . . . was reduced to tears of distress or despair in describing their struggles to write the anonymous letter implicitly demanded by their receiving the gift of life." Davies on the UK: "attempts by either donor or recipient to construct a social relationship is viewed by professionals as pathological, something to be treated through counselling."

Wasson supplies the formula for what commodification leaves behind: "Alienation of any object – severing it from its original context and classifying it in new ways that enable it to be transacted – leaves a remnant of strange 'life' in the alienated object: ghostly traces of the labour and the relations of production that enabled its manufacture." And Lindberg: "the imperceptible transformation of gratuitous organs into precious commodities is one of the big taboos of the transplantation medicine."

**Miscarriage, which is the quietest and possibly most useful passage in the chapter.** Gowland: "The cells of the foetus have a functional lifespan even if the foetus itself fails to develop." Shildrick's extension, which she says Gowland does not make: "To know that the intuitive, but often silenced, psycho-social sense that the child lives on in a different medium is supported by bioscience may be of great comfort to women in counter-balancing feelings of bereavement."

Freeman's **chrononormativity** supplies the term for what she is displacing, used four times.

**Chapter 8, the (bio)ethics of a new imaginary (pp. 195-210).** The transhumanism/posthumanism split, with the Transhumanist Declaration (2002) quoted and Steve Fuller's self-description used as the pivot: "starkly put, posthumanism is anti-humanist, while transhumanism is ultra-humanist." Her own line: "It is not that the humanist project is always ethically wrong or without merit – many of its tenets remain crucial tools in the protection of individuals – but that it is deeply inadequate to the world in which we live."

**She disagrees, at length and by name, with a friend thanked in her own acknowledgements.** On Patricia MacCormack's *The Ahuman Manifesto*: "**As the ethics of a new imaginary, that thesis is hard to fault and yet I disagree.**" The disagreement is specific: "life itself in MacCormack's account seems not to include our own species as at least one element of an ongoing vitalism, as though homo sapiens were ethically disqualified. This seems to me to be a mistaken approach . . . it seems contradictory to both celebrate that interdependency and advocate the extraction of the universalised category of the human." She quotes MacCormack's position fairly first, including "The death of the human species is the most life affirming event that could liberate the natural world from oppression", and notes that MacCormack "does not advocate radical, albeit humane, extermination for humans but rather proposes that we let extinction run its course". She also quotes MacCormack's line that fits her own argument: "We are always parasite, never host."

**She also differs from Braidotti, in the series her own book is published in**, and says where: "where I differ from her is around her apparent view that the ethical impetus can only be effective if it is grounded in a positive mode of coming together. Her approach explicitly rejects the notion of an intrinsic vulnerability that might draw together communities of living beings . . . as being a negative bond." And on biogenetics specifically: Braidotti "sees [it] not as a powerful realisation of communality at the cellular level, but as an expression of 'a shared form of vulnerability'. . . . Because, for Braidotti, vulnerability is a negative quality, and perhaps because she does not explore the impact of such micro-biology on innovative bioscientific thought."

**And she differs from Butler**, on scope: "Much as I agree with what Butler has to say about the lines of enquiry that she takes up, her analysis of vulnerability is still primarily focused on the human and on external relations."

**Her own positive claim about vulnerability**, which is the book's ethical conclusion: "Vulnerability, then, is much more than a passive union of shared helplessness: rather it is the very condition of interdependent, chimerical life." And: "To acknowledge that vulnerability is not a debased condition of the other, but the very condition of becoming is a step of profound importance."

She closes by quoting her own 2002 book at length, which is the only place she does so: "To resist closure, to be open to the trace of the other within, the other that is both self and irreducibly alien in its excess, to resist the normalisation of the strange, is to accept vulnerability. It is the very possibility of our becoming, for ourselves and with others, and it commands us to give up the comfort of familiarity and willingly embrace the risky ethics of uncertainty" (*Embodying the Monster*, 2002: 132). Then: "What I was unaware of at that time of writing, and what greatly strengthens the case that no sustainable division can be drawn between the diverse elements of a prosthetic relationship, is the significance of micro-biology."

Her blunt statement of the limit of political action, which sits oddly with the rest and should be quoted whole rather than paraphrased: "Put bluntly, we cannot save ourselves as humans and nor can we save those others with whom we co-exist and die. The exemplary desire to overturn racism, disableism, speciesism and many other forms of social and environmental violence is an important first stage that demands vigorous engagement, but the challenge of alterity is not that we should take action in that register so much as recognise the ultimate futility of sovereign endeavours."

The last line of the book: "Our possible futures and the full realisation of the posthuman in the imaginary are yet to be explored but the adventure is already underway."

# PART THREE: THE METAPHOR QUESTION, AND THIS FOLDER

## 10. What she does with the word metaphor, counted

This is the most precise finding available from the whole book for the live question in this folder, so it is given as a count and a list rather than as a summary.

**"Metaphor" and its forms occur 26 times in 117,111 words. "Figuration" or "figurations" occurs exactly once**, and that once is inside her paraphrase of someone else's term (Mitchell and Snyder's "narrative prosthesis", endnote 7 to chapter 1).

Of the 26, **two** are her flagging a figure of her own:
- "I use the term visceral prostheses to indicate both the embodied physicality and, **more metaphorically**, the 'stickiness' of prosthetic assemblies."
- and, in chapter 7, the observation that on living on "the approved notion is supposed to be **purely metaphorical**".

**One** is her explicitly rejecting a metaphor she had been using: the intermingling of corporeal materials "**cannot be adequately expressed by the metaphor of hybridity**" (ch 3 conclusion). She had used hybridity throughout chapters 1 and 2.

The remaining 23 are all her naming and criticising other people's figures. The catalogue, because it is the substance of the finding:
- "it is instructive how ubiquitous such metaphors are in Western languages" (on "at the heart of the matter")
- the gift of life as "the privileged **metaphor** for the donor organ and the act of donation in Anglo-American contexts"
- "even adventurous bioscience finds it difficult to escape **metaphors of origin and completion**", with Stilling et al's "Who is the puppet, who the puppeteer?"
- "the language in which the microbiome enters into socio-cultural awareness is still biased towards **militaristic metaphors, such as 'competing armies'**"
- immunology's "series of **pugnacious metaphors** expressing aggression, invasion, outright warfare and foreignness being met by a swath of self-defence mechanisms such as the forcefully named natural killer (NK) cells", where "the very same metaphors still hold sway in popular discourse"
- the shift in reproductive discourse "from dominant **metaphors of foetal intrusion** . . . to a new language of trafficking and migration"
- the "spare parts" **metaphor** in stem cell transplantation, and "the seemingly laudable **metaphor** of the gift"
- the **metaphor of parasitism**, which she tries and discards twice
- Sharp's reading that organ procurement "relies on elaborate forms of **metaphorical thinking**, on the part of all those involved, that ultimately obscure the origins of displaced body parts"
- "the clinical **metaphor** of replaceable machine parts"
- Leder's "'Blood' is the **metaphor** for this viscerality"
- Garoian's approach to prostheses "as a way of exploring **metaphors** of embodiment in art-based research"
- and, on Nancy, the point that the in-coming stranger "is **not a metaphor** for the grafted heart", and that after transplantation "Nancy **refuses the metaphor** of either ownership or connection"
- plus the key one in the endnotes: Mitchell and Snyder's narrative prosthesis, where "figurations of disability may be used **wholly metaphorically** to transmit meanings quite separate from any understanding the lived experience itself", so that "disability is framed as an uber-signifier that always points to something else that defers any real engagement with those others who live with its effects."

**So her position on figure is a practice rather than a statement.** She does not define metaphor, does not discuss what a figure is, and does not use "figuration" of her own concepts. She uses the word almost exclusively as a diagnostic, to mark where someone else's language is carrying a politics they have not examined, and she applies it to herself once, by withdrawing hybridity. That is close to McKnight and Whitburn's entailments argument in method, arrived at without their vocabulary and without their general claim.

**Six positions on figure are now in this folder.** Recorded, not adjudicated.
- **Neimanis.** Her water figures are "always more than metaphorical", working by "contiguity or continuity (rather than analogy)"; "concepts are always also material".
- **McKnight and Whitburn.** A figure's entailments arrive whether intended or not; the test is affective; banning metaphor is "a task beyond us as authors".
- **Betterton.** A figure of creativity applied to pregnancy erases the agency of the person it is applied to; she quotes Davis on theory's "tendency to privilege the body as metaphor" and defers the problem.
- **Jackson.** Prefer "figuration" to "metaphor", because figuration keeps contradiction and disjunction.
- **Shannon.** The word appears only in denials: Barad's diffraction is "not writing metaphorically", and some artworks "are not metaphors . . . [they] event concepts".
- **Shildrick.** No statement; a practice. Metaphor is the name for what other people's language is doing to them, applied to herself once as a withdrawal.
- *(Donnarumma uses neither word at all, which stands as an absence rather than a seventh position.)*

## 11. Where this book sits beside the other reads in this folder

**Shildrick 2015, the *Hypatia* article.** This book is the full statement of what that article compresses. Both run on Haraway's question. Both make the degree-not-kind argument. Both use the same list of what surrounds an embodied life ("the specific national benefits system that impedes or supports embodied difference; the paid assistants"), the article at a medal ceremony, the book around a disabled artist's work. The article's argument that the prosthesis "constructs the very thing that they purport to merely enhance" is here given its Derrida source and its Kamuf gloss. What the book adds and the article does not have at all: the whole micro-biological half, microchimerism and the microbiome, and the empirical PITH and GOLA material. And her self-citation of the article gets its page range wrong by one page. See the top of this file.

**Donnarumma.** Two connections, one of them direct. First, the acknowledgements: he and Pevere thought through this book's cover image with her. Second, his essay's method rests on the Shildrick sentence quoted in chapter 1 of this book, "a prosthesis may both extend functional agency and radically destabilise specifically human agency as such", which he cites from the 2013 *Somatechnics* article at p. 278. So the two texts read today are linked both personally and argumentatively. Her account of incorporation against extension is also his, in different words: she rejects "the idea of instruments and body technologies as extensions or appendages that only augment the body or extend the mind", he rejects "the characterization . . . of the prosthesis as an aiding device". Where they differ: her prostheses are mostly organic and mostly inside, his are mostly technological and sonic, and he never goes to microchimerism or the microbiome. Nothing in either decides anything about the other.

**Shannon.** No citation either way. Four points of contact, all left as contact and not as agreement. Both refuse the neutral word for a device, she with "sensor" implicitly and he with "sensor" explicitly. Both take their notation from D/deaf studies: his A/autisms is modelled on D/deaf, and her endnote 4 to the introduction uses the same reference point, that people "who identify as Deaf, for example, may see Sign Language as simply an alternative but equally effective form of communication". Both use "queer" as method rather than identity, and she says so in endnote 8 to the introduction: "I use the term 'queer' to denote a deviation, or more radically a rupture, from any entrenched norm rather than as a term of sexual identity . . . queering operates, then, as an analytic methodology", adding that it "has much in common with and often interchangeable with the term 'crip'". And both cite Puar's *The Right to Maim*, he for the moment-by-moment question of how abled and how disabled, she for debilitation. Their difference: he holds four orientations in friction and refuses to pick, she moves through three theorists in sequence and says each time which she is leaving behind.

**Betterton.** Direct contact in citation. Betterton 2006 builds her objection to Quinn's marble on Price and Shildrick, *Vital Signs* (1998), and cites Shildrick's *Embodying the Monster* (2002) in an endnote. This book quotes *Embodying the Monster* at length in its final chapter, so the line runs from the piece Betterton cited to the book that closes with it. Thematically: Betterton's monstrous maternal and Shildrick's chimera are both figures of the body that is more than one, and Shildrick gives the chimera its myth and its biology where Betterton gives the monster its art history. Betterton's argument that a figure of creativity applied to pregnancy erases agency sits beside Shildrick's catalogue of reproductive metaphors in chapter 6, which is the same operation on a different corpus. Not adjudicated.

**Jackson and Mazzei.** No citation either way, and the methods are genuinely different. The PITH method is a phenomenologically designed interview study that then became post-qualitative, video-recorded to catch what the words contradict. That is not Jackson's rhizovocality and not Mazzei's Voice without Organs, though it shares their refusal of the transparent speaking subject: Shildrick's version is "there is no single truth to be told", reached from the video rather than from Deleuze. Her PITH team "did not seek to impartially engage with the data set as though there could be a wholly objective truth of the matter to uncover, and we were less interested in the descriptive question 'how?' than to ask the question 'why?'", which is the exact inverse of Mazzei's Deleuzian "how does it work?" and of Jackson's "not what voice means . . . but what it does". **That inversion is worth recording and not resolving**: three post-qualitative researchers, two choosing "what it does" over "what it means" and one choosing "why" over "how".

**Fries and Fahey.** No citation. The link is the artists. Bufano's "attraction/repulsion aspect" and "something beautiful in futility", DV8's deconstruction of "the trope of perfection", Graeae's interweaving of deaf and hearing actors, and Shildrick's claim that art "stages contestation" because it can "mobilise the production of knowledge along new pathways" are all adjacent to Fries's "manifested as an encounter rather than as an illustration or description" and Fahey's naming of a failed body of work as "illustrating an idea, rather than embodying an exploration of the question". Four refusals of illustration across four texts, none citing the others.

**Romero.** No contact. Barad appears only four times in 117,111 words, and never as a load-bearing citation; agential realism is mentioned once among "broader debates . . . in ontological anthropology, new materialism and STS (Mol 2003; Barad 2007; Woolgar and Lezaun 2013) **without clearly aligning with any of them**". So the Barad borrowing question that Shannon asserts and Romero's chapter addresses from the physics side simply does not arise in this book. That is an absence worth noting given how central Barad is elsewhere in the folder.

**Butler.** Cited 9 times across 273 pages, including a 2006 and a 2014 text on vulnerability, and *Bodies That Matter* does not appear in the bibliography at all. Judith Butler sits on this series' editorial board. So Butler is present as a colleague and as a vulnerability theorist, and absent as the author of the abjection and performativity arguments that the book's material repeatedly touches. Noted, not a fault.

## 12. Her self-flags, counted and placed

More than any other text read in this folder today. They are listed because they are a model of how to hold a position and its limit together.

- On her own grounding: "My own position as a postmodernist and feminist philosopher and body theorist".
- On decolonial work she says she cannot do: "My ambitions outrun my expertise and what I offer here is a stepping-stone to a differently realised project that would not be primarily reliant on contesting some forms of colonial thought from an ostensibly more sensitive position within the same colonising system." And later: "As a scholar living and educated under the humanist imaginary of the global North, my affinity with others is disastrously underdeveloped but I can recognise that it need not be."
- On what she will not promise: "The intention is not to deliver a route map to a better life in which all lives are equally valued". "My aim is to raise questions, and stimulate further speculation, rather than to provide answers." "I cannot promise answers along the lines of better regulations and protocols."
- On Deleuze: "I offer no loyalty to any canon, but simply rummage in the Deleuzian toolbox."
- On animal research: "It is not an ethically comfortable position."
- On her own earlier attempt at a term: "In previous research, I have toyed with rethinking organ transplantation in terms of parasitism, but it is a term usually used negatively."
- On her own partial explanation of PITH distress: "I say that explanation is 'partial' advisedly."
- On the whole posthuman vocabulary: "I will continue to use the term posthuman embodiment as **an imperfect aspiration** but one that continues to do a lot of work in progressing the task of thinking differently."
- On who should carry it on: "far more attention needs to be paid to alternative forms of knowledge – both traditional and speculative – than I have been able to give here and I hope others who are less embedded in Western models of thought will find different starting places and destinations."
- And a small but telling one: in endnote 13 to chapter 1 she quotes Ingwersen describing Amanda Baggs and inserts "[sic]" at his "her", having herself written "they" of Baggs in the same note.

## 13. Internal inconsistencies and things to check before citing

All of these are in the source, not in extraction.

- **Her self-citation of the *Hypatia* article gives pp. 13-30 where the article prints 13-29.** This is the one that matters, because that article is now read and will be cited.
- **"Nancy (2002)" and "Nancy (2008)" are both used for *L'Intrus* material**, with page numbers from two different editions, and the bibliography carries both. Check which edition a given page refers to before quoting.
- Chapter 1 endnote 10 gives a record comparison without dates or sources: "The world record for 1,500 m for a conventional athlete is almost fifteen seconds slower than that for a wheelchair athlete." Unverifiable as printed.
- Chapter 5 endnote 6 has a broken citation: "as Holmquist et al (20) report", with the year truncated.
- A reference inside a long quotation is left dangling: Nancy's L'Intrus material is quoted at "(2002: 13)" in two places with slightly different lead-ins.
- The claim "all of this was discussed" in chapter 5 rests on inference from the interview material and she says so: "It seems unlikely from the Lübeck interview material that any of this was discussed with the families involved."
- Several web citations are bare URLs with no retrieval date: the WHO dementia fact sheet, caressesrobot.org, mediglobus.com, recompose.life, Bufano's video page, the Physician's Surrogacy site, the WHO transplantation statistics page.
- Chapter 5's Lübeck collection is cited as forthcoming in 2022 under two slightly different titles, "The Child's Body: Stem Cell Transplantations between Siblings as Social Phenomena" in endnote 2 and "Stem Cell Transplantations between Siblings as Social Phenomena" in the acknowledgements.
- The acknowledgements contain two small typos: "A later grant from the KONE Foundation **competed** the book project" for "completed", and a stray paragraph mark before "List of Illustrations".
- Chapter 1 prints "Erevelles" where the scholar's name is usually Erevelles, and quotes her at "(Erevelles 2014: np)".
- Chapter 4 gives "Thodberg et al (2016)" for a 124-resident Danish study and also cites "Calo et al (2011)" for the Danish assessment criteria; the two are conflated in one sentence and should be separated if either is cited.

**OCR and extraction artefacts**, which are extraction faults and not source errors: the PDF's soft hyphen before some words comes out as a stray character at the start of headings (shown as "­Deleuzian somatechnics", "­CHAPTER ONE"); en dashes in "self–other" and "gut–brain" sometimes render as en dashes and sometimes as hyphens; "Muñoz" and other diacritics survive but the embedded table of contents carries byte-order marks around "PITH" and "Merleau-Ponty's". Quotations above are given with these repaired and nothing else changed. In several places the source itself prints an en dash where a comma would read better; where that dash would be an em dash in my own prose I have broken the sentence instead, and the words are unchanged.
