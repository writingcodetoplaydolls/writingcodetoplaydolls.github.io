## 3. Chapter 7: the argument against pathologisation

The chapter opens on four epigraphs from autistic people, then this:

> "What is it we really mean when we say 'human?' According to autistic Amanda Baggs, we certainly don't mean 'autistic.' We mean neurotypical, we mean oriented to interaction with other humans, we mean almost exclusively tuned to human language."

The target is Simon Baron-Cohen's **mindblindness**, which Manning quotes and then dismantles:

> "Baron-Cohen's argument is that the autistic cannot bridge the worlds of the physical and the imaginary... And, as we well know, without empathy you are not considered truly human."

Her counter is not that autistic people are relational after all in the normal way. It is that the relation runs elsewhere. She quotes Mukhopadhyay on empathy for trapped miners:

> "my empathy would probably be towards the flashlight batteries of those trapped coal miners... Or my empathy would perhaps be towards the trapped air around those coal miners."

and comments: **"Empathy not explicitly for or toward the human, but with the world in its emergence. An ethics of relation."**

### The passage you will want

Mukhopadhyay, on being assessed:

> "'Does he flap his hands all the time?' the clinical psychologist in a Calcutta hospital asked Mother. . . . I chose to stand in the corner between two glass doors, so that I could see as many reflections of my flapping hands as possible through the glass. . . . And each time I was brought near the table, I would go back to stand in front of the cupboards. **How could I tell them that the shadows and reflections made me feel secure?**"

Repetitive movement described from inside, as a perceptual practice with a purpose, by the person doing it, inside the very encounter that is coding it as a symptom.

And Baggs, on perception without buffers:

> "To me the world comes in such great detail that it is hard for me to put the easy interpretations on it that most people use; the way they divide it into pieces and make it abstract is foreign to me."

Manning also quotes Baggs's warning, and takes it seriously enough to quote against her own project:

> "If we were real people, people would see us as individuals, rather than heroes, tragedies, inspirations, or representatives of our entire impairment group."

## 4. Chapter 8 on Tustin: this is the argument your draft needs

I reported on the Ivinson and Renold papers that their choreographer reads the girls' ticks as evidence and then works to remove them: "it is about trying to acknowledge them and then **dissipate** the busi-ness... the ticks and habits detract from that," and the authors follow her into "clear lines formed by purpose, not habit."

**Manning takes apart the same move, made by Frances Tustin, in print, forty years earlier.** Tustin, 21 mentions, quoted directly:

> "'Autistic shapes are not merely psychological curiosities. **They are blocks to more normal functioning**' (1984, 284)."

Manning's response:

> "This is why Tustin must so quickly move toward the 'curing' of autistics of their shapes... The autistic who lingers in the shape of enthusiasm must therefore be divested of her attachment to the processual, must be taught to speak, and, more specifically, must be taught to articulate the content of experience in terms of already-formed objects and events."

> "The core of the problem in Tustin's analysis of shapes is that she begins with species instead of speciations. To begin with species is to have already put into place a hierarchy of forms. The hierarchy itself then becomes a pathology."

And the sentence that generalises it:

> "pathologization takes the fully formed individual as the starting point to experience and erects it as a norm."

**Why this is load-bearing for you.** Three texts now in your folder sit in one lineage and Manning is the dissent inside it:

| | |
|---|---|
| Walkerdine 2010 | Takes **Bick, Tustin, Winnicott and Anzieu** straight, uses "second skin" as her central concept, uncritically |
| Ivinson & Renold 2021 | Takes "second skin" from **"Esther Bick, 1987, cited in Manning, 2013"**, which is this book, and then follows a practice that dissipates the ticks |
| Manning 2013 | **Attacks Tustin by name** for exactly that move |

Ivinson and Renold cite this book for Bick and do not carry across its argument against Tustin. That is a gap in the literature you can occupy without attacking anyone: the concept travelled, the critique attached to it did not.

## 5. Deligny, and your sections 3.5 and 3.6

Chapter 8's second half is **Fernand Deligny**, who from 1967 ran encampments in the Cévennes at Monoblet with autistic children described as *hors parole*, out of language. Manning:

> "Deligny turns his back on the psychiatric institution and especially on the psychoanalytic tendency to pathologize 'the language we speak.' He proposes instead **a cartography of everyday life**... The project is not to make autistics more 'like us'."

What came out of it:

> "a singular iteration of experience in the making begins to take the form of maps of movement, **'tracings,'** as Deligny calls them. These tracings, a collaboration between the network and the autistics, become **incipient cartographies** of an associated milieu of relation."

> "These tracings at once trace the regularity of habit and routine and the unrepresentable of its drifts. **They do not reproduce movement, they activate it.**"

The challenge Deligny set himself: "to learn 'to see language from the point of view of a mute child'." Manning calls it "a choreographic proposition."

Your research questions name **cartography**. Your Chapter 3 is **3.5 Traces** and **3.6 Reading the traces**. Deligny gives you a fifty-year-old art-adjacent practice of tracing autistic movement as cartography, reproduced as images in this book, and the sentence "they do not reproduce movement, they activate it" is a one-line answer to what a trace is for.

Note the ethics, which is also yours to handle: Deligny's tracings were made **by the adults, of the children's movement**. Not self-tracing. The reversal, tracing your own, is open.

## 6. The constraint, restated because it is the one that bites

> "let me repeat: **autistic perception is a tendency in perception on a continuum with all perception, not a definition of autism.** It is a style that has been remarked upon by the many autistics whose voices we've heard throughout."

Manning universalises the concept on purpose, to stop it functioning as a diagnosis. The cost is that it stops functioning as a standpoint. If autistic perception belongs to perception generally, then being autistic does not license privileged access to it, and a method grounded in your own autism has to say what its relation to Manning's concept is.

Three ways through, all available in the book itself:

1. Manning works **from other people's accounts**, quoted at length and credited, never her own. The book is built out of Baggs, Mukhopadhyay, Savarese, Bedward, Rubin, Bissonnette. First-person autistic method is not what this book does.
2. Deligny's tracings are made **of** autistic movement by others. Self-tracing is unoccupied.
3. Manning's own vocabulary is not uniformly careful: chapter 4's endnote 13 says "those who **suffer from** motor activation problems such as autism," and chapter 7 says she does "not want to underestimate the complex motor and neurological challenges that accompany autism." The deficit register surfaces even here.

## 7. Four cautions

**It is a hard book.** 129 uses of "individuation," 193 of "more-than," 38 of Simondon, and the argument is carried in a vocabulary that assumes Whitehead and Simondon. Reading it will cost real time, and the concepts do not detach cleanly into quotable units.

**The OCR is poor in places.** Running heads are mangled throughout ("C H Al'T l! R S l\V ll N"), and some quoted passages have lost characters. Every quotation above was read in context, but anything going into the dissertation must be checked against the PDF.

**"Stim" and "stimming" do not appear once.** Nor does "minor gesture," which arrives in the 2016 book. Proprioception appears 6 times against your 7. The vocabulary your research questions use is not this book's vocabulary, and the sentence "autistic perception is a tendency in perception... not a definition of autism" is the reason.

**It is not art practice as research.** Research-creation twice, practice-as-research zero, arts-based zero. Manning is a philosopher who works with dancers, architects and the Deligny archive. The maker-researcher position is not hers.
