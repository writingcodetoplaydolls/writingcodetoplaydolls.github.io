# Hayles's "field theory of culture": did anyone ever pick it up

30 Oct 2026. The question asked: whether anyone ever took up the Whitehead-based culture theory Hayles mentions. The Whitehead is **Alfred North Whitehead**, and the phrase is from *Science and the Modern World* (1925).

## 1. What she actually wrote

*The Cosmic Web* (1984), Introduction, **p. 22**:

> A more accurate and appropriate model for such parallel developments would be a field notion of culture, a societal matrix which consists (in Whitehead's phrase) of a "climate of opinion" that makes some questions interesting to pursue and renders others uninteresting or irrelevant. **Such a field theory of culture has yet to be definitively articulated, and is beyond the scope of this study.** But it is already possible to see some of the elements it would include. It would, for example, define more fully how a "climate of opinion" is established, and demonstrate that it is this climate, rather than direct borrowing or transmission, that is the underlying force guiding intellectual inquiry.

It is a call for a theory, placed in the Introduction and then dropped. The book does not return to it.

## 2. The short answer

**Yes, one person picked it up: Hayles herself, six years later, and she said so in as many words. Nobody else has, under that name. The phrase "field theory of culture" appears to have been used once, in 1984, and never again.**

## 5. The reading, which is mine and not hers

Three things seem worth saying, and the third is the one that may be useful.

1. **The idea was picked up; the name was not.** What Hayles asked for in 1984 she supplied in 1990, but she renamed it on the way, and the renaming is probably why the call reads as unanswered. Anyone searching the phrase finds a dead end. Anyone searching "cultural field" lands in Bourdieu.
2. **She also narrowed it.** The 1989 interview says the single matrix was too simple: "culture is highly fissured and stratified, not by any means homogeneous or monolithic." The 1990 version keeps the fissures, which is why the arrows do not all point the same way.
3. **The strict answer to the question as asked is no.** If "picked up" means somebody other than Hayles took the 1984 call and built the theory it asked for, nobody did. Even the 2025 commentary written entirely about this book quotes up to the edge of the passage and does not quote the call.

### 8.1 The overturned finding: somebody other than Hayles did pick it up

Sections 2, 3 and 7 say that nobody other than Hayles took the concept up. **That is wrong, and `RCEI_50_( 2005)_03.pdf` is the counter-example.** Manuela Rossini, at p. 25:

> These different types of science/fiction form a **"cultural matrix"** in which **a causal perspective gives way to an understanding that lines of influence between texts or disciplines are never direct but interwoven in very complex ways.** As part of a larger network of forces, the cultural matrix participates in the equally complex, material-semiotic construction, the imagineering, of the future, including the future of the human species and, last but not least, the humanities.

Her footnote 14 on that sentence reads: **"N. Katherine HAYLES, *The Cosmic Web: Scientific Field Models and Literary Strategies in the Twentieth Century* (Ithaca: Cornell UP, 1984) 23-24."** She cites the pages where the concept is set out, uses it in Hayles's own sense, and goes on using it as a working tool at p. 31: "The almost identical rhetoric of Stelarc and Clark confirms the earlier argument that **culture (arts, in this case) and science are part of the same cultural matrix**."

**So the corrected finding is: the concept was taken up, by name, with a page citation, by at least one scholar other than Hayles, twenty-one years after the book.** It did not travel under the phrase "field theory of culture", which still appears nowhere but Hayles 1984 p. 22.

A second, weaker case in the same batch: **Dwight 2004** writes that "hypertexts exist as a symbiosis of human and machine **within a cultural matrix**", and cites Hayles elsewhere in the essay, on disembodiment, but **attributes the phrase to nobody**. Recorded as ambient use, not as uptake. **Desilets 2026** uses "the architectural and cultural matrix" in her own sense and engages Hayles's *Unthought* critically; **not uptake of this concept.**

### 8.2 Hayles, in 2025, says the call went unanswered, and says why

In her reply to Beer, at p. 193:

> *The Cosmic Web* had the vision, and the ambition, of my later books, but not the method. Published in 1984, it attempted to tackle a huge problem with the (inadequate) resources at my command as a newly credentialed assistant professor. **Had I been trained as an intellectual historian, say, like Martin Jay, I could have perhaps been able to trace more fully the idea of an encompassing field that cut across any number of areas in the early twentieth century**, including linguistics, philosophy, mathematics, social theory, and the physical sciences. **Certainly such an analysis would have included the work of Alfred North Whitehead, of which I was largely ignorant at the time.** As it was, I was able to instance only a few parallels that had come into my ken, such as quantum field theory and Saussure's linguistics.

That is the 1984 call, named as the thing she did not have the training to write, forty-one years later, and with **Whitehead named as what such an analysis would have had to include**. It also says in plain terms that when she borrowed his phrase in 1984 she did not know his work.

She also writes, of Beer: "Beer stops his analysis with my second book, *Chaos Bound* (1990), but **if he had continued his line of thought, more parallels would have come to light**", and "Its shortcomings are even more obvious to me now than at the time, but I think I would not revise it if offered the chance."

### 8.3 The 1989 interview, now verified

The quotation used at second hand through Beer in sections 3 and 7 is **confirmed verbatim** in `ijls-2516-araas-vesely.pdf`. **One citation detail is still open**: Beer's reference list gives the journal as the *Iowa Journal of Literary History* 10(1): 1-7, the filename abbreviates it `ijls`, and the masthead page carries no extractable text, so the journal's own name has still not been read off the article. The interview text confirms only the University of Iowa and a March 1989 Iowa conference.

## 9. A closer read of Hayles 2025 for what she now says about the theory

`K:\OneDrive\UNM\Dissertation\todo October 26\191-200+Hayles.pdf`. Ten pages, printed 191 to 200, body ending at p. 198. Read in full.

### 9.1 The first thing to notice is what is absent

| Term | Occurrences in the whole essay |
|---|---|
| articulation | 16 |
| cognition, cognitive | 34 |
| culture, cultural | 12 |
| **cultural matrix** | **1** |
| **Whitehead** | **1** |
| **field theory of culture** | **0** |
| **climate of opinion** | **0** |
| affect, affective | 0 |
| Braidotti | 0 |
| Barad | 0 |

**She does not name the 1984 call.** She does not quote her own p. 22, does not use the phrase "field theory of culture", and does not mention the climate of opinion. **The cultural matrix appears exactly once in ten pages, at p. 194, and inside a subordinate clause.** Whitehead appears once, at p. 192, and only as what a better-trained author would have had to read.

### 9.2 She lets Beer choose the through-line, and it is not the matrix

The essay opens by accepting Beer's framing: "David Beer's insightful and sympathetic article ... **rightfully highlights the 'problem of articulation' as a major conclusion from that study**" (p. 191). The whole essay is then organised as the history of that one problem, from 1984 to 2025. **The cultural matrix is not the thread she picks up.**

Her verdict on the book itself, p. 192:

> Reflecting on Beer's article, I am reminded of Aldous Huxley's comment, when in middle age he was offered the chance to revise *Brave New World*, that he did not want to spend his later years correcting the mistakes of his youth; consequently, he opted to re-publish it unchanged. **So with *The Cosmic Web*. Its shortcomings are even more obvious to me now than at the time, but I think I would not revise it if offered the chance.**

> ***The Cosmic Web* had the vision, and the ambition, of my later books, but not the method.**

### 9.3 Where the theory actually went: it became one half of constrained constructivism

The single appearance of the matrix, p. 194, is the answer to what happened to it:

> In my terms, constrained constructivism meant that STS research was correct. **Scientific models are not only culturally influenced but culturally constructed, partaking of contemporary ideologies and fully immersed in what I called the cultural matrix. The crucial point is that they are not merely culturally constructed; they are also constrained by scientific methods** that test whether or not they are consistent with reality as we experience it.

**The matrix stops being the thing to be theorised and becomes a premise.** In 1984 the question was how parallels arise with no line of transmission. By 1991, in "Constrained Constructivism" (*New Orleans Review* 18: 76-85), the question has changed: how can science be culturally constructed and still reliable. The matrix supplies the "constructed" half and the scientific method supplies the "constrained" half. The 1984 question is not answered; it is retired.

Her account of why she needed this, p. 193, is the Science Wars:

> For me personally, this was a painful period, akin perhaps to **an adult watching her two parents fight.** My lineage included my scientific training in chemistry, which had imbued me with a rock-solid belief that scientific methods were, and are, our best means of reaching reliable and robust knowledges. But my training in literary studies ... had also made me aware that **all of us are immersed in our cultural contexts, which have enormous influences over what and how we think, scientists included.**

### 9.4 Then culture itself is demoted

p. 196, and this is the sharpest sentence in the essay for this question:

> The "articulation problem" had morphed into what I might call the **species cognition problem. Why stop with the influence of culture when the biological capacities of a species have an even greater determinate influence on how a species constructs (its version of) the world?**

**Culture is explicitly subordinated to species-level cognitive capacity.** A theory of culture is no longer the container; it sits inside a bigger one.

### 9.5 The successor concepts that do the non-linear work

| Concept | p. | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| Second-order cybernetics, von Foerster's *Observing Systems* | 195 | "from one perspective, an observer views a system, but from another perspective, **the observer himself is inside a system that can be observed in turn**. At last, here was a version of the 'articulation problem' located within a scientific discipline itself" |
| Maturana and Varela, autopoiesis | 195 | "any system processes the world solely in terms of its own organization and structure ... **no information from the outside ever enters a cognitive system, but only triggers a response**". She is "sympathetic", but judges that eliminating causation gives "unnecessarily convoluted rhetoric and **twisted-pretzel thinking**" |
| Luhmann's **operational closure** | 195 | Luhmann "wisely changed the idea of informational closure to operational closure ... the system processes information only in terms of its own structure ... **It achieves a kind of wrenched referentiality, however, by proliferating differences within its own categories, thus mimicking the complexities of its environment, but in its own terms**" |
| Luhmann's **blind spot** | 195 to 196 | Drawing the line between system and environment "not only creates a system but at the same time and with the same gesture, **inevitably introduces a blind spot, that is, all the alternative ways in which that original line might have been drawn differently**, resulting in different environments and different systems" |
| **Technogenesis** | 196 | "our biological, cognitive, and psychological capacities have, since the beginning of *Homo sapiens*, **coevolved through strong feedback loops with our technologies**" |
| Nonconscious cognition | 196 to 197 | "**a creature does not need to possess consciousness to have cognitive abilities**", and in *Bacteria to AI* (2025) "**all living creatures have cognitive abilities**" |

**Luhmann's operational closure is the closest thing in this essay to the 1984 problem restated.** A system mimics the complexity of its environment while taking in nothing from it directly. That is influence with no transmission, in systems language rather than cultural language, and Hayles endorses it.

### 9.6 Her own warning about reading the trajectory

The last paragraph of the body, p. 198:

> Of course, **this account is told from a retrospective viewpoint, and it therefore tends to impute more clarity and coherence than may have been present at the time. There were plenty of false starts, intuitions imperfectly grasped, and inadequate intellectual resources along the way.**

### 9.7 One divergence worth stating plainly, and not resolving here

In 2025 the capacity Hayles extends beyond the human is **cognition**: five criteria in "Modes of Cognition" (2025b), applied to plants, microorganisms and Large Language Models alike. It is not affect. In ten pages the cognitive words outnumber the affective ones 34 to 0. Braidotti and Barad are not mentioned. **The reading of what passes between nodes is therefore not the same reading on the two sides, and that difference is not adjudicated here.**
