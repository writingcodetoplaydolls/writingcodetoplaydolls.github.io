### Cartography

As navigators we are the nomadic subjects, moving through a cartography of reality/the Zoe as it is in its specific time/space/matter configuration. Our movement along a path is guided by our affirmative ethics.

Trinh T. Minh-ha is among the most precise illustrations of this figure. As a Vietnamese filmmaker working in Senegal, the United States, and across the postcolonial world, she is not metaphorically nomadic. She inhabits multiple positions simultaneously without resolving them into a stable identity. She describes her practice as "a constant flow from the inside out and outside in," and insists that the challenge lies in "materializing a reality" in which the personal is not naturally political but every form of knowledge carries a specific location (Trinh, 1992, p. 82). Her films --- Reassemblage (1982), Naked Spaces: Living is Round (1985), Surname Viet Given Name Nam (1989) --- do not represent nomadism as subject matter. They enact it as method: refusing the position of the expert-observer who speaks about, insisting instead on a "speaking nearby" that "reflects on itself and can come very close to a subject without, however, seizing or claiming it" (Trinh, 1992, p. 87). The nomadic cartography does not claim to map a fixed territory. It traces a movement through a territory that is itself in motion, attending to what becomes visible from this location at this moment --- and offering that trace as evidence rather than conclusion. Fuser (2019) reads Trinh's entire cinematic project through Braidotti's nomadic subjectivity, arguing that her aesthetics produce a "politics of speaking nearby" that structurally destabilizes the gaze of alterity and challenges the epistemic authority of the Western observer (Fuser, 2019, pp. 107--121). The nomadic subject, in Trinh's practice, is not a philosophical figure. It is a filmmaker with a camera, a specific body, a specific history, working in specific material conditions, refusing exhaustive capture.

Braidotti's cartography is "a non-hierarchical and non-linear mapping" where "any form of encounter with the world... constitutes a legitimate resource of meaning." Forgetting to Forget, on Rosi Braidotti

Cartography as method is explicitly a politics of location as much as a navigation tool. "Situated locations draw a cartographic map of power relations and thus can also help identify possible sites and strategies of resistance. Nonlinearity and a nonunitary vision of the subject do not necessarily result in either cognitive or moral relativism or social anarchy" --- Braidotti frames them instead as "significant sites for reconfiguring feminist political practice" (Braidotti, 2011, p. 12). What connects Braidotti's cartography to the Adornian three steps that follow is precisely this: both name the ethical labor of navigating a world without stable ground. Adorno's ouroboran concept-making, the willingness to be the clown, the practice of lingering --- these are the comportments of the nomadic subject moving through a cartographic field, refusing the fantasy of a view from above, staying inside the entanglement. Cartography is not a map   consult before entering the terrain. It is what   produce by moving through it accountably.

### Nomadic Subjectivities

I write from a particular location. I am an artist, educator, researcher, parent, and neurodiverse person. My background is in anthropology and art (BA), art education (MA), and experimental art and technology (MFA). My PhD research entangles posthumanism and new materialism with each of these. Adrienne Rich’s poetry has been with me for thirty-five years; encountering her as the locational ground of feminist materialism reads, to me, as coming home. The same is true of Trinh T. Minh-ha, whose work I almost pursued in graduate study in visual anthropology before the path turned. My study of quantum science shows me that the relational, indeterminate character of the world has been repeatedly obscured to maintain authoritarian controls governed by the Platonic dualism this document traces and refuses.

The form of the document is part of its argument. Multiple overlapping descriptions are not redundancy but posthumanist method: each frame produces what the others foreclose, and no single account exhausts the phenomenon. The reader who expects a single line of argument, exhaustively defended, will read this against the grain. The reader who reads it as a set of overlapping cuts on a shared ground will see what it is doing.

When I first began trying to write for academic audiences, to fit all my thoughts into twenty-five linear pages of careful citations that moved with military precision into the field of battle, where I must fight to prove I can make sense (not to my immediate colleagues and mentors, but to the academic world at large), I struggled with the enormity of the field, the sheer volume of words and ideas and becomings and connections. I could see them all, all at once, always. In the first chapter of Jane Bennett's Vibrant Matter, she asks how to help people learn to see the vibrancy of matter, to open themselves up to the agential swarm, the primordial swerve, to notice, to linger in the world of material becomings. I was struck and annoyed, because I already live in the world of vibrancy and frequently I am exhausted with people who don't see as I see, because they frequently have no idea what I am going on about, like maybe there is a giant rabbit behind my shoulder whispering into my ear (or at least they think that I think there is a giant rabbit behind my shoulder whispering into my ear).

After months of wrangling and too many to count false starts in organizing my thoughts so they made sense in twenty five linear pages, I realized the audience I care most about making sense to is the audience I should be writing for, and then I had a vision, a lens, a path that was clear. I would write to my students, for my students, past/present/future students. The classroom for me, for the past twenty-two-ish years (and maybe before that when my hat leaned towards student), is like an art gallery (my experience/perception/invention of an art gallery). ### Bohr's viewpoint theory

Hayles gives Bohr's position in five moves, and the sequence is the argument. **One**, the Uncertainty Relation is about language and not disturbance: Bohr "never endorsed the 'disturbance' concept of Heisenberg. Rather, in his view, **the Uncertainty Relation was deeply bound up with the limitations of language**," and Aage Petersen recounts that Bohr loved to repeat "**we are suspended in language**" (Hayles, 1984, p. 52).

**Two**, complementarity in Bohr's own words: "The fundamental postulate of the indivisibility of the quantum of action . . . forces us to adopt a new mode of description designated as **complementary in the sense that any given application of classical concepts precludes the simultaneous use of other classical concepts which in a different connection are equally necessary for the elucidation of the phenomena**" (quoted, p. 52). **Three**, the classical terms cannot simply be dropped, because they "reflect the essential distinction between subject and object that is the absolute prerequisite for the process of observation to begin," and from that division "follows the meaning of every concept, or rather every word, **the meaning depending upon our arbitrary choice of viewpoint**" (p. 53).

**Four**, the core: "**To speak is to speak from a position that is defined as separate and distinct from that which is spoken about. Language thus implies a viewpoint, a specific place at which the subject-object split is made.** But because of the Uncertainty Relation, this viewpoint will always result in an incomplete and partial description. To complete the description, another viewpoint is necessary which makes the subject-object split in a different place. **But these viewpoints will be mutually exclusive, because the subject-object split can only be made in one place at a time. Hence no matter which viewpoint is chosen, there will always be aspects of reality that can only be understood from another, mutually exclusive viewpoint. To switch to that new viewpoint will render indistinct and hence indeterminate aspects that may have been clear in the former viewpoint.**" With Bohr: "we must, in general, be prepared to accept the fact that **a complete elucidation of one and the same object may require diverse points of view which defy a unique description**" (pp. 53 and 53). And the generalisation: "any concept whatever, that is, **any definition of reality that is external to us, will have the same built-in limitations of viewpoint**" (p. 53).

**Five**, and this is what makes it a method rather than a lament: "For Bohr, the fact that we remain 'suspended in language' does not mean that we cannot make progress . . . we progressively refine our viewpoints not by attempting the impossible, that is, **observing without a viewpoint, but by recognizing the ways in which our description of reality depends on the viewpoint we have chosen**" (pp. 54 to 55). Bohr's own account of the regress: even the most elementary concepts are "based on assumptions initially unnoticed," and an "explicit consideration" of them yields "a classification of more extended domains of experience" which in turn rests on further "unrecognized presuppositions" (quoted, p. 54). Hayles's conclusion: "**Thus progress is made not by ignoring or underplaying limitations of viewpoint, but by systematically examining and exploiting them**" (pp. 54 to 55).

### Bohr's viewpoint theory (Hayles, pp. 52 to 55)

This is the same claim as Barad's complementarity, which means "simultaneously 'mutually exclusive' and 'mutually necessary'," arriving from Bohr through a literary critic rather than through Barad. It also states the commitment about divergent readings in its procedural form, with an instruction attached: the divergence is not to be resolved, it is to be examined and exploited. Carries into 1.2 (Braidotti) and 2.2.

### Affirmative Ethics

If the world is as § 4.1 reports it, and we inhabit it as § 4.2 reports, and we know it from inside as § 4.3 reports, then the question of what to do follows from the question of where we are. Borgdorff, Peters, and Pinch generalize the commitment: “research methods do not only observe and represent materials, issues, and events but in fact act upon and intervene in these materials, issues, and events” (2020, p. 7). For this commitment, I will use Braidotti’s term affirmative ethics as a location to read through.

For Braidotti, the raison d’être of the whole project is affirmative ethics, and its ontological ground is zoe. Because the individual “I” is not the center of life but a temporary passing of it, the logic of retribution, rights-claims, and compensation: all premised on a stable individual subject: dissolves. What replaces it is an “ethics of affirmation” that “suspends the quest for both claims and compensation and resists the logic of retribution or rights” (Braidotti, 2010, p. 214). In Transpositions, she writes that “an ethics of sustainability” structures her argument and that “this transformative ethics includes a critical or reactive and an affirmative or active phase” (Braidotti, 2006, p. 5). She asks how to cultivate “the political desire for change or transformation” and describes this as a change in both “our schemes of thought” and “our ways of inhabiting the world” (Braidotti, 2006, p. 5). In later work she states that “our politics must be guided by an affirmative ethics” and defines ethics as “a discourse about forces and relations, and not as a set of normative moral injunctions” (Braidotti, 2024, p. xiii). Bodily materialism, in this account, produces “a new empirical-discursive methodological approach that combines critique with the creation of alternatives” (Braidotti, 2024, p. xiii). Braidotti defines affirmative ethics directly as “the establishment of mutually empowering relations based on cooperation and the productive combination of the specific degrees of intensity or potentia of each living entity,” which “aims at increasing each entity’s capacity to preserve themselves against adverse forces” (Braidotti, 2022, p. 97). She also names it as “the affect that binds together the heterogeneous components of the complex zoe-geo-techno-subjects” (Braidotti, 2022, p. 97).

Haraway frames the same orientation through response and relation. In Staying with the Trouble, she describes the task as “learning to stay with the trouble of living and dying in response-ability on a damaged earth” (D. J. Haraway, 2016, p. 2). On the opening page, she writes that the task is “to make kin in lines of inventive connection as a practice of learning to live and die well with each other in a thick present” (D. J. Haraway, 2016, p. 1). Haraway names the task as cultivating “the capacity to respond to worldly urgencies with each other” (D. J. Haraway, 2016, p. 7).

Bennett does not use the phrase affirmative ethics in Vibrant Matter, but she explicitly presents her project in ethical and political terms. The political project, she writes, is “to encourage more intelligent and sustainable engagements with vibrant matter and lively things.” She asks how political responses might change if we took seriously “the vitality of (nonhuman) bodies,” defining vitality as the capacity of things not only to impede human designs but also to act “as quasi agents or forces with trajectories, propensities, or tendencies of their own” (Bennett, 2010, p. viii). She also writes that “alongside and inside singular human agents there exists a heterogeneous series of actants with partial, overlapping, and conflicting degrees of power and effectivity” (Bennett, 2010, p. 23). Later in the preface, she describes the ethical task as cultivating “the ability to discern nonhuman vitality” and becoming “perceptually open to it” (Bennett, 2010, p. xiv).

Barad makes ethics a matter of accountability for the cuts that enact inclusion and exclusion (Barad, 2007). “Objectivity,” Barad writes, means “being accountable for marks on bodies, that is, specific materializations in their differential mattering.” Barad continues: “We are responsible for the cuts that we help enact,” and “the cuts that we participate in enacting matter” (Barad, 2007, p. 179). Because “intra-actions cut ‘things’ together and apart,” ethics is not about a radically exterior other, since “‘they’ and ‘we’ are co-constituted and entangled through the very cuts ‘we’ help to enact” (Barad, 2007, p. 179). Agency, in this framework, includes “the possibilities and accountability entailed in reconfiguring material-discursive apparatuses of bodily production, including the boundary articulations and exclusions that are marked by those practices” (Barad, 2007, p. 214). Acknowledgment of “nonhuman agency” does not reduce human responsibility. It requires “that much more attentiveness to existing power asymmetries” (Barad, 2007, p. 219).

The cut is therefore, as Barad puts it, the site at which responsibility and possibility meet: “particular possibilities for acting exist at every moment, and these changing possibilities entail a responsibility to intervene in the world’s becoming, to contest and rework what matters and what is excluded from mattering” (Barad, 2003, pp. 826–827).

Braidotti’s raison d’être, Haraway’s response-ability, Bennett’s perceptual discipline, and Barad’s agential cut resonate across the four accounts of affirmative ethics.

If this is what the world is, then we are already in it, and always have been.

Bennett states human power is also a kind of thing-power. From the perspective of evolutionary time, mineral efficacy becomes visible in the emergence of bone: “We are walking, talking minerals” (Bennett, 2010, pp. 10–12). The human is not an exception to matter but one of its temporary organizations. Bennett extends this further in her discussion of microbial life: “the its outnumber the mes,” so that it is no longer adequate to imagine the body as a single bounded being. “We are, rather, an array of bodies, many different kinds of them in a nested set of microbiomes” (Bennett, 2010, p. 113).

Alaimo elucidates on what this looks like at the level of a body. “Trans-corporeality means that all creatures, as embodied beings, are intermeshed with the dynamic, material world, which crosses through them, transforms them, and is transformed by them” (Alaimo, forthcoming, p. 1). The figure-ground relation between organism and environment dissolves as the outline of the human is traversed by substantial material interchanges. The trans-corporeal subject is generated through and entangled with biological, technological, economic, social, and political systems at vastly different scales (Alaimo, forthcoming, pp. 1–4). The ontological ground is therefore not elsewhere than embodiment.

The primary unit of reality necessary for navigation varies across theorists. For Bennett, the unit is the thing or actant within a distributed agency of assemblages (Bennett, 2010, pp. viii–ix, 37–38). For Barad, it is the phenomenon produced through specific material-discursive practices and agential cuts (Barad, 2007, pp. 128–129, 146, 175–176). For Braidotti, it is the nomadic subject traced through cartographies and transpositions (Braidotti, 2018, pp. 26–29).

Leibnizean matter/body has no voids: “folds replace holes,” and matter offers, as Deleuze glosses him, “an infinitely porous, spongy, or cavernous texture without emptiness,” each body elastic, “pierced with irregular passages,” dependent for its coherence on the pressure of external forces (Coole, 2010, p. 111). For Deleuze’s Leibniz, “the point of view is in the body” (Deleuze, 1993, p. 10). The world here is not made of discrete units standing in external relation, but of folds: matter endlessly folding and unfolding, generating provisional forms as styles of existing rather than fixed substances. It is not the flat geography of Cartesian extension, laid out before the lumen naturale “without dark recesses, crevices, or hollows” (Coole, 2010, p. 94). It is a topography of curves, labyrinths, and hollows, coiled and creased and rippled, with clarity “endlessly plunging into obscurity” (Coole, 2010, p. 111). The exemplary science of this ontology is not Euclidean geometry but origami, and its afterlives run through the pre-Socratics, Whitehead, postclassical physics, and the folded proteins of microbiology (Coole, 2010, p. 110).

Merleau-Ponty reads the same Leibnizian fold as a reversibility that “always miscarries at the last moment,” producing the dehiscence through which emergence remains possible rather than closing into inert repetition (Coole, 2010, p. 107). This is a teeming, turbulent, convoluted world of infinite folded matter, continuously folding and unfolding, its modulations yielding “provisional forms as styles of existing” rather than fixed substances (Coole, 2010, p. 111). The body does not perceive the world from a detached point of view. It does not inhabit a flat, geometrical, fully determinate plane but a milieu, an Umwelt that remains ambiguous, indeterminate, resonant with expressive significance.

Merleau-Ponty discerns French painter Paul Cézanne (1839-1906) as trying to capture these perspectival distortions as they occur in perception, in order to convey “an emerging order,” where the object is “in the act of appearing, organizing itself before our eyes” (Coole, 2010, p. 104). Cézanne’s painting brings the material world alive; it does not measure or represent reality but emulates the way it materializes in perception (Coole, 2010, p. 106). Coole notes that Merleau-Ponty explicitly contrasts this with the Cartesian tradition of classical perspective — what he calls “Renaissance perspective as a cultural fact” and “impoverished” as artistic style precisely because its Euclidean geometry presents nature in fixed, objective coordinates rather than in the ambiguous, indeterminate, resonant field in which it actually appears. Cézanne’s refusal of that convention could be read as both an aesthetic choice and a phenomenological position: the attempt to suspend culturally fashioned perception and uncover the “vertical” world of “brute” or “wild” perception as it emerges (Coole, 2010, p. 106).

The quantum ontology and the art-historical refusal of fixed perspective are entangled. Sullivan states when Cézanne captured a scene from multiple views, he “anticipated what the physicists were scratching their heads about at the time”, the late 1800s, early 1900s growth of explicit quantum theory (Sullivan, 2010, p. 12). Kontturi and Tiainen (2021) identify Bolt (2000) as one of the first art scholars to name quantum theory as a resource for visual practice, working from Kirby’s reading of Bohr rather than from Barad — placing art scholarship alongside, not downstream from, Barad’s development of agential realism.

Merleau-Ponty’s account of Cézanne resonates with Bennett’s account of lingering. Bennett espouses lingering as the practice adequate to inhabiting this world. She calls for methods that cultivate a “patient, sensory attentiveness to nonhuman forces operating outside and inside the human body” (Bennett, 2010, p. xiv). Without this “countercultural kind of perceiving,” the world appears as if it consists only of “active human subjects who confront passive objects and their law-governed mechanisms” (Bennett, 2010, p. xiv). Bennett renders the Deleuzian virtual as “a life”: “a pure event, freed from the subjectivity and objectivity of what happens,” a “subjective current visible only fleetingly” that “tears the fabric of the actual without ever fully coming out in a person placed or thing”; it is “matter of movement or matter energy, a matter in variation that enters assemblages and leaves them” (Bennett, 2010, p. 53).

A knower who is part of what she knows cannot know from above. She has to know where she is to find a location from which to travel.

Feminist poet Adrienne Rich (1929-2012) names the body as the starting location. In “*Of Woman Born*”, she writes:

> Patriarchal thought has limited female biology to its own narrow specifications. The feminist vision has recoiled from female biology for these reasons; it will, I believe, come to view our physicality as a resource, rather than a destiny. In order to live a fully human life we require not only control of our bodies (though control is a prerequisite); we must touch the unity and resonance of our physicality, our bond with the natural order, the corporeal ground of our intelligence. (Rich, 1976, p. 40)

In “Blood, Bread, and Poetry”, Rich advises: “Begin not with a continent or a country or a house, but with the geography closest in: the body” (Rich, 1984, p. 212). Bennett identifies “feminist studies of the body” as part of the larger trend that put “desire and bodily practices back on the ethical radar screen,” a key precondition for her own move toward nonhuman vitality (Bennett, 2010, p. xi). Braidotti traces feminist neo-materialism to Rich’s politics of location, tracing a line from Rich through situated knowledges and standpoint debates (Braidotti, 2022). She identifies “feminist philosophies of neo-materialism and diversity” as a fundamental source for navigating the posthuman convergence and defines the politics of location as “a method based on the notions of embodiment and lived experience” (Braidotti, 2024, p. xvii). Feminist theory, she writes, emphasizes the “situated and accountable nature of knowledge,” introduces “the embodied and embedded,” and holds that “Being embodied: sexed and gendered subjects embedded in the world: is the starting point for critical reflection” (Braidotti, 2024, pp. xvii–xviii). Bodily materialism produces “a new empirical-discursive methodological approach that combines critique with the creation of alternatives” (Braidotti, 2024, p. xviii). Braidotti’s nomadic subject and cartography give this orientation its methodological form: nomadic because the knower moves rather than settles, cartographic because the account is a map drawn from somewhere rather than a picture taken from above.

Trinh T. Minh-ha brings the politics of location and the refusal of closure into film practice. In “Documentary Is/Not a Name,” she argues that “meaning should be prevented from coming to closure at either what is said or what is shown,” and identifies “the interval, a break without which meaning would be fixed and truth congealed” as the condition that prevents closure (Minh-ha, 1990, p. 77). Haraway later takes up Trinh’s formulation of “critical difference within” in “The Promises of Monsters” (1992, pp. 299–300), linking this refusal of closure to feminist accounts of situated knowledge and difference. Haraway pushes the epistemological argument further in “Situated Knowledges.” The “god trick,” the claim to disembodied universal vision, is not a neutral description of scientific method but an effect of power. Against it, Haraway argues for “politics and epistemologies of location, positioning, and situating, where partiality and not universality is the condition of being heard to make rational knowledge claims” (D. Haraway, 1988, p. 589).

Somerville names the condition in research practice as the post-human I. Accompanying children on weekly creek walks, she writes: “I feel as if I am changed through these videos, learning to be human differently in the company of the creek and children” (Somerville, 2016, p. 1165), and later: “I become other-to-myself, as if I migrate outwards into these scenes that form and reform around me” (Somerville, 2016, p. 1167). There is, she writes, no sense of following a research question and finding an answer, only “complete immersion in the event of place” (Somerville, 2016, p. 1167) (citing Massey, 2005). The account remains hers, but the “I” is no longer outside the field it describes.

This navigation requires methods be cartographic rather than classificatory. Cartography is generative: it maps forces, movements, and encounters as they unfold, producing a “living map”, Braidotti’s phrase, rather than a fixed taxonomy. Cartographic methods trace forces, movements, and encounters as they unfold rather than assigning phenomena to pre-existing categories. Cartographic methods are accountable to the apparatus: every account is grounded in specific political and material conditions rather than in a claim to universality that suppresses the apparatus that produced it (Braidotti, 2024, p. xvii) and the instrument of knowing is not neutral with respect to what it knows but constitutive of it, and rigor lies in naming and specifying the apparatus rather than treating it as absent (Barad, 2007, pp. 29–30, 140–141). They are affirmative rather than only critical: they attend not only to what is wrong but also to what is being produced and what is becoming possible (Braidotti, 2024, pp. xiii, xviii). Cartographic methods refuse exhaustive capture: generativity is treated as a condition of accountable knowledge in a world of ongoing becoming.

The critique of researcher bias has traveled a specific arc across fields. The positivist ideal -- the view from nowhere, the neutral observer, the God's trick Haraway names -- gave way, under pressure from feminist, postcolonial, and interpretive traditions, to the demand for positionality disclosure. The researcher was asked to name her location: her race, gender, institutional affiliation, theoretical commitments. This disclosure was framed as a corrective -- a way of flagging potential distortion so that readers could adjust. The assumption remained that bias was a contamination to be acknowledged and, where possible, minimized. Research was still understood as something that would ideally happen without a situated subject in the way.

Posthumanist and new materialist frameworks reject the premise. If the world is always-becoming through intra-action, if there is no phenomenon prior to the apparatus that produces it, if matter and meaning co-constitute each other through specific material-discursive arrangements -- then there is no view from outside. The researcher is not adjacent to the research. She is part of the apparatus. Her situatedness does not introduce error into an otherwise clean process; it is constitutive of the process itself. Barad is unequivocal: the apparatuses that produce phenomena are not separate from what they produce. The agential cut that makes a phenomenon legible is always already enacted from within the entanglement, never from outside it. On this account, the demand that a researcher minimize her bias is a demand that she pretend to be something the ontology says she cannot be.

This move -- from situatedness as contamination to situatedness as constitutive -- was not invented by posthumanism. It was worked out first in feminist practice. Rich's "politics of location" is its founding gesture: the demand to begin not with a pretense of neutrality but with the body, the specific geography closest in, as the only honest epistemological starting point. Braidotti traces the line from Rich directly into new materialism's situated epistemology (*New Materialism*, p. 22), and Mąkowska (2022) shows that what Rich was enacting in her poetics -- mapping her own flesh as medical history, sociopolitical inscription, and epistemological ground simultaneously -- is structurally identical to what Barad will later formalize as the researcher's position within the apparatus. The apparatus argument is not a posthumanist novelty. It is feminist body theory formalized. Haraway's "God's trick" critique is the same critique Rich was making in 1984 when she insisted that there is no speaking from outside one's location, only from within it. What changes between Rich and Barad is vocabulary and philosophical frame. The underlying claim -- that the knower is always already inside what she studies, that this is not a problem to be corrected but a condition to be worked -- is continuous.

The artist-researcher takes this one step further, but the move is not toward greater centrality -- it is toward a more precise account of her position within a distributed apparatus. Braidotti's cartography is not the map one neutral observer makes of a pre-existing terrain. It is the specific trajectory of a specific nomadic subject moving through a specific configuration of forces, in intra-action with the other human and nonhuman actants that constitute the apparatus alongside her. The knowledge produced is not generalizable in the traditional sense. It does not claim to represent what any researcher would find. It claims something more precise and more honest: this is what became thinkable through this particular assemblage -- this subject, this material, this set of encounters, this configuration of time/space/matter -- and the research artifact produced through it is offered as evidence to other entities capable of perceiving it. The situated cartography is not the packaging around the findings. It is the findings.

What the artist-researcher brings specifically is a transposition framework -- the unique mediation through which the assemblage produces a research artifact that can be shared as evidence with other perceiving entities. She is one actant within the apparatus, not its sovereign. She does not produce the research phenomenon alone; she enters into intra-action with materials, spaces, other bodies, institutional structures, and the accumulated force of prior encounters, all of which participate with equal ontological weight in what gets made. Her situatedness is the particular node through which the apparatus becomes this inquiry, generating this knowledge, for this community of reception. Specificity is not a limitation on generalizability. It is the condition of possibility for any knowledge at all.

Positionality and accountability for one's unique nomadic position is not a methodological add-on. It is constitutive of how artists especially do research and participate in knowledge work. The artist-researcher is always already an embodied transpositional apparatus -- as Rauschenberg was, as Cage was, as Cézanne was -- enacting the ontological ground before and beyond any vocabulary available to name it. What changes with the vocabulary is not the practice but its legibility: to other practitioners, to institutions, to the broader knowledge community.

The distinction between posthumanism and new materialism established across Part One is a theoretical distinction -- it maps different emphases, genealogies, and methodological centers of gravity. What confirms it from the direction of practice is that the protocols researchers have developed under the posthumanist and new materialist umbrella reproduce exactly the division of labor the theoretical account predicts. Lambert (submitted) uses NM concepts -- intra-action, assemblage, spacetimemattering -- to operationalize the PH ethical stance of refusing the humanist reflective practitioner. Hill (2017) frames the three modes of becoming-diffractive in explicitly PH terms -- the decentered subject, the refusal of the individual teacher as autonomous agent -- but operationalizes them through NM conceptual tools: intra-action, becoming-with, assemblage, affect. Martin, Sayers, and Carroll (2025) develop their six transposition practices on the PH side of the distinction -- critique of Man, nomadic subject, affirmative ethics -- but anchor them in a NM ontology of relational becoming. In each case, PH provides the critical-ethical vector that says why the practice matters and what is at stake, and NM provides the material-ontological vector that says what the world is made of and how phenomena are constituted. Neither framework can do the other's work. The practice texts confirm the theoretical distinction not by citing it but by enacting it.

This is where Part One opens onto Part Two. If posthumanism and new materialism together establish that the world is relational, processual, and virtual before it is actual, then the question becomes how to inhabit and work within that world as a researcher and practitioner. Diffractive practice is the response to that question -- and it is the subject of what follows.
