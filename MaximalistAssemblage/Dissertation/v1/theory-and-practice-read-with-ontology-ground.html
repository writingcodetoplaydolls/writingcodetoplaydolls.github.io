#Assemblage: agencement in translation, read against the art term

My framing also needs to trace the roots of the term assemblage as a translation from the French \[agencement\] see Deleuze read against the history of the term in art

\[Note\] This section follows one word through two histories and reads them through each other, the method in 2.0. The word already does a lot of work across the proposal: your Maximalist Assemblage notes (1.4), your earlier works ("Assemblages (at the time I called them experiential films)," 1.3), Bennett's assemblages (2.1), and Rauschenberg's combines as assemblages "in Bennett's sense" (2.3 bridge, 2.4). It sits at the end of Chapter 2 for now; it could move up to follow 2.1, where the key terms are defined. Your own definition was cut from the final:

All three describe a universe where there are no essential enduring qualities of any thing, thus the thing itself is not completely knowable. Because reality is created through specific configurations of space and matter that only exist as they are in one unit of time, as soon as we think we have divined some essential nature, some knowable character, the configuration is gone. Bennett stages us in assemblages of agential swarms of thing-power flowing in and out of the primordial swerve. Braidotti connects Bennett's always-becoming assemblages as situated cartographies from and through which nomadic subjects enact affirmative ethics. Through these entangled assemblages and cartographies and unique material discursive engagements we enact the agential cuts through embodied apparatus and transpositions.

An assemblage is a unique configuration of things and actants that come together in a particular space/time/matter configuration. The things act on each other through a mechanism of call and response. Agency is distributed, thus an assemblage can also be imagined as an agential swarm. The foundational unit of reality for Bennett is the thing or actant.

#Strand 1: agencement, the philosophical term

\[Note\] What the sources say so far. Check every page number and date against the copies you cite.

In everyday French, agencement means arrangement, layout, fitting, or fixing (Kennedy et al., 2013). Puar glosses it as "design, layout, organization, arrangement, and relations," with "the focus being not on content but on relations" (Puar, 2012, p. 57).

Deleuze and Guattari "first introduce the agencement concept in Kafka: Toward a Minor Literature (\[1975\] 1986) and hold on to it all the way to their late work What is Philosophy? (1991)" (Prokić, 2021, p. 547). Kafka is already on your v35 glossary list. In Kafka, speech belongs to "collective assemblages of enunciation" rather than to an individual speaking subject. That is a direct line into language and literacy; find the page in the Polan translation.

A widely cited definition is in Dialogues (1977). Online copies differ slightly, so check the wording against the print edition:

What is an assemblage? It is a multiplicity which is made up of many heterogeneous terms and which establishes liaisons, relations between them, across ages, sexes and reigns – different natures. Thus, the assemblage's only unity is that of co-functioning: it is a symbiosis, a "sympathy". It is never filiations which are important, but alliances, alloys; these are not successions, lines of descent, but contagions, epidemics, the wind. (Deleuze & Parnet, 2007, p. 69)

The translation. Paul Foss and Paul Patton first rendered agencement as "assemblage" in their 1981 translation of "Rhizome." Later translators, including Brian Massumi in A Thousand Plateaus (1987), kept it (Bogic, 2017, p. 140).

The critique of the translation.

Phillips (2006) argues that Deleuze and Guattari rarely used the French word assemblage in a philosophical sense, and that the English rendering can "give rise to connotations based on analogical impressions" (quoted on Wikipedia; confirm in Phillips).

Bogic: in French the word "implies both a process and a state of being: both the act of fitting/arranging and the arrangement itself," while "the English word assemblage tends to imply only the state of being, therefore eliminating the active aspect" (2017, p. 140).

Puar: "'Assemblage' is actually an awkward translation of the French term agencement," and the question is "the generative effects of this 'mistranslation'" (2012, p. 57).

Frohmann: "Agencement suggests design, a sense missing from the French assemblage" (2012, cited in Goehring, 2019, p. 13).

Downstream. DeLanda builds a large-scale social ontology on the English term (2006; 2016). Prokić (2021) and Buchanan (2015) argue that it departs from Deleuze and Guattari. Bennett's "agency of assemblages" (2010, ch. 2) is the line that reaches your comps. Pennycook's Language Assemblages (2024), already in your references, carries the term into applied linguistics, and Siffrinn and Coda (2025, p. 195), in your comps, take the assemblage as the basic unit of inquiry there: your LLSS bridge. Your comps keep the units distinct (Bennett's thing or actant, Barad's phenomenon), so assemblage is not the universal unit.

#Strand 2: assemblage, the art term

Strand 2: assemblage, the art term

\[Note\] What the sources say so far:

Jean Dubuffet used "assemblage" from 1953 for his assemblages d'empreintes, relief collages that included butterfly wings. He is credited as the first to use the word for art practice (National Galleries Scotland, n.d.; Seitz, as summarized in Cotz, 2023).

William C. Seitz's exhibition The Art of Assemblage opened at the Museum of Modern Art in fall 1961 (October 2–November 12 per Koregos; Wikipedia gives October 4), then traveled to Dallas and San Francisco in 1962. His catalogue sets two criteria: the works are "predominantly assembled rather than painted, drawn, modeled, or carved," and "entirely or in part, their constituent elements are preformed natural or manufactured materials, objects, or fragments not intended as art materials" (Seitz, 1961; confirm the page in MoMA's free PDF of the catalogue).

Seitz traced the first assemblage to Picasso in 1912 (Cotz, 2023). The show placed Braque, Picasso, Schwitters, Duchamp, and Dubuffet beside Man Ray, Cornell, Rauschenberg, and the West Coast artists Bruce Conner, George Herms, Jess, and Edward Kienholz.

Also in the lineage, and worth checking against Seitz's list: Elsa von Freytag-Loringhoven; Louise Nevelson's found-wood walls; Noah Purifoy's assemblages from the debris of the 1965 Watts rebellion (66 Signs of Neon, 1966); John Outterbridge; Betye Saar's The Liberation of Aunt Jemima (1972).

A lead for your erasure genealogy (2.4): MoMA publishes the 1961 master checklist. Counting who was in the room, by gender and race, would let you test the canon rather than assert it.

#Reading the two strands through each other

Reading the two strands through each other

\[Note\] Interference points to test, not conclusions. Keep, cut, or rewrite any of them.

Order of arrival. The art word came first: Dubuffet in 1953, the MoMA canon in 1961. Agencement came in 1975, and "assemblage" as its English rendering in 1981. So in English the philosophical concept arrives in a word that already names art made from found matter. Phillips' "analogical impressions" may include exactly this. Does the art history leak into how English-language theory, Bennett included, pictures an assemblage: as a thing you can stand in front of?

The verb that went missing. Bogic says the English word keeps the arrangement and loses the act of arranging. The art term holds both, but in sequence: the act happens in the studio, and the museum receives the fixed object. A generative installation and a website that keeps rearranging put the verb back. Your artifact can be an agencement in the full French sense.

Layout. One everyday meaning of agencement is layout. A website's layout is an agencement that the people moving through it rearrange. Your reading site (2.0) already works this way.

"Not intended as art materials." Seitz's second criterion is about matter with a prior life: thing-power named from the art side, half a century before Bennett. It supports your claim that Rauschenberg's combines were assemblages "in Bennett's sense before Bennett named them" (2.4).

Collective enunciation. For Deleuze and Guattari, speech belongs to collective assemblages, not to a sovereign speaker. That joins the art lineage's "refusal of a sovereign authoring subject" (your v35 phrase about Rauschenberg) to literacy: reading and writing as arrangements of many voices, which is what your thick-text site did for students.

Translation as a cut. Bogic writes that "translation flows in academia and elsewhere are not coincidental but are rather reflections of geopolitics, socio-historical moments, and power relations" (2017, p. 145). A translation that fixed a process into a thing may be a small instance of the erasure you trace in 2.4: the insistence on the word, the one stable meaning. That one is my association; it is yours to keep or cut.

Your own naming. You called your earlier works assemblages (1.3). Your naming sits in the art lineage, and your theory sits in the agencement lineage. The proposal can be where the two interfere.

Trinh. Reassemblage (1982), already in your comps, is a third node: a film titled with the word, made against the ethnographic gaze.

#Diffraction: assemblage and the found object

\[Note\] Both practices take up matter "not intended as art materials" (Seitz's phrase, Strand 2). Read through each other, they place agency differently. What the sources say, then interference points to test.

The readymade. Tate: "Term used by the French artist Marcel Duchamp to describe works of art he made from manufactured objects." The first is Bicycle Wheel (1913). Fountain (1917), a urinal signed with a false name, was submitted to the Society of Independent Artists exhibition in New York (Tate, n.d.).

Choice as the act. The anonymous editorial in The Blind Man (May 1917): "He CHOSE it. He took an ordinary article of life, and placed it so that its useful significance disappeared under the new title and point of view – created a new thought for that object" (quoted in Tate, n.d.).

Said at the assemblage show. Duchamp gave "Apropos of 'Readymades'" at MoMA's The Art of Assemblage: A Symposium on October 19, 1961: the choice was "based on a reaction of visual indifference, with at the same time a total absence of good or bad taste" (quoted in Tate, n.d.). The readymade's own account was given inside the exhibition that canonized assemblage.

Whose choice. Irene Gammel first proposed that Elsa von Freytag-Loringhoven made Fountain; Glyn Thompson and Julian Spalding argue it. Dawn Adès and Bradley Bailey disputed it in the Burlington Magazine (2019): "there is not a single piece of direct evidence that ties her to Fountain" (Whiddington, 2023).

Bennett's found assemblage. Vibrant Matter opens thing-power with debris in a storm drain:

On a sunny Tuesday morning on 4 June in the grate over the storm drain to the Chesapeake Bay in front of Sam's Bagels on Cold Spring Lane in Baltimore, there was: one large men's black plastic work glove one dense mat of oak pollen one unblemished dead rat one white plastic bottle cap one smooth stick of wood (Bennett, 2010, p. 4)

Found material as teaching. Albers taught at Black Mountain with everyday materials such as leaves, paper, and wire; confirm in Horowitz and Danilowitz (2006). See 2.6.

Found language. Found poetry is made from existing text. Prendergast (2006) uses it as a literature review, and Butler-Kisber (2002) traces it in qualitative research: a lead for 2.0.

\[Note\] Interference points to test:

One chooser or many relations. The readymade puts the act in a single choice and the frame that receives it ("He CHOSE it"). Assemblage puts it in the relations among many found things (Deleuze and Parnet's "co-functioning"). Your v35 phrase about Rauschenberg is "the refusal of a sovereign authoring subject." Does the readymade keep the sovereign chooser that assemblage gives up?

The frame as apparatus. A urinal becomes art through the frame that receives it: the exhibition, the signature, the title. In Barad's terms the frame is the apparatus, and the readymade makes its cut visible. Assemblage folds the cut into the arrangement.

"He." The 1917 editorial says "He CHOSE it." The attribution dispute turns on that pronoun. Read it with the erasure genealogy in 2.4.

Nobody chose the storm drain. Bennett's glove, pollen, rat, cap, and stick were gathered by water and a grate, not by an artist: found and assembled with no chooser, an agencement in the everyday sense of an arrangement.

Found text. Your reading site finds terms and sets them in relation across texts. Is that closer to the readymade (selection), to found poetry, or to assemblage (relation)?

Your work. Your words: "the previous work begins with the potato bin." Is the potato bin a found object, an assemblage, or the apparatus that does the finding?
