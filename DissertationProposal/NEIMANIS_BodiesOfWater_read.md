## 1. Figuration, and the answer to today's question

This is the clearest statement of the metaphor position found anywhere in the folder, and it arrives in the first ten pages.

She starts from **Grosz (2012: 14-15)** on concepts as "the production of immaterial forces", "virtualities of matter", "excess over matter", where "materiality does not contain this incorporeal", and refuses it:

> "Instead of sticking with the Deleuzian concept . . . I therefore prefer the posthuman feminist understanding of concepts as '**figurations**'. **I suggest we might understand figurations as embodied concepts.** Donna Haraway (2007: 4-5) calls them '**material-semiotic**' knots, referring to their conceptual power, but also to their worldliness. Similarly, Rosi Braidotti (2011: 10) refers to figurations as '**living maps**' that acknowledge 'concretely situated historical positions'. Figurations are keys for imagining and living otherwise, but **unlike a concept unfettered by the world we actually live in or as, figurations are importantly grounded in our material reality** (I have never been entirely convinced by theory that frames anything as wholly 'immaterial'). I like the idea that **our best concepts are already here, semi-formed and literally at our fingertips, awaiting activation. Never conceptual fantasy or metaphor, these imaginative 'interventions' (Braidotti 2011: 14) describe what we already are, but amplified.**"

**"Describe what we already are, but amplified"** is the operative clause, and *amplify* becomes a method verb in chapter 1.

On the tradition she takes it from, which is the sentence to set beside everything found today:

> "**In these works, the wateriness of bodies is always more than metaphorical, and watery matter is recognized as a literal wellspring for new ontological and ethical paradigms.**"

Three further things in the same section. Figurations "are not arbitrary, but **arise in response to a particular contemporary question or problem**", so the choice of water rather than air, rock, earth "even plastic at a growing rate" is argued rather than assumed. Figuration as protest, quoting **Braidotti (2006: 170)**: a "**literal expression**" of those parts of us that the "phallogocentric regime" has "declared off-limits" and "does not want us to become". And **Haraway (1992: 86)**: "**feminist theory proceeds by figuration at just those moments when its own historical narratives are in crisis**", which is about "resetting the stage for possible pasts and futures".

**Her three starting points are this project's three starting points.** "Bodies of Water actually began . . . in three places simultaneously. In theoretical terms, one of these was **embodied phenomenology, learned primarily through Maurice Merleau-Ponty**. The second was **the rhizomatics of Gilles Deleuze and Felix Guattari** . . . But **French écriture féminine**, the third starting point, is for me the most significant", naming **Irigaray and Cixous** as "**literally writing a new kind of embodiment into being**". The space "that combusted between Merleau-Ponty and Deleuze and Guattari, that is between phenomenology and posthumanism", is chapter 1.

## 2. The made and the real, with both guards in place

The companion section, "Water is what we make it", takes its title from **Jamie Linton (2010: 3)**, geographer, whose genealogy of "**modern water**" is the frame: water expressed as H2O only in the eighteenth century, made an object of the hydrological sciences in the nineteenth, "at which point we became able to conceive of it as **abstracted from bodies and environments**", "deterritorialized, rendered '**placeless**'", "all water . . . made known as an **abstract, isomorphic, measurable quantity**". Linton's claim: "**water bears the traces of its social relations, conditions, and potential**" (7). Neimanis translates that into Barad: "**the water crisis is worlded from the entanglement of material water scarcity and pollution with our idea of water**."

Then the two-sided claim, made in two moves:

> "So I agree with Linton: 'water is what we make it'. **In the first place, 'water' is our idea**, our imaginary, our naming of matter in a way that corresponds to our worldviews . . . **But water is also 'what we make it' quite literally.** Since we human and more-than-human animators of life are all bodies of water, **we give material form, shape, and meaning to that which exists in the abstract in only a very circumscribed way.** . . . Water is thus also specifically what we make it, in the sense that **it is not simply something 'out there', environment, resource, commodity, backdrop, but also the stuff of human bodies**."

And the guard against the obvious objection, in her own words rather than a reader's:

> "**Crucially, in understanding the ways in which we give shape to water, we can't slip into a naïve constructivism, as though this wet stuff were somehow conjured by human minds. Water, whatever we call it, calls to us too, from a realm of materiality that is also, simultaneously, more-than-human and beyond any kind of intentional grasp.** . . . **dissolution of knowability, or containability, or mastery, is part of water's ontologic. And, inasmuch as it is what we make it, water is always making us too.**"

## 3. The method: "Access, amplify, describe", and proxy stories

Chapter 1's second half is a worked method, and it is the nearest thing in this folder to what a practice-based first-person project needs.

**The procedure, named.** It comes from Mallin's **body hermeneutics**, where "anything useful or interesting needs to be **teased out, poked, prodded, and coaxed through a descriptive practice**", and Neimanis compresses it to three verbs: "**Access, amplify, describe.**"

**The problem she then raises against her own method**, which is the honest part:

> "in a very practical sense, it is not immediately clear how the tools of an embodied phenomenological analysis would be applicable to experiences that are **below or beyond human-scaled perception. How do we grasp the virtual?** How do we trace the wateriness of our distant pasts, our unknowable futures, or of our body's own microscopic internal seascapes? How do we map transcorporeal transits that happen out of view or out of focus . . . the workings of the cerebral spinal fluid that occupies my subarachnoid space, or **the journey of my SSRI-laced urine into estuarine communities downstream**, or the dissipation of my perspiration into a humid forest atmosphere?"

**Her answer, and it is the part that bears on the metaphor question:**

> "I suggest that '**proxy stories**' can be avenues for de-sedimenting our human-scaled perspective. **These stories are not substitutes for embodied experience; they are its amplifiers and sensitizers. Art, for example, is an amplifier.**"

> "**The desire to draw on these stories does not represent a failure of phenomenology to get 'back to the things themselves'; it is rather an affirmation that these stories too are pulled from a material world, but then condensed, concentrated, and given back to us such that we can more readily access and amplify them, anew.**"

A proxy story is not a lesser stand-in for the experience. It is drawn from the same material world and returned concentrated. **That is the claim about figures, restated as a claim about artworks, inside a methodology.**

**Two artworks are then described in the first person as the method being performed.** Not analysed: used.

> **Felix Gonzalez-Torres**, bead curtain, Koffler Gallery, Toronto, summer 2002: "between two rooms hangs a curtain, hundreds of strings of crystalline blue beads, plummeting freely but in unison to the gallery floor, looking for the lowest place, the path of least resistance. I position myself in the middle of the curtain, and let this cataract pour itself over me, **a baptism of string and glass**. I can't move. Beneath its persistent pressure, I am prevented from floating away. I feel the weight of water . . . **Even though this 'water' isn't 'wet', it activates and amplifies in me the lived experience of water as undertow, as stronger than my own measly self.**"

> **Rebecca Belmore**, *Fountain* (2005), video installation: "projected behind a wall of cascading water, Rebecca Belmore gathers the sea in a bucket, and grunting and struggling throws its contents at me, the viewer: a thick ropy red runs down the screen, and **I feel the waters of my white settler body flow into the colonized waters of Turtle Island** (also known as Canada). Belmore's video installation *Fountain* (2005) **allows me to access, and amplify, my own watery politics of location**, channelling through my corporeal seas."

**And science as a second kind of proxy story**, which is the 2.2 and 1.5 material. **Ulrich Beck**, via Alaimo (2010: 19): many contemporary embodied experiences "require . . . the '**sensory organs**' of science, theories, experiments, measuring instruments, in order to become visible or interpretable". **Alaimo's "syncretic assemblages"** of knowledge, "needed to understand the ways in which our bodily matter is implicated in a world that cannot be adequately grasped through one mode of inquiry alone". **And a recorded disagreement between two held authors**: "**Even if Alaimo (2009: 23) elsewhere insists that the 'trans-corporeality' she describes is 'not a phenomenological . . . stance', perhaps this surmisal gives short shrift to the value of phenomenological description**, and also instates too wide a gap between the attunement of phenomenologists and that of natural scientists to the wonder of the world."

**Rona Lee (2012: 16)**, artist in residence at the National Oceanography Centre Southampton, is the practice-as-research-inside-science case, and it is better than Dalziell's because the artist was embedded: "despite the seemingly scopic emphasis of much ocean science and its apparent alignment to what might be called an **epistemology of distance**", "conversations at NOCS have revealed the extent to which **sensuous understandings; emotional response and imaginative projection inform oceanographic study**". One scientist told her that the sight of small jellyfish led him to reflect that "**had we evolved in an aquatic medium, buoyancy would have given rise to an unthinkably different metaphysics**"; another that a black smoker's temperature "**was brought home to him not by the gauges on his instrument panel, but the experience of watching a length of ducting**" deform.

## 4. Merleau-Ponty, and two pages for the page map

The plan holds a Merleau-Ponty **page map** built from the two Kirby books. This book adds two pages from two further works, and uses one of them against itself.

**Proximal distance, *Phenomenology of Perception* (1962: 302)**, which is a scale argument through phenomenology and is not in the map: the optimal distance between perceiver and perceived, beyond which "we would begin to lose our comfortable grip on things", illustrated as "**standing too far away from a painting, or trying to listen to the radio with the volume too low**". Neimanis extends it to time, "a nanosecond may not even seem to 'exist' to us, while deep time is equally hard to fathom", and then states the posthuman move: "**A posthuman phenomenology seeks to acknowledge our sense of comfort with this proximal distance while at the same time continuing our investigation of our lived experience beyond this point. When we slip beyond our comfortable proximal distance, we can better attune ourselves to our more-than-humanness.**" With the question that follows: "**what more might we learn about our bodies of water if we could stretch or shrink this proximal relationship?**"

**1962: viii, quoted twice and turned around.** First against science: phenomenological description over analysis and explanation is "**from the start a foreswearing of science**". Then, on the same page, for it: "**all my knowledge of the world, even my scientific knowledge, is gained from my own particular point of view, or from some experience of the world without which the symbols of science would be meaningless.**" Neimanis: "This is an opening to posthuman phenomenology, where **our bodies parse the findings of scientific knowledge through their various sensory apparatuses**."

**Merleau-Ponty, *Nature* (2003: 260)**, a third book and a new one for the map: "**we do not find either less numerous or simpler types by going back in the history of the earth**", used against evolutionary progress narratives.

## 5. Permeability, with sources: Hypersea, carrier bags, remembered water

**Hypersea** is the concept the permeability notes were missing, and it has evolutionary scientists behind it. **Mark and Dianna McMenamin (1994: 25)**: Hypersea is "**the interconnected system of terrestrial life that has extended the sea and taken it along for the ride**". Neimanis's gloss: the water that gave us life "is also the water that we humans in turn carry with us, in us. **We have literally incorporated this water.**"

**The adaptations, each with a citation, and all of them about boundary and intake.** The amniotic egg, which "kept amniote embryos perpetually in water thanks to their hard, calcium-rich shells", replacing the jelly encasing of fish and amphibian eggs. Salt and water uptake mechanisms ranging from "**the infiltration of the porous oral and anal surfaces of terrestrial woodlice**" (Little 1990: 204) to the Namibian desert beetle that "when fogs are dense, scuttles to the top of a sand dune, stands with its head down and belly up, and drinks the water that condenses on and then flows down its body toward its mouth" (205). And the list from Zimmer (1998: 109): "**the production of tough skin (to prevent excessive water loss), absorptive intestines (to allow water in), big lungs (to replace oxygen intake through water), and tears (to keep exposed eyes moist and allow vision to become acute)**."

**The carrier bag**, from **Ursula Le Guin's "The Carrier Bag Theory of Fiction"** (1989: 166-169) and **Elizabeth Fisher (1975)**: the earliest cultural invention was "a container to hold gathered products and some kind of sling or net carrier", not a weapon, so "**carrying . . . is the first trick that we learned to do**". Le Guin's reason for retelling it is first-person and is the practice-as-research point: "it '**grounds me, personally**', she writes, 'in human culture in a way I never felt grounded before'". Neimanis's extension: "**we ourselves, as bodies of water, are also evolutionary carrier bags**, facilitating the proliferation of the new by holding water and becoming literal gestational milieu for the other. **What we hold is this potential for diffractive relationality.**" And the bag "**holds things in a particular, powerful relation to each other and us**" (169). Her refrain through the chapter: "**Again, it matters what stories we tell.**"

**Water as memory and trace**, for 3.5 and 3.6. The sea squirt, "while its newborns are vertebrate, this ascidian reabsorbs its rocky spine somewhere along the path to maturity", so that "'progress' is like **a strange kind of amnesiac repetition**, repetition as the forgetting of a vertebrate future that for this creature quickly slips away as a morphogenetic past . . . **Past and future collide and switch.**" With the line for the traces chapters: "**Perhaps the watery bodies we gather in our carrier bags are also memories of a could-have-been and traces of futures not yet lived.**" And, on an expectant mother imagining the foetus as tadpole, "**Her pasts literally well up inside her, time a crumpled up tissue, now responding to the swell.**" Also "**Like those rarest of rivers, repetition flows both ways.**"

**Three things to be careful with in this chapter.** Neimanis states that **Haeckel "got it wrong"**: "ontogeny, as a rule, does not recapitulate phylogeny, nor is the reverse true", so the recapitulation material is used knowingly and against itself. The **Ferenczi** material (2005: 45), where dreams of water recall "the phylogenetic 'catastrophe' of the drying up of the seas", is introduced with her own hedge, "**lest these rememberings all sound a bit too precious**". And **Elaine Morgan's *The Aquatic Ape* (1982)** is in her reference list, which is a disputed hypothesis; check how she handles it before using anything from that thread.

## 6. The claim, and the first-person writing

The argument the whole book makes is stated on its second page, and it is the permeability claim with its three targets named:

> "**Blood, bile, intracellular fluid; a small ocean swallowed, a wild wetland in our gut; rivulets forsaken making their way from our insides to out, from watery womb to watery world: we are bodies of water.**"

> "as bodies of water **we leak and seethe, our borders always vulnerable to rupture and renegotiation**. With a drop of cliché, I could remind you that our human bodies are at least two-thirds water, but **more interesting than these ontological maths is what this water does**, where it comes from, where it goes, and what it means along the way. Our wet matters are in constant process of intake, transformation, and exchange, drinking, peeing, sweating, sponging, weeping. **Discrete individualism is a rather dry, if convenient, myth.**"

> "The bodies from which we siphon and into which we pour ourselves are certainly other human bodies (**a kissable lover, a blood transfused stranger, a nursing infant**), but they are just as likely a sea, a cistern, an underground reservoir of once-was-rain."

> "Watery embodiment thus presents a challenge to three related humanist understandings of corporeality: **discrete individualism, anthropocentrism, and phallogocentrism**. We also note that these three 'isms' are all deeply entangled, mutually enforcing the claims of each other."

She calls her version of situated knowledge a "**posthuman politics of location**", parsed "through contemporary feminist and posthuman understandings of **agential realism, transcorporeality, and queer temporalities**", with **posthuman gestationality** as its central term: "**the facilitative logic of our bodily water for gestating new lives and new forms of life, never fully knowable**", where "human reprosexual wombs are but one expression of a more general aqueous facilitative capacity: pond life, sea monkey, primordial soup, amphibious egg, the moist soil that holds and grows the seed."

**The first-person writing is the method in operation, not a defence of it.** The strongest instance:

> "A pressing thirst can similarly disorient the organizing project of our subject-selves. **Thirst diffracts me, I lose my focus. I cannot concentrate on the words on the page, or keep my thoughts trained. My throat searches for some forgotten cache of saliva and the incessant attempts to swallow distract me. ('What did you say? What was that again?')**"

And it is written to include bodies that are not hers: "those of us for whom our subjectivity has always been situated in/as a more-than-humanness: **one might be subjected to a chronic illness that means one's visceral body is perpetually unruly**; or one may be dependent on a water source too distant, too polluted, or too costly to respond to one's bodily needs. In these instances, one's assemblage of watery bodiedness can spill beyond the discrete comfort of the human, and in doing so **reemphasize the flimsy membrane that just barely holds this conceit together, but which is patrolled all the more for it**."

Her epigraphs are worth recording as a set, because one of them is already in the place file: **Jamie Linton**, "Water is what we make of it"; **Braidotti (2006a: 16)**, "We are in this together"; and **Adrienne Rich (1986: 217)**, "**The problem was that we did not know whom we meant when we said 'we'.**" With **Virginia Woolf (2000: 124)**, "**there are tides in the body**", and the Syilx Okanagan poet **Jeanette Armstrong (2006)**, "water is siwlkw" and siwlkw is "coursing / to become the body".

## 8. Cautions

**Masaru Emoto.** Neimanis describes his "high-speed photographs of water that capture the unique and revealing structures of water crystals at their moment of freezing", in which water exposed to the word "happiness" forms a symmetrical crystal and water exposed to "unhappiness" forms a broken one. **Emoto's work is widely regarded as pseudoscience and is not a citable experimental result.** Neimanis does not assert it: she writes "**Just a good story? Perhaps.** Or perhaps Emoto's photos reveal an intercorporeal . . . embodiment of affect, experienced at a more-than-human scale." **If any of this is used, it must be used as she uses it, as a proxy story offered with that hedge, and the hedge quoted.** The claim is logged in the citation-check file.

**Three other things to check before citing.** Every page number above is Neimanis's citation and none has been checked, including Grosz 2012: 14-15, Haraway 2007: 4-5 and 1992: 86, Braidotti 2011: 10 and 14 and 2006: 170, Linton 2010: 3, 7, 9, 14, 18, McMenamin 1994: 25, Little 1990: 204-5, Zimmer 1998: 109, Le Guin 1989: 166-169, Merleau-Ponty 1962: viii and 302 and 2003: 260, Ferenczi 2005: 45, Alaimo 2009: 23 and 2010: 19, Rona Lee 2012: 16, Rich 1986: 217, Woolf 2000: 124. **Elaine Morgan's *The Aquatic Ape*** is in the reference list and the aquatic ape hypothesis is not accepted; how she uses it was not read. And **Haeckel's recapitulation** is explicitly disowned in the text, so nothing in the "waters remembered" material should be quoted as if she endorsed it.

**Unread.** Chapter 2 in full (Irigaray, *Marine Lover*, gestationality as sexuate difference, "the onto-logic of amniotics"), the first half of chapter 3, and all of chapter 4 (the Anthropocene chapter, which holds the Belmore material at length and a section called "Learning from anticolonial waters"). **Chapter 2 is the Irigaray chapter and chapter 4 is the art chapter, so both are likely to repay reading.**
