## 1. Her two terms, in her own words

> "**Touching impermanence describes the experiential moment in an art encounter when one senses the enchanted reality of one's interconnections within the sentient matter-flow of existence.**"

> "**All matter in existence is constantly vibrating, changing, assembling and evolving into forms and organisms, cycling through decay and disintegration, then reforming again with diversity and difference; this is the impermanence of sentient matter-flow.** Humans are just one form of these reciprocal assemblages; **we are within and part of sentient matter-flow. We also co-create with sentient matter-flow, changing these cycles on micro and macro levels, just as they change us.**"

**The method is named as a methodology of touch, and the materials are listed:**

> "My art processes involve working with tactile materials such as **beeswax; tree branches, stumps and bark; paper; ash; rocks; ice; snow; charcoal; light and fungi. Engaging with these materials co-creatively involves a methodology of touch, multisensorily following materialities' sentient matter-flow. Acting with the material, I am present to the material's own sense of time, interactions, agency, histories, layers of interbeing and interconnections with surrounding matter. This requires being open to the mysteriousness of materials**, inviting moments of enchantment within art encounters and the realisation of touching impermanence."

The theoretical frame: "**drawing on the intersections between New Materialism discourses and Buddhist philosophy** to address aspects of phenomenology and eco-philosophy in the complexities of these art practices and artwork encounters." Nonduality is the Buddhist term doing the work: "**Nonduality is a fundamental concept in Buddhism, which understands subject and object as interconnected and enmeshed within webs of interbeing.**"

**And the thesis has a death in it, named as the source.** From the introduction: "it is **my only real, experiential, source material for what life is**. Gradually I have found that **reflecting on my father's final three weeks of life has compelled me to revaluate my own embodied existence through my art practice, and now more directly in this written thesis**, challenging me to more deeply understand how I experience the sense of touch connecting me to the world, to life and to the impermanence of existence."

## 2. The skin passage, which is the one to have

It is written as a meditation instruction and then turned into the claim. This is the longest sustained piece of first-person writing about skin in anything held.

> "There is a meditation practice of sitting with your eyes closed and focusing on being attentive to how your skin feels. **Touching the floor, its texture, feeling gravity and the weight of the parts of your body making contact with the ground, allowing yourself to sink into it. Feeling the air on your skin, its temperature and sometimes the slight movement of a breeze or draught. Noticing the sensation of your clothing on your skin: fine or coarse, smooth or rough, loose or tight around your body.** This awareness can extend to the hairs on your head, the heaviness of your eyes, the tension or relaxation of your muscles, your posture. As you feel the air moving gently in and out of your lungs with every breath, you begin to feel present within your body and your surrounds."

> "One aspect of this meditation method that particularly stays with me afterwards is **the sense of my skin as this permeable boundary, where my self meets and interacts with the world constantly**. Being attentive to how the air feels on my skin, **how it moves from external to internal as I breathe it in through my nose**, sensing its temperature, humidity and movement, and back out again. How the textures of the floor feel under my feet, whether barefoot or not. The textures and constraints of my clothing against my skin, whether my clothing is warm enough, or too warm, even sweaty. Sometimes I think about **how my skin feels in contact with the world when I'm swimming**, focusing on feeling how the water moves against my body, **or when I'm cycling, feeling the air rushing past**."

## 3. Leaky bodies, and the sentence that states the whole claim

> "The way one thinks of one's own body is challenged again by the new frontiers of microbial research. These Microbiome biological research projects show that **the human body is made up of 90% nonhuman micro-organisms**. Indeed, **our concept of our bodies as being purely human and a containment of self becomes increasingly porous. The boundaries of our bodies are not only growing and changing, they are permeable, we have 'leaky bodies', an unsettling, messy and very realistic term coined by feminist Margrit Shildrick.** Her notion of 'leaking' feels uncontrollable, somewhat repulsive and viscerally evocative. **Yet leaking is utterly essential, as Tim Ingold explains: 'things can exist and persist only because they leak: that is, because of the interchange of materials across the surfaces by which they differentiate themselves from the** [medium]'."

**That Ingold sentence is the permeability claim in one line**, and it arrives for the second time in three days: **Ingold's *Making* (2013) is the source Fahey builds her casting chapter on**, and here Ingold is the source for why leaking is necessary rather than shameful. **Two unrelated art doctorates, both on Ingold, neither of them in the reading plan.**

**Shildrick's "leaky bodies" is the third Shildrick item to surface this week**, after her 1996 monstrous-body chapter (held, OCR'd on 29 Oct) and the 2015 *Hypatia* article "'Why Should Our Bodies End at the Skin?'" found in Fahey's footnote. **The permeability lane now has one author with three separate items, two of them held.**

And a line that belongs with it, from the Bio Art discussion: "viewed from a microscopic perspective, **when a human dies the body actually becomes increasingly alive, teaming with masses of bacteria living on and consuming the skin**." With Bio Art described as working "to explore **the porous boundaries of what life is**", among practices "investigating **the expanding and contracting measurements of what is considered to live and be alive**", where "such margins are becoming increasingly blurred and porous". Her example is **Wim Delvoye's *Cloaca*** installations, which "mimic a precisely controlled human digestive system".

## 4. Touch is not cutaneous, and the senses are all touch

This is the argument of chapter three, and it is the part that reorganises the whole sensorium.

> "**Although the skin is the largest organ of the body, touch is not just cutaneous skin contact; it is deeper and more profound. Touch can be interpreted as uniting the senses of the human body, as without touch no other senses are possible.** Touch is not just cutaneous; what we feel with our hands and what comes into contact with our skin's surface (somatosensory). It encompasses **the impact of light touching our eyes so we can see (ophthalmoception); the vibrations picked up by the fine hairs and membranes in our ears so we can hear (audioception); the airborne molecules touching the inside of our noses so we can smell (olfacoception); and our tongues and palates touching food in our mouths so we can taste (gustaoception)**."

**And the four internal senses, named and defined**, via **Paterson, *The Senses of Touch: Haptics, Affects, and Technologies*** (ix, 20): "the perception of one's body in relation to gravity, movement and balance (**vestibular**); the perception of one's limbs in relation to each other, coordination, and the strength or effort being employed (**proprioception**); the ability to sense heat and cold (**thermoception**); and the perception of pain (**nociception**)." With Paterson's own caution, which is worth keeping: "**There is something ineffable, something barely articulable about internal bodily states and somatic sensations.**"

**Her chosen sense is thermoception**, and the body in it is named as skin:

> "**Focusing specifically on thermoception, I consider several art encounters that hinge on the touching felt contact of one's warm cutaneous body with freezing surrounds.** From **Ana Mendieta's snow *Siluetas*** to my snow, ash and ice installations and **Nicolas Folland's frozen chandeliers**, these experiential works rely on **the receptivity of touch** to connect the co-creative art encounter with sentient matter-flow."

The works: her own **Embrace (burrow) (2015)** and **Beguile (2015)**, made in Finnish snow, and **Handheld Melting (2016)** with ice and icicles; **Mendieta's snow *Siluetas* (1977)**; **Folland's *The door was open…* (2006)**, an icy frozen chandelier. "Art encounters with melting ice and snow form a felt present time experience, and invariably reference climate change concerns."

**Two more things in the chapter.** Touch defined as reaching and being reached: "Touch is both physical and emotional, **from the intentional act of reaching towards to the open receptivity of being touched by, and being in touch with, existence**." And touch as what makes detachment impossible: "this association of sight with analytical thinking and objective distance **is frequently confounded by the immediacy and bodily entanglement of touch, bringing interconnection to the fore and making detachment and impartiality nigh impossible. Touch is connection, and we are always, unavoidably touching the world around us.**"

## 5. Merleau-Ponty, used and then limited

Merleau-Ponty runs to 27 mentions, and the two terms she takes are **synergic totality** and the **intentional arc**:

> "Phenomenologist Maurice Merleau-Ponty argued that **the senses work together in 'synergic totality', forming an 'intentional arc' uniting the body and mind with one's experience in the world.**"

> "**Synergic totality is the way that our human senses intentionally reach towards the world. As such our senses arc into a porous experiential sphere where we encounter the world as a sensorium.** We perceive life from within this '**intentional arc which projects around us our past, our future, our human milieu** … [it] ensures that we are situated within all of these relationships. This intentional arc creates the unity of the senses, the unity of the sense[s and the intelligence]'."

**And then the limit, stated twice and in the same terms both times**, which is the objection that matters because this project runs on both phenomenology and posthumanism:

> "**because phenomenology privileges the first-person point of view, it is entirely human centric. Hence phenomenological empirical knowledge alone is insufficient to understand how humans are inextricably embedded within the world, and how the world is interconnected with them.**"

> "**twentieth century phenomenology is inescapably human-centric; it does not attribute value to materiality and sentient matter-flow.** To expand phenomenological theories, by acknowledging things' intentionality and human receptivity, is to incorporate the interconnections of experiential touch, as nondual, into a wider **co-creative felt phenomenology**."

Her resolution is the new materialisms, which "counter the dominant strains of twentieth century phenomenology by asserting '**a radical and imaginative realism that not only claims that things do exist beyond the purview of human conception, but that this existence is** [knowable]'". **Set beside Neimanis's "posthuman phenomenology", which does the same repair by a different route and keeps Merleau-Ponty's proximal distance, there are now two held texts repairing phenomenology's anthropocentrism in two different ways.** The difference is where the claim is.

## 6. One terminological collision worth not mistaking for a continuity

Her source for sentience is **Colin McGinn, "The Origin of Sentience", in *Prehension: The Hand and the Emergence of Humanity*** (MIT Press, 2015), 87-88: "the senses have been around for a long time, much longer than language or even thought", and sentience "**evolved from a kind of proto-sentience found in all matter. The preexisting form for whole-organism sentience is simply small-scale sentience, sentience at the molecular and atomic level.**"

**That book is called *Prehension*, and its prehension is the hand's grasp, not Whitehead's.** The plan tracks prehension as Whitehead's term, through Manning and through Renold and Ivinson's 36 uses. **These are two different words that happen to be spelled the same**, and a search on the term will now return both. Worth a line in the citation file so the two are never merged.

She also carries **the "Freeing of the Hands Hypothesis", "initially articulated by Charles Darwin in 1871"**, and **haptic thinking** defined plainly: "**thinking through doing with one's hands or body**", which "remains central to almost all modes of art practice".

## 7. Touch is under-researched, said in the same shape as fluidity

> "**Touch is however largely under researched.** Cultural historian **Constance Classen** argues that in the academic world '**touch has often passed under the radar. Like the air we breathe, it has been taken for granted as a fundamental fact of life, a medium for the production of meaningful acts, rather than meaningful in itself.**' Classen's seminal ***Book of Touch*** marks a growing interest in social studies research into this sense. **Touch is usually taken as a given, overlooked as 'somehow superfluous or inconsequential … relegated to common sense or parcelled off as automatic biological function, unworthy of more** [attention]'."

**That is the same sentence as Elizabeth Stephens on fluidity**, found three days ago and also newly visible: "Despite this focus on particular bodily fluids, **the concept of fluidity itself remains strangely uninterrogated**." **Two held texts, two of this project's own terms, the same diagnosis: the thing is everywhere and the concept is unexamined.**
