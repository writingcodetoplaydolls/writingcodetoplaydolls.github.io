## 1. The concept, and where the notation comes from

The whole article turns on a piece of notation, and he says where he got it: "In formulating A/autisms, I am indebted to a similar move within D/deaf studies, whereby the lower-case 'd' deaf indicates the state of non-hearing, while the upper-case 'D' indicates the rich linguistic and cultural identity of Deafness."

The problem it is for: "A tension is evident in critical autism studies as to how to orient oneself as a researcher towards neurodivergence. The contingency of the category 'autism', the importance of Autistic identity, and the material reality of autistic ability and disability mean that any singular tracing of autism is always problematic."

His answer is not to pick one. "Rather than understand these orientations as incommensurate, I draw from the queer inhumanisms and Karen Barad's account of diffraction to suggest that we might hold onto this tension as a site of queer friction."

He writes the four out in a list he gives twice, once mid-article and once in the conclusion. The conclusion's wording: "A/autisms is a contingent, problematic speciation of tendency"; "A/autisms is a 'real' (scare quotes) disability with implications for how life is lived and particularly for those from whom diagnosis is routinely withheld"; "A/autisms is a fabulous (neuro)queer counter-identity"; "A/autisms is a (neuro)queering disruption of identity."

And he gives the one-line reason for refusing to choose: "rather than 'pick one', holding all four in tension is a 'necessary queer labor of the incommensurate' (Muñoz, 2015, p. 209): necessary in that A/autisms are already all of these things (and more!)."

In the body he writes the four as a run-on with Deleuzian "and … and … and …", which is the only place in the article where that cadence appears, and he does so without citing Deleuze, who is absent from the article entirely.

## 2. Diffraction, and the claim he makes about it

He gives the physics first, in his own words: "Diffraction is what happens to light when it passes through a pair of holes in a filter. The two streams of light interact as they emerge from the filter: canceling each other out in some places and amplifying each other elsewhere. This is called an interference pattern. The type of interference pattern differs based on how the experiment is set up: setting up the experiment to materialize the particle-like properties of light simultaneously prevents the materialization of its wave-like properties."

Then the methodological claim: "Barad contends that the theoretical orientation to the research apparatus partially constitutes the 'thing' being researched."

**And then the sentence that matters for the metaphor thread in this folder.** "In describing diffraction as a methodological tool, Barad is not writing metaphorically: the theoretical orientation of the researcher to the research encounter quite literally materializes one interference pattern, while simultaneously preventing the materialization of any other."

The consequence he draws, which is the ethical engine of the article: "In this way, to orient is to erase, multiple-y so when researching in the early childhood classroom, with populations already subject to the multiplying invisibility experienced at the intersection of neurodivergence, racialization, and girlhood. In short: orientations matter."

The Barad quotation he builds friction on: "A diffraction pattern does not map where differences appear, but rather maps where the effects of difference appear" (2007, p. 300, his emphasis).

His addition to Puar's friction is his own, and he flags it as his own: "Adding to Puar's thinking around friction, I suggest that, in the same way that friction as a physical force is produced by the underlying electromagnetic attraction between the objects rubbed together, friction between concepts is generative precisely because of some underlying attraction." So: "rubbing counter- and dis-identity together with contingency and disability is productive because of their simultaneous mutual incommensurability and their mutual attraction."

## 3. The four orientations, in his order

**One, contingency.** Autisms (lower case) as "loose, contingent, explanatory frameworks that are draped over body(mind)s, and by which a set of divergent behaviors are speciated as a 'type' of person". The genealogy he cites names Kanner, Lovaas and Asperger, and he summarises two findings: McGuire that "the possibility of neurodivergence relies on early twentieth-century concerns of racial purity", and Gibson and Douglas on "the co-constitutive emergence of autism with the Feminine Boys Project in the work of Ole Ivar Lovaas."

**Two, the reality of disability, which he phrases as a caution to his own side.** "In short, in deconstructing autisms, I think we need to be careful 'what we wish for'." The argument: as "non- or intermittently apparent disabilities", autistic people "are often erased in discussions of disability, compounding the erasure disabled people are already subject to", and "autistic people are elided from neuronormative doxa, both in abled and disabled spaces."

The distributional point is given with numbers: "in both the UK and North America, Black students remain underrepresented in autism diagnoses, yet overrepresented for communication and behavioral differences. Girls also remain underrepresented by 4:1 in the UK." His framing: "we should be careful from whom diagnosis (no matter how 'invalid') is routinely withheld: or in Barad's terms, whose autism we try to dematerialize."

**Puar's reformulation, which is the sharpest single move in the section.** "Instead of asking 'are you disabled?' we should ask, moment-by-moment, 'how abled are you? and how disabled are you?'" And the comparison he draws from it: "much as how 'queer' at its least capacious and least disruptive often comes to be synonymous with white male homosexuality, the overrepresentation of white middle-class males in autism diagnoses fails to account for the debilitation evident at its intersection with other patterns of marginalization."

The sentence he takes from Eli Clare and keeps: "we shouldn't pretend that the material reality of autistic disability is any less real for being 'made-up'."

**Three and four, the two identity orientations, which he separates deliberately.** Four is dis-identification after Muñoz, which "resists the notion of stable identity, whether the dominant, normative identity, or resistant forms of counter-identity". His own illustration of why nobody is cleanly typical: "the fact that A/autisms are traced through behavioral checklists, particularly checklists with such woolly borders that most people could probably identify something autistic about themself at some point, allows us to think about the leakage by which nobody is absolutely (neuro)typical."

Three is Autistic identity as "a fabulous site of cultural production and counter-identarian political activism", carried by Yergeau's question, quoted at length: "clinical exegeses of autism pinpoint many of the core markers of what might be termed identity, in all of identity's fluidity and fluctuations, affect, intent, concept of self, concept of others, empathy, sensation, cognition, motor coordination, mental processings, interests and hobbies, relationality, communication, and so on. If these are the items my doctor terms autism, how am I not to say that autism is me?"

**And then he argues against his own fourth orientation being available to everyone**, which is the part of the article most worth keeping. Muñoz: "disidentification is not an appropriate strategy for all minoritarian subjects all of the time". Puar on the privilege, "financial security, protection from rather than subjection to state violence, that allows some (white) queer subjects to flaunt failure and refuse to 'identify' or counter-identify". His own conclusion: "Autistics are not widely valued in the ways that (some) queer and (some) D/deaf individuals have come to be. This is evident, for instance, in the decisions across Europe to ban gay conversion therapies while continuing (or even beginning) to invest in ABA. For this reason, in this moment, I think we need to reiterate Autistic (upper-case 'A') identity, even if it's in a more counter-identarian, less disruptive (arguably 'less queer') form."

## 4. The queer inhumanisms, and the question put to the ontological turn

He names the tension in the turn itself, via Luciano and Chen: "universalizing and locating impulses", impulses "that seek simultaneously to de-center (Man-as-)human experience without establishing a new unitary 'post'-human that continues to erase the specificity of marginalized experience."

The objection he reports and does not dismiss: "some critical theorists have questioned the perversity of asking racialized and disabled people who've always been excluded from the category of the human to simply 'let go' of it." And the question he quotes from Zakiyyah Iman Jackson, with her emphasis: "What and crucially *whose* conception of humanity are we moving beyond?"

**This is where Shildrick is cited**, and the role she is given matters: she is placed among those "who have looked to the long history of alternatives to European Man already proposed by scholars in critical scholarship (e.g. Jackson, 2013; Keeling, 2007; Shildrick, 2015; Weheliye, 2014)."

What he wants instead of resolution, after Manning: "Not to solve problems, or to resolve questions, but to illuminate regions of thought through which problems-without-solutions can be intuited."

How he reads the "in" of inhuman, after Truman: it "emphasizes both difference and intimacy", so that "it both desires identification with the human while seeking to unsettle it". And the reason he wants the concept kept unfinished, after Muñoz: "the most utopic formulation of queerness is the one that remains on the horizon", because "if we were ever to allow it to arrive and sediment and know what it was, we would just establish another mode of unitary humanism."

## 5. The method, the consent arrangement, and the device critique

**Research-creation, defined against the obvious misreading.** "I understand research-creation as the simultaneous intersection of art, research, and theory: note that research-creation as I understand it is not the post hoc artistic representation of qualitative research, for instance, singing an interview transcript, but rather the doing of the research and the theory as the making of art." And Springgay, quoted: "art instantiates theory . . . [some works of art] are not metaphors, nor representations of theoretical concepts; rather, some works of art event concepts."

**The project.** *Neuroqueer(ing) Noise*: weekly hour-long music composition episodes, May 2018 to July 2019, with one class of thirty 5- and 6-year-olds, their classroom adults, and him, in an inner-city primary school in northern England. Episodes began from "a proposition", sometimes his, sometimes the staff's, sometimes the children's. Recorded on a pair of Shure SM-58 microphones into Digital Performer. Two children per episode wore an Empatica E4 electrodermal activity device.

The class composition is given in full: 17% self-identify as Black African, 10% Black British, 7% Black Caribbean, 4% Black European, 17% Pakistani, 4% Indian, 10% East Asian, 20% white Eastern European, 13% white British; 70% of the cohort identify as speakers of one of forty-two different home languages; approximately 25% of the cohort moves on each academic year; 52% of young people in the ward live in poverty and the catchment falls within the 5% most economically disadvantaged wards in the country.

**The consent arrangement, which is the most transferable thing in the article.** Graduated consent: "whether or not to participate, whether or not to be audio recorded, and whether or not to wear an Empatica E4." Separate explanatory workshops for parents and for children, "in order to inform their consent". And the ongoing mechanism: "I also facilitated ongoing consent by running each episode twice: children in the research class could refuse to take part in the research by walking through the adjoining door into the grade's second class, who would later participate in a 'research free' but otherwise identical music episode. This also kept provision equitable across both classes in the grade." Devices removable at any point.

**Why he will not call the device a sensor.** "This is also why I avoid the neutral-sounding word 'sensor': the 'gizmo' isn't just reading something off the autistic body(mind); it's actively doing something to the body(mind) so as to intervene in it." The technical basis he states twice: "EDA is not a measure of a pre-existing current, but instead a measure of the body(mind)'s resistance to a current generated by the gizmo."

The account of why EDA is popular in autism research: "due to the doxa that understand A/autistics as already closer to the technical and the technological, or the robotic, and so to the electrical", with Yergeau's reading of the prefix, "the A/autistic narrated as an A/auto-maton", and the blunt version: "use of electrodermal gizmos to indicate an oncoming crisis assumes the A/autistic is like an 'If This Then That' algorithm."

Ruha Benjamin's "New Jim Code" supplies the structure, defined in her words as the "employment of new technologies that reflect and reproduce existing inequities but that are promoted and perceived as more objective or progressive then the discriminatory systems of a previous era", with her point that "tech designers encode judgements into technical systems but claim that the racist results of their designs are entirely exterior to the coding process." His application: "the electrodermal gizmos activate and covertly reinforce neuro-ableist logics that frame A/autistic people as automaton-like, lacking agency, and akin to the technological."

And he says where he stands on the device, without softening it: "I use the word gizmo deliberately and humorously, but not out of a desire to make light of the technology: make no mistake, I find the technology deeply sinister."

## 6. The three vignettes

**One: absurdity as a method.** The EDA device's own operating parameters are the argument. He lists them from the literature: temperature below 22 or above 24 degrees, and "coughing, deep respiratory movements … , sneezes and excessive talking", or movement of any kind, "disturb the position of the electrodes and make the signal illegible". His response was to demonstrate this to the children by breaking it: "I jumped about, sang, yelled, and fake-sneezed (these were pre-COVID days), and pointed out how erratic and distorted the linegraph had become."

The Walking Scoring Devices are deliberately useless: cardboard, a bulldog clip, string and a toilet roll. "Although implying a certain usefulness, the Walking Scoring Boards are cumbersome, impractical, and absurd. Juggling the toilet roll, the ream of used toilet paper, the board, and a pen quickly gets you in a tangle. The boards and toilet paper turn to mush in the rain." The reason the toilet roll is right: "unlike rectangular pieces of paper, toilet rolls don't have borders and so can be endlessly unraveled to allow a continual line or score to be drawn."

Sianne Ngai's gimmick is the warrant: "We call things gimmicks when it becomes radically uncertain if they are working too hard or too little, if they are historically backward or just as problematically advanced, if they are wonders or tricks." Applied: "the gizmos imply technological sophistication, and yet as research devices are ultimately absurd: working too hard and yet clearly not working hard enough."

What the children did with the lines. They scored Villa-Lobos's *The Little Train of the Caipira*, tore the toilet roll, then reassembled it in different orders: "Some children tried to match up a perfect line. Others aimed for polyphony, creating congruent parts, and sometimes even layering fragments of the score on top of one another, where traces from different parts of the composition could be read through one another: futures and pasts bleeding together." Later they did the same with the EDA line from a walk around the school: "they flipped it upside down and stuck peaks where there should have been troughs. In this way, they intervened in the legibility of the (autistic) body(mind)."

Sylvia Wynter's sociogenetic principle carries the theoretical weight here: "the biological body(mind) is porous to social structures", "semiotic processes are able to override the biological tract: in this way, the social is viscerally lived", and what he says is distinctive about it, "What is unique to Wynter's conception is the ways in which she centers raciality."

His formula for what the absurdity accomplishes: "our absurd failure to do method properly . . . subverts the failure that had already chosen the autistic body(mind). . . . Instead, it applies that failure to the gizmo." And the question he ends the section on: "what interference (patterns) can be generated by that absurdity?"

**Two: two improvisations treated differently.** Rei, "a neuroqueer, Chinese girl", improvised a six-note melody "perfectly aligned with the half-whole diminished scale starting on B" during a percussive activity. "Rei's learning support assistant attempted to silence her six-note improvisation, and its manifestation of spectral hearing, because this was a rhythmic (or at least percussive) activity: no pitch welcome. In this way, her improvisation, although gorgeous, failed to 'pass'." The comparison is the whole vignette: "This was further evident when another child, Kwodwo, began his own improvisation: his improvisation lasts 55 seconds but is highly rhythmic and non-tonal, combining beatboxing, giggles, and spoken rhythms. Kwodwo's improvisation was not interrupted."

Halberstam supplies the reading of failure: "failure often means being relieved of the pressure to measure up to patriarchal ideals", so "these micro-failures are also moments of release from neuronormative expectations, and so are also moments of counter-identarian activism."

He also declines a term he is citing. Fessenden's "spectral hearing" is "perceptual strengths in tasks involving pitch, and weaknesses involving time", which Fessenden calls "a cognitive imbalance". Shannon: "having always been very poor at rhythm and much more interested in harmony, I don't know that I understand it as such."

And he keeps both readings of Rei together rather than choosing: "Rei's (neuro)queer temporo-rhythmic failure in this encounter is a counter-identarian refusal of heterosexual-temporalities, while still subjecting her to the material oppression experienced at the intersection of racializing and neurodiverging assemblages."

**Three: synesthesia, and the accident that made the session.** He introduced Oliveros's *Deep Listening*; the children listed what they listen to, in their own words, which he records: "music", then Britney's "No! Other music that you listen to when you're doing writing!", then "TV, Batman, and Turkish music", Aaron's "Gangam Style's my favorite song", Ioan's "a balloon … when they break", and Abayan's stage whisper, "That's the same as voices Mr. Shannon".

The accident: one child asked for ear defenders, "which quickly lead to a stampede for the remaining ear defenders", and he had fifteen blindfolds in the room by chance. "Suddenly, we were doing deep listening with a bunch of children who couldn't hear anything, and another bunch wearing sleep masks." Then the thing he noticed about his own interruptions: "I suddenly realized that the children wearing ear defenders couldn't hear me and yet were managing perfectly well without my interruptions, and so I stopped talking completely for about three minutes. The children carried on listening."

The two utterances the next session was built on, and he names the child who asked to be named anonymously: Abayan's "dark" as a description of a sound, and Suryanshu, wearing ear defenders: "One thing that I heard was that the sun was on my eyes and it was so bright."

**The claim about synesthesia is the article's most compressed.** "Synesthesia is a neurotypical concept: it relies on what Erin Manning calls 'an account of sensation that can be parsed … cleanly between sense modalities and between the bodies that are said to be the locations of sense'." And the link back to the device: "to delineate synesthesia as a unique mode of sensory experience doesn't just require 'cleanly parsed' senses: rather, it also requires 'cleanly parsed' bodies capable of experiencing those senses. These 'cleanly parsed bodies' are also the same unit of inquiry that the electrodermal gizmo explores."

**Biolooping, used in two senses, and he distinguishes them.** In autism research the term "refers to the ways in which the institutional and familial responses to autism diagnoses might cause more autistic-like behaviors: in other words, with the social construct of diagnosis reinforcing the biologic etiology of what it is that's been diagnosed". His own use is different: "Here however, biolooping indicates the ways in which ostensibly separate EDA signals from neatly parsed children's body(mind)s fed back through and amplified one another." The tables in the carousel: a Bluetooth speaker with the bass raised, held by six pairs of hands at once; two Empatica devices on iPads with claves; ear defenders and sleep masks; and adjectives from the term's topic work on materials ("metal, hard, shiny, blue, and heavy") sorted to instrument sounds.

And the limit he puts on the whole thing, in one sentence: "This is not say that the children 'experienced' what it's like to be autistic or have sensory processing differences, but rather that our experiments complicated the pathology of those experiences."

## 7. The metaphor question: a fifth position, and his is a double negation

The word metaphor appears twice in 12,103 words, and both are denials.

"In describing diffraction as a methodological tool, **Barad is not writing metaphorically**."

And, quoting Springgay: "[some works of art] **are not metaphors**, nor representations of theoretical concepts; rather, some works of art event concepts."

He uses "figuration" once, and not of his own concept: "the overrepresented Euro-Western figuration of the human".

So the positions now in this folder:

- **Neimanis.** Her water figures are "always more than metaphorical", working by "contiguity or continuity (rather than analogy)"; "concepts are always also material".
- **McKnight and Whitburn.** A figure's entailments arrive whether intended or not; the test is affective; banning metaphor is "a task beyond us as authors"; crystallisation and diffraction were tried and did not displace the lens.
- **Betterton.** A figure of creativity applied to pregnancy erases the agency of the person it is applied to; feminist theory has a "tendency to privilege the body as metaphor" (quoting Davis); the problem is deferred to her book.
- **Jackson.** Prefer "figuration" to "metaphor", because figuration keeps contradiction and disjunction.
- **Shannon.** The word is used only to deny: diffraction is not metaphorical, and some artworks are not metaphors but event concepts.

Five positions. Recorded. Not adjudicated.

**And a second thread, now three-way, on Barad's diffraction specifically.** Shannon asserts the literal reading, that the orientation "quite literally materializes one interference pattern". Shildrick's note 3 records that Barad "is explicit that her use of 'entanglements' goes beyond its disciplinary origins in physics". Romero's chapter addresses borrowings of quantum language from the physics side, with "the so-called wave-particle duality actually does not exist" and the measurement problem described without an observer, which is the exact feature Shannon's account of the two experimental setups turns on. Three texts, three different relations to the same borrowing. None of the three addresses either of the others. Nothing here decides between them.

## 8. Where this sits beside the other reads in this folder

**Shildrick, and a confirmation.** Shannon's reference list gives: "Shildrick, M. (2015). '"Why should our bodies end at the skin?": Embodiment, boundaries, and somatechnics.' *Hypatia*, 30(1), 13-29. doi 10.1111/hypa.12114." That is the exact article read earlier today, with the correct year and pages, which independently confirms the correction made this morning. The November 2 inventory's 2014 is wrong; Shannon's entry and the article's own header agree on 2015.

But the use he makes of her is not her own argument. He cites her once, in a parenthetical list of scholars who proposed "alternatives to European Man", not for the somatechnics or prosthesis argument that the article is actually about. Her name does the work of an example; her claim is not engaged.

**A finding worth acting on: three articles from the same *Hypatia* issue.** Shannon cites, besides Shildrick, two more from *Hypatia* 30(1), Winter 2015: Merri Lisa Johnson, "Bad romance: A crip feminist critique of queer failure", pp. 251-267; and Margaret Price, "The bodymind problem and the possibilities of pain", pp. 268-284. The folder holds only the Shildrick article from that issue. Price's "bodymind" is the term Shannon writes throughout as "body(mind)", and Johnson's crip critique of queer failure is directly on the Halberstam failure argument Shannon uses in vignette 2. Neither is held.

**Puar and Shildrick on degree.** Puar's reformulation here, "instead of asking 'are you disabled?' we should ask, moment-by-moment, 'how abled are you? and how disabled are you?'", sits beside Shildrick's "none of us is entitled to claim a singular body" and her immediate concession that the disabled body is "only a difference in degree, and hardly even that, rather than a difference in kind". Both are arguments against a kind-distinction. They are not the same argument: Puar's is temporal and distributional, Shildrick's is ontological, and Shannon cites Puar for this and not Shildrick. Recorded as adjacent, not identical.

**Jackson and Mazzei, and a name collision to be careful about.** Same journal. Shannon's article is explicitly in the post-qualitative and feminist-materialist lineage, and yet **Deleuze appears nowhere in it**, where Jackson's rhizovocality and Mazzei's Voice without Organs are both built on Deleuze and Guattari. His theoretical spine is Barad, Puar, Muñoz, Wynter, Manning, Halberstam, Yergeau. Different lineage inside the same turn.

**The name collision.** The "Jackson" in Shannon's references is **Zakiyyah Iman Jackson** (2013, 2015, 2020), not Alecia Youngblood Jackson. Both are now in this folder's reading, in the same journal thread, and a bare "Jackson 2013" or "Jackson 2015" could go either way. Always give the initials.

**Fries, Fahey, and research-creation.** The closest tie in kind. Shannon's definition of research-creation is "not the post hoc artistic representation of qualitative research, for instance, singing an interview transcript, but rather the doing of the research and the theory as the making of art", with Springgay's "some works of art event concepts". Fries's standing formula is that a work is "manifested as an encounter rather than as an illustration or description". Fahey names a failed body of work as "illustrating an idea, rather than embodying an exploration of the question". Three refusals of illustration, in three different vocabularies, none of them citing the others.

Two further adjacencies worth holding without collapsing. Fahey's glitch arrived as plasterboard air bubbles confounding the laser and she recognised it from her radiography; Shannon's gizmos "glitched-out with alarming regularity, switching themselves on and off, and suddenly flashing indicator lights in ways that weren't described in the manual", and he builds method out of that. And Shannon's noticing that the children listening with ear defenders "were managing perfectly well without my interruptions" is adjacent to Kotzé's balcony passage, where her own method is defined by what she stopped doing in the scene. Adjacent. Not the same claim.

**Butler.** Absent. Zero occurrences. Notable because the article's subject is identity, counter-identity and dis-identification, and because Muñoz's disidentification, which carries orientations three and four, is itself built on Butler's performativity in *Disidentifications*. The lineage runs through a text Shannon does not cite. An absence to note, not a fault to fix.

## 9. His self-flags

Seven places where he marks his own limit, which is more than any other text read in this folder today.

His positionality, stated before the argument: "writing from my own positionality as a neurotypical, cis-, gay but 'straight-passing(ish)', white male: In homonormative times, writing in solidarity with neurological queerness requires great care."

Where he thinks a cited authority understates it: Goodley writes that any intervention on autism "has the potential to upset somebody", and Shannon answers, "Yet, and while agreeing with Goodley, I worry that 'upset' undersells the importance of orientation."

On his own four-way cut: "my rubbing together of four orientations toward A/autisms risks a further dematerialization of those myriad autisms I haven't brought along", and "I remain cautious of joining the ranks of neurotypical white men deciding once-and-for-all what autism is or could be."

On his own framing of the paper's shape, with his own exclamation mark: "It is my hope, then, that cutting around these four orientations makes this discussion manageable in such a short piece, but without closing down A/autisms' intrinsic capaciousness (which is the whole point of the paper!)."

On the terminology of a cited author, in endnote 3: "I use the singular 'autism' here to better reflect Yergeau's singular use of the term. As a neurotypical scholar drawing from the work of autistic scholars, it is important to accurately represent Yergeau's notion of what their autism is."

On the conclusion: "The tracings of A/autisms that I offer here are limited, not least by my neurotypical intrusion into this academic space. No doubt there are A/autisms that I have not yet been lucky enough to encounter, and so have failed to adequately trace."

And he turns the incompleteness into the position rather than apologising for it, citing a companion article: "As Michael Orsini (this issue) describes, all too often researchers find the complexity and intransigence of autism alluring; he contends that we should adopt an 'epistemology of ignorance', wherein the A/autistic body(mind)'s refusal to explain itself is itself generative."

## 10. Citation faults and things to check before citing

All of these are in the source, not in extraction.

- **Two in-text citations have no reference entries.** "Douglas et al., this issue" and "Michael Orsini (this issue)" appear in the body; neither is in the 78-item reference list, and the special issue is never named. Chasing either means identifying the issue first: it is *IJQSE* 38(5), 2025, or the 2021 online cohort.
- **One citation is to work not yet published.** "Truman, S. E., & Shannon, D. B. (under review). The queer temporal contours of Alpha Centauri", quoted in the body as "(n.p.)". Status as of 2021. Whether it ever appeared is unchecked.
- **Four citations use Kindle locations instead of page numbers**, so they cannot be checked against a print edition: Muñoz 1999 (loc. 3307), Muñoz 2019 (loc. 4028), Berlant and Edelman 2019 (loc. 1090), Ceraso 2018 (loc. 328, cited twice).
- **A factual error in the source.** He describes the kalimba as "an Indonesian 'thumb piano'". The kalimba is an African instrument, in the mbira family, associated with Zimbabwe and the Shona. Worth noting because the article's own argument is about racializing logics in classroom material.
- **A quotation carries "then" for "than".** Benjamin's definition of the New Jim Code is quoted as "more objective or progressive then the discriminatory systems of a previous era". Whether the slip is Benjamin's or the transcription's is unchecked; it is in the article as printed.
- **One child's words are given two ways.** First as "the sun was on my eyes and it was so bright", then as "hearing the so bright sun" and "Suryanshu's 'hearing the so bright sun'". The first is presented as the quotation.
