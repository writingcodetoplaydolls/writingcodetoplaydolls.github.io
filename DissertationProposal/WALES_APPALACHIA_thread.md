## 2. Ivinson 2014 is the one, and it is remarkable

**Ivinson, G. (2014). "Ghosts from the past: Exploring community cultures and school cultures in relation to poverty." *Improving Schools* 17(3), 250-260.** DOI 10.1177/1365480214556872. Verified against Crossref. University of Aberdeen. Paywalled.

It opens with four ghost stories. Two are posted by ex-miners on a Welsh coal-mining forum; two are told by Alwyn, aged 13, in a south Wales valley school. In one of the forum stories, two men cut through into an abandoned working, see a figure sitting by the road, and when one touches him, "the figure crumbled into dust and bone before his very eyes."

Alwyn has never been down a pit. The pits closed before he was born. He tells his grandfather's stories, and he tells them in his grandfather's cadence.

### What Ivinson does with that

Alwyn roams the ex-industrial landscape, which is being built over, and reads it:

> "I have been in the new school [building site] at the top of the colliery, they had a boring machine, down to 30 metres... I could feel a little rumble in the ground. Water came up two metres, there was a stream down there... they could not stop the water."

He digs in holes and finds things, and they are not small to him:

> "He came across pottery, pieces of jugs... you know like when copper goes black, after a while... 'wow'. I put my hand through, put my hand so close to it. What was that then? Rubbed it with my finger. Queen's crest, with a tiger on the top with its crown... can't be... like this... dreaming like."

He attaches the finds to a dead grandmother: "when I think of it now, it was three years after my nan dies... a kind of sign."

Ivinson's argument is that Alwyn is not failing to be a schoolboy. He is succeeding at being a miner, in a place with no mines. He has acquired, outside school and by hanging around ex-colliers, the attunement the work required: listening for rumbles, watching for cracks, reading the ground for what might come up through it. He has acquired it in a landscape where the work no longer exists. The school registers the result as an oddity, gives him literacy support, and writes him off.

> "Just as the miners attuned themselves to the underground environment where they needed to be wary of sounds that might indicate danger such as a sudden escape of methane gas, so Alwyn attuned himself to sounds, textures, ripples as well as cracks and crevices that opened up into the underground world of mining shafts."

And, on why the stories persist at all:

> "these stories were told and retold within families as a way to manage the trauma of loss, not just the loss of the jobs, but a loss of a way of life and sometimes the untimely death of an infant, a brother, an auntie or a nan."

### The caveat that matters most

**This is not the diffractive Ivinson.** Counts across the article: Bernstein 10, affect 11, Walkerdine 4, Wulf 4, mimetic 3, Deleuze 1, assemblage 1, **Barad 0, diffraction 0, intra-action 0, posthuman 0, new materialism 0.**

It runs on Basil Bernstein's code theory plus Valerie Walkerdine on post-industrial affect. That is a sociology-of-education register, not a new materialist one.

That is worth knowing rather than working around, because it reveals something useful: **Ivinson's own published trajectory on the same community moves Bernstein to Deleuze to Barad.**

| Year | Work | Frame |
|---|---|---|
| 2013 | Ivinson & Renold, "Valleys' girls" (*Gender and Education* 25(6)) | Re-theorising bodies and agency |

## 3. The theoretical spine Ivinson points at, which is not in your folder

Ivinson 2014 leans on two works that do the post-industrial affect theory properly. Neither is in the corpus. All citations verified against Crossref DOI records.

| Source | Status | What it gives |
|---|---|---|
| **Walkerdine, V. (2010). "Communal Beingness and Affect: An Exploration of Trauma in an Ex-industrial Community." *Body & Society* 16(1), 91-116.** DOI 10.1177/1357034X09354127 | Paywalled | The source of the "second skin" idea Ivinson uses: a community holding itself together affectively after the thing that made it is gone |
| **Walkerdine, V. & Jimenez, L. (2012). *Gender, Work and Community After De-Industrialisation: A Psychosocial Approach to Affect.* Palgrave.** DOI 10.1057/9780230359192 | Paywalled | The book-length version. Chapter 5 is "De-Industrialisation, Suffering, Crisis and Catastrophe" |
| **Walkerdine, V. (2016). "Affective History, Working-Class Communities and Self-Determination." *The Sociological Review* 64(4), 699-714.** DOI 10.1111/1467-954X.12435 | **OPEN**, post-print at `https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/94493/3/Affective%20historyFINALFINALrevisedsubmitted.pdf` | The free one. Start here |

### And a term that is doing exactly this work, under a name

**Geoff Bright, "social haunting."** Used of former coalfield communities in the UK, built on Avery Gordon rather than Derrida, and worked through arts-based participatory "ghost labs."

- **Bright, N. G. (2016). "'The lady is not returning!': educational precarity and a social haunting in the UK coalfields." *Ethnography and Education* 11(2), 142-157.** DOI 10.1080/17457823.2015.1101381. **OPEN**, post-print at `https://e-space.mmu.ac.uk/617130/9/`
- **Bright, N. G. (2018). "'A Chance to Talk Like This': Gender, Education, and Social Haunting in a UK Coalfield."** In *Education and Working-Class Youth*, Palgrave, pp. 105-129. DOI 10.1007/978-3-319-90671-3_5. Paywalled
- **Simmons, R. & Simpson, K. (eds.) (2022). *Education, Work and Social Change in Britain's Former Coalfield Communities.* Palgrave.** DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-10792-4. Includes a chapter on attuning to ghosts
- **Simpson, K. (2021). *Social Haunting, Education, and the Working Class.* Routledge.** DOI 10.4324/9781003099451

A named concept, a literature, and an arts-based method, all on the same object. It is not posthumanist, and that may be an advantage: it is a second discipline saying the thing, which is the transdisciplinary argument.

## 4. The Wales and Appalachia connection: what exists, and what it is

**Nothing in the 804-document corpus joins Wales and Appalachia.** Four files mention both, and in every case the co-occurrence is accidental. In *Staying with the Trouble* the "Wales" is New South Wales. In *Learning Bodies* the two words are in different chapters.

Outside the corpus the connection is real, documented, and older than this project.

| Source | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Hansell, T. (2018). *After Coal: Stories of Survival in Appalachia and Wales.* Morgantown: West Virginia University Press.** | A book and a documentary film. Built on four decades of actual exchange between coalfield communities in Appalachia and south Wales. Reviewed in *Appalachian Journal* 48(3-4), 294-297; *Journal of Southern History* 85(4), 956-957; *Register of the Kentucky Historical Society* 118(1), 211-212. **This is a comparative Appalachia-Wales study carried out as art practice**, which is three of your commitments at once |
| **Lewis, H. M., Johnson, L. & Askins, D. (eds.) (1978). *Colonialism in Modern America: The Appalachian Case.* Appalachian Consortium Press.** DOI 10.2307/j.ctt1xp3n1t | The internal-colony argument. Helen Matthews Lewis is also the Appalachian scholar behind the original Welsh exchange. Chapter 1 is Lewis & Knipe, "The Colonialism Model," pp. 9-31 |
| **Lewis, R. L. (2008). *Welsh Americans: A History of Assimilation in the Coalfields.* Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press.** | Welsh miners recruited into American coalfields. **The migration is documented**, so a Welsh-to-Appalachian coalfield link is a historical route, not an analogy |

### A distinction worth keeping straight

Two different claims are available, and they are not equally strong.

**The structural one.** Two coal regions, mountainous, previously subsistence-farming, industrialised for extraction by outside capital, abandoned within living memory, both described as internal colonies, with documented labour migration between them. Ivinson's Alwyn and an Appalachian child inherit from the same kind of collapse. This claim is supported by Hansell, by both Lewises, and it is the one Ivinson's and Walkerdine's arguments actually license, because their mechanism is **intergenerational transmission inside a community that stayed put**: Alwyn learned from ex-colliers he sat with, not from anything he was born with.

**The descent one.** Welsh ancestry, Appalachian birth. Factually yours and not in dispute. But it is a different mechanism, it is not what any of this literature argues, and in a dissertation that runs on Braidotti, grounding a subject position in biological lineage sits against the framework rather than inside it: the nomadic subject is explicitly not a descent claim. If the two are run together the reader will hear the weaker one.

The usable form is probably that the biography is what made the question findable, and the structural history is what makes it arguable. The nomadic subjectivities section can hold the first without the dissertation's argument resting on it.

## 5. The find that was not being looked for

In the reference list of *Learning Bodies*, in a chapter on photo methods:

**Ewald, W. (1985). *Portraits and Dreams: Photographs and Stories by Children of the Appalachians.* New York: Writers & Readers.** Revised and expanded edition, MACK, 2020.

Ewald began the project in 1975 in Letcher County, Kentucky, living, in her own words, on "one of the most remote and beautiful hollers in Letcher County." She gave each child a camera and interviewed them about growing up in the mountains. The 2020 edition returns to eight of the original children about 35 years later and prints what they say now.

So: **cameras handed to working-class children in a coal-country holler, by an artist, as a sustained practice, published twice across 35 years.**

That is the Appalachian counterpart to "Girls, Camera, (Intra)Action," in the same method, on the other side of the Atlantic, forty years earlier and without the theory. Your Chapter 1 opens on "Camera" and "The 110 Camera." Ivinson and Renold give the diffractive reading of camera-child assemblages; Ewald gives the Appalachian practice that the reading could be run on. Neither was in your folder.

What camera Ewald issued is not established here and should be checked before any claim is made about format.

## 6. Secondary, from the same batch

**Poysa, V. & Ristaniemi, H. (2025). "Assembling places, technologies and young people: becoming rural youth with vehicles and digital technologies in Finland." *Journal of Youth Studies* 28(3), 479-495.** DOI 10.1080/13676261.2023.2271875. **OPEN** at `http://urn.fi/URN:NBN:fi:jyu-202401111167`.

157 uses of "rural" in 8,257 words, the densest rurality treatment in the whole corpus. It theorises rurality itself as assembled rather than given, which is the move needed if Appalachia is going to be a node in an assemblage rather than a biographical fact. Finland, not Wales, and no class-trauma argument, so it does a different job from Ivinson: it supplies the concept of rurality, where Ivinson supplies the place.

**Renold, E. & Ivinson, G. (2022). "Posthuman co-production: becoming response-able with what matters." *Qualitative Research Journal* 22(1), 108-128.** DOI 10.1108/QRJ-01-2021-0005. **OPEN** via `https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/152910/`. Not in the folder. This is their own methodological statement, and the most recent one. Worth having before the camera chapter arrives.
