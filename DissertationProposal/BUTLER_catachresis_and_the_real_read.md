## 1. Catachresis, defined

Butler's working definition arrives through Irigaray, and it is compact:

> "the feminine appears for Irigaray only in catachresis, that is, **in those figures that function improperly, as an improper transfer of sense, the use of a proper name to describe that which does not properly belong to it**, and that return to haunt and coopt the very language from which the feminine is excluded."

And the practice that follows from it: "**the catachrestic usurpation of the 'proper' for fully improper purposes**", which is how she characterises Irigaray's method, "For she mimes philosophy, as well as psychoanalysis, and, in the mime, **takes on a language that effectively cannot belong to her**, only to call into question the exclusionary rules of that language."

**The point for this folder is the structural one.** A catachresis is not a decorative substitution for a literal term that was available. It is used where no proper term exists. So it cannot be measured against a literal original and found wanting, because there is no literal original. Butler puts this as the condition rather than the defect: of Plato's receptacle, which "is neither a universal or a particular" and so "cannot be named", she writes that Plato "**nevertheless proceeds to name what cannot be properly named, invoking a catachresis**".

Two further instances in the same register. **Spivak's** "subaltern feminine, understood as a catachresis, **whose exclusion from representation has become the condition of representation itself**". And matter itself, in Irigaray's hands, occurring "in two modalities: first, as a metaphysical concept that serves a phallogocentrism; second, **as an ungrounded figure, worrisomely speculative and catachrestic, that marks for her the possible linguistic site of a critical mime**".

## 2. The naming argument, which is a measurement argument

This is the part that belongs beside the 2.5 cluster, because it is about what repeated use does to a referent.

> "by virtue of the very reiterability of the name, the necessity that the name be reiterated in order to name, to fix its referent, **this risk of catachresis is continually reproduced. Hence, the very iterability of the name produces the catachrestic divergence from the chain that the referent is meant to forestall.** And this raises the further and consequential question of **whether the permanent risk of catachresis does not 'unfix' the referent.** It also raises . . . the consequential question of **whether the referent is itself always only tenuously fixed by this regulation of its use**, that is, by the outlawing of this catachrestic divergence from the chain of normative usage."

Her case is **Kripke's own counterexample**: "If I hear the name 'Napoleon' and decide it would be a nice name for my pet aardvark, I do not satisfy this condition." Butler's reply is that this improper usage "**inheres in the possibility of the proper usage**, indeed, remains that over and against which" the proper is defined. And on baptism: it "is an act which is 'initial' or 'primal' only to the extent that **it imitates the originating Adamic act of naming, and so produces that origin again through mimetic reiteration**."

So the apparatus that fixes a referent is the regulation of use, and the fixing is never complete, because the repetition that does the fixing is also what lets the name slip. **The same shape as the measurement instances already in 2.5**, with naming as the instrument.

## 3. "The Rock of the Real", which is the chapter on the figure and the real

Chapter 7 argues with Žižek's reading of Lacan, and the section that matters is where Butler shows that **the figure used to mark off the unfigurable is itself a figure**.

> "Žižek begins his critique of what he calls 'poststructuralism' through the invocation of a certain kind of matter, a '**rock**' or a '**kernel**' that not only resists symbolization and discourse, but is precisely what poststructuralism, in his account, itself resists and endeavors to 'dissolve.' **This solidity figures the Lacanian real, the outside to discourse construed as symbolization, and so is a figure that fortifies the theoretical defense of that which, for Žižek, must remain unfigurable, and so might be said to perform the impossibility that it seeks to secure. The rock thus figures the unfigurable, and so emerges not only as a catachresis, but as one that is supposed to secure the borders between** what he will call sometimes symbolization and sometimes '**discourse**,' on the one hand, **and the 'real,' on the other, where the latter is designated as that for which no symbolization is possible.**"

And the observation that the figure will not hold still:

> "the 'real' that is a 'rock' or a 'kernel' or sometimes a 'substance' is also, and sometimes within the same sentence, '**a loss**' a '**negativity**'; **as a figure it appears to slide from substance to dissolution**, thereby conflating the law that institutes the 'lack' and the 'lack' itself."

With the rhetorical question she puts to the text's own form, which is a method question as much as a critique: "**What is the relation of the textual propositions in *Sublime Object* to the law that it enunciates and 'defends'?** . . . Is the '**contingency**' of language here mastered in and by a textual practice that **speaks as the law**, whose rhetoricity is domesticated by the declarative mode?"

**What this gives the metaphor question.** Not "a figure is as real as the thing", and not "everything is a figure". Butler's move is narrower and harder to answer: **the position from which a figure could be called lesser than the real is itself held open by a figure**, and that figure can be shown to be unstable. It is the same objection Hausman puts to Turbayne in the 1981 *Leonardo* review found earlier today, in different vocabulary: how would one know that figures are not identical with reality, except from somewhere?
