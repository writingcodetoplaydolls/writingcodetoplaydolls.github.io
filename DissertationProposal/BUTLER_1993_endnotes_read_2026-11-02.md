# Butler, *Bodies That Matter*: the endnotes

2 Nov 2026. New file. The companion is `BUTLER_1993_BodiesThatMatter_read_in_full_2026-11-02.md`, which read the whole body text and recorded the endnotes as unread. **This file reads the endnotes in full (pp. 243-283): 1 to the preface, 19 to the introduction, 49 to chapter 1, 34 to chapter 2, 19 to chapter 3, 8 to chapter 4, 23 to chapter 5, 19 to chapter 6, 22 to chapter 7, 21 to chapter 8.** The index of names was looked at and is unusable (see the footer).

**Why they are worth the time.** Several of the notes are not citations. They are short essays carrying argument the chapters do not make, and three of them (introduction n. 19, chapter 1 n. 28, chapter 6 n. 14) run to a page or more. **The clearest statement of race as performative in the whole book is in a footnote, and so is the passage on white guilt.** Sections 3 and 4 below set those out.

## 2. Definitions and sources the chapters use without explaining

| | |
|---|---|
| **abjection** | Intro n. 2: "in Latin, *ab-jicere*, literally means **to cast off, away, or out** and, hence, presupposes and produces a domain of agency from which it is differentiated", resonating with *Verwerfung*; the entry is sourced to the "**Forclusion**" entry in Laplanche and Pontalis's *Vocabulaire de la psychanalyse*. And the abject zone is given a sentence of direct speech: "**constituting zones of uninhabitability which a subject fantasizes as threatening its own integrity with the prospect of a psychotic dissolution ('I would rather die than do or be that!')**" |
| **the chiasm** | Ch2 n. 12 gives the source of the chiasmic language the chapter uses for language and matter: **Merleau-Ponty, "The Intertwining: The Chiasm", in *The Visible and the Invisible***, on "the flesh of the world" and "the intertwining of touch, surface, and vision" |
| ***hyle* and *hypodoche*** | Ch1 n. 22 is a compact philology note and the scholarly floor of the chapter: *hyle* "is for the most part Aristotelian", known only by analogy (*Metaphysics* 1036a), defined as potency (*dynamis*), "isolated as one of the four causes" and "described as the principle of individuation"; Aristotle faults Plato for not distinguishing *hyle* from *steresis* (privation) yet identifies the receptacle with *hyle* (*Physics* 4, 209b); like *hyle* the *hypodoche* "is indestructible, can only be known by means of 'bastard reasoning'", and "**there is no definition of matter, only of eidos**" (*Metaphysics* 1055b). **And the sequence matters: "It is only once Aristotle supplies an explicit philosophical discourse on matter that Plotinus writes a reconstruction of the Platonic doctrine of matter. This then becomes the occasion for Irigaray's critical citation of Plato/Plotinus."** |
| ***phusis*** | Ch1 n. 27, one line with a reference: "**For a discussion on how physis or phusis meant genitals**, see John J. Winkler, 'Phusis and Natura Meaning "Genitals"', in *The Constraints of Desire*" |
| ***dechomenon*** | Ch1 n. 25: in the *Theatetus* it "is described as a '**bundle of wax**,' so Aristotle's choice of the 'wax' image in *de Anima* to describe matter might be read as **an explicit reworking of the Platonic dechomenon**" |
| **"assume" a sex** | Ch3 n. 5, which is the book's best small note: "**Note the theological roots of 'assume'** in the notion of 'assumption' (*assomption*) in which **the Virgin is said to be 'assumed' into heaven** . . . Significantly, though, this assumption of sex is figured through **the upward travel of the Virgin, a figure of chaste ascension, thus installing a prohibition on female sexuality at the moment of ascending to 'sex'**. Hence, taking on a sex is at once the regulation of a sexuality and, more specifically, **the splitting of feminine sexuality into the idealized and the defiled**" |
| **fantasy, phantasmatic** | Ch3 n. 2 distinguishes her two words: "**phantasmatic**" for Laplanche and Pontalis's sense, in which "the identificatory locations of the subject are labile"; "**fantasy**" and "fantasize" kept "for those active imaginings which presuppose a relative locatedness of the subject". Ch3 n. 7 then quotes their "Fantasy and the Origins of Sexuality" at length, including the line that explains the whole apparatus: "**In fantasy the subject does not pursue the object or its sign: he appears caught up himself in the sequence of images** . . . the subject, although always present in the fantasy, may be so in **a desubjectivized form, that is to say, in the very syntax of the sequence in question**"; and her own gloss: "the essay offers itself as an account of the 'origin' of fantasy, but it suffers under the same prohibition. Thus, **the effort to describe theoretically the origins of fantasy is always also a fantasy of origin**" |
| **baptism** | Ch7 n. 16: Hobbes describes baptism as "**the sacrament of allegiance of them that are to be received into the kingdom of God**" (via the OED), so "the giving of the first name is **the initiation into the order of divine paternity**". "My thanks to **Lisa Lowe** for a timely intervention on this matter" |

## 3. Notes that carry argument the chapters do not make

**Race as performative.** The clearest statement in the book is **chapter 6, note 4**, in full:

> "This suggests one sense in which '**race**' might be construed as **performative**. Bellew produces his whiteness through a ritualized production of its sexual barriers. **This anxious repetition accumulates the force of the material effect of a circumscribed whiteness, but its boundary concedes its tenuous status precisely because it requires the 'blackness' that it excludes.** In this sense, **a dominant 'race' is constructed (in the sense of materialized) through reiteration and exclusion.**"

**White guilt.** **Chapter 6, note 14** begins as a gloss on Freud (conscience as sublimated homosexual libido, so that "the pangs of conscience are nothing other than the displaced satisfactions of homosexual desire"), and then turns, unannounced, into the most forceful paragraph in the apparatus:

> "This consideration of guilt as a way of locking up or safeguarding desire may well have implications for the theme of white guilt. For the question there is **whether white guilt is itself the satisfaction of racist passion, whether the reliving of racism that white guilt constantly performs is not itself the very satisfaction of racism that white guilt ostensibly abhors.** For white guilt, when it is not lost to self-pity, **produces a paralytic moralizing that requires racism to sustain its own sanctimonious posturing**; precisely because white moralizing is itself nourished by racist passions, **it can never be the basis on which to build and affirm a community across difference**; rooted in the desire to be exempted from white racism, to produce oneself as the exemption, this strategy virtually requires that the white community remain mired in racism; **hatred is merely transferred outward, and thereby preserved, but it is not overcome.**"

**Materiality in one sentence.** **Chapter 1, note 12**, a page-long note on Foucault, ends with: "**'Materiality' appears only when its status as contingently constituted through discourse is erased, concealed, covered over. Materiality is the dissimulated effect of power.**" It also supplies the sentence the chapter needs and does not say: "**There is no 'power,' taken as a substantive, that has dissimulation as one of its attributes or modes.**"

**Temporality, which the book never theorises in the body.** **Introduction notes 7 and 8** are the most philosophically dense pages in the apparatus. Note 7: "an act is itself a repetition, a sedimentation, and congealment of the past which is precisely foreclosed in its act-like status. **In this sense an 'act' is always a provisional failure of memory.**" Note 8 then turns on her own vocabulary: "**Efforts to describe or name this temporal span tend to engage spatial mapping**, as philosophers from Bergson through Heidegger have argued"; "**the notion of the 'moment' may well be nothing other than a retrospective fantasy of mathematical mastery imposed upon the interrupted durations of the past**"; "this temporal account of construction presupposes a spatialization of temporality itself, what one might, following Heidegger, understand as **the reduction of temporality to time**". **And she faults Foucault for exactly this, having built on him for two chapters:** "Foucault's account of convergence **does not fully theorize what is at work in the 'movement' by which power and discourse are said to converge** . . . the 'mapping' of power does not fully theorize temporality"; and his words "augmentation," "proliferation," "convergence" "all of which presume **a temporal domain not explicitly theorized**". Her suggested remedy, in one line: "**Here one might consult the work of Pierre Bourdieu to understand the temporality of social construction.**"

**A defence of Lacan against Foucault**, which is the longest note in the book (**introduction n. 19**) and sits oddly against the book's general direction. Foucault's charge, quoted, is that the Lacanian law of desire is "monotonous . . . seemingly doomed to repeat itself". Butler's answer: "**Foucault presumes that this repetition is a repetition of what is self-identical** . . . **But repetition is not subjectivating in Lacan in the way that Foucault implies. In fact, repetition is not only the mark that subjectivation has in some sense failed to occur, but that it is itself a further instance of that failing.** That which repeats in the subject is **that which is radically excluded from the formation of the subject**."

**Nietzsche's God, turned on Lacan's law.** **Introduction n. 13**: Nietzsche argues that the ideal of God was produced "'in the same measure'" as human wretchedness, so that "the production of God was, indeed, the idealization which instituted and reenforced that wretchedness". Her application: the law's production of failure is "usually understood as a promising sign that the law is not fully efficacious", but "**to what extent does this conception of the law produce the very failure that it seeks to order, and maintain an ontological distance between the law and its failed approximations such that the deviant approximations have no power to alter the workings of the law itself?**"

**Race as construction that is still lived.** **Introduction n. 15** ends with Patricia Williams, and the handling is careful: *The Alchemy of Race and Rights* closes on "**A complexity of messages implied in our being**", and "While being black has been the most powerful social attribution in my life, **it is only one of a number of governing narratives or presiding fictions by which I am constantly reconfiguring myself in the world**". Butler: "the attribution, however fictive, is not only 'presiding' . . . but it is also, paradoxically and with promise, **a resource, the means by which her transformation becomes possible** . . . **calling race a construction or an attribution in no way deprives the term of its force in life.**"

**Marx on matter as transformation.** **Chapter 1, note 5** quotes the first thesis on Feuerbach and draws out a definition of materiality the chapter uses implicitly: "**the object is not only transformed, but in some significant sense, the object is transformative activity itself** and, further, its materiality is established through this temporal movement from a prior to a latter state . . . **the object materializes to the extent that it is a site of temporal transformation. The materiality of objects, then, is in no sense static, spatial, or given, but is constituted in and as transformative activity.**"

**Catachresis as psychotic speech.** **Chapter 7, note 17**, which is the most consequential small note in the book for anyone using her catachresis argument:

> "**Catachresis might be thought in terms of what Lacan refers to as 'neologism' in the language of psychosis.** Insofar as the catachresis of naming the aardvark Napoleon constitutes within discourse a resistance to symbolic paternity, it might be understood as **a politically enabling deployment of psychotic speech**. The 'neologism' in Lacan is the index of psychosis because **a word is coined to cover over a signifier that is excluded**; both catachresis and neologism might be construed as a linguistic modality of suturing."

**De Man on the performative, which undercuts the whole apparatus and she quotes it anyway.** **Chapter 8, note 4**: "the distinction between constative and performative utterances is confounded by the fictional status of both: '**. . . the possibility for language to perform is just as fictional as the possibility for language to assert**'. Further, he writes, '**considered as persuasion, rhetoric is performative, but considered as a system of tropes, it deconstructs its own performance**.'" She also concedes in the same note that "it is, of course, **never quite right to say that language or discourse 'performs,'** since it is unclear that language is primarily constituted as a set of 'acts'".

**Derrida's "Chora", and her question back to him.** **Chapter 1, note 28** runs over a page. Derrida calls the inscriptional space "a third gender or genre" and a "neutral space", "neutral because participating in neither pole of sexual difference", and describes it as "**in a third genre/gender and in the neuter space of a place without place, a place where everything marks it, but which in itself is not marked**". Butler's reply is two questions, and they are the sharpest thing in the apparatus:

> "**Have we discovered here the unmarked condition of all inscription, that which can have no mark of its own, no proper mark, precisely because it is that which, excluded from the proper, makes the proper possible? Or is this unmarked inscriptional space one whose mark has been erased, and is under compulsion to remain under permanent erasure?**"

## 4. Notes in which she corrects herself, or others, in her own voice

**Her own earlier essay corrected.** **Chapter 8, note 11**: "Theatricality is not for that reason fully intentional, but **I might have made that reading possible through my reference to gender as 'intentional and non-referential'** in 'Performative Acts and Gender Constitution'", and then the clarification: "**I use the term 'intentional' in a specifically phenomenological sense. 'Intentionality' within phenomenology does not mean voluntary or deliberate**, but is, rather, a way of characterizing consciousness (or language) as having an object."

**The sharpest sentence in the notes** is in **chapter 2, note 32**, after a long and hostile exposition of Maria Torok on penis envy, which Butler reads as installing "the Bad Mother whose primary task is to prohibit masturbatory pleasures" and as routing lesbian sexuality into "envy" and so into infinite dissatisfaction:

> "**That this essay is found useful by some feminists continues to surprise and alarm me.**"

**A reviewer's prurience named.** **Chapter 3, note 3** objects to James Miller's *The Passion of Michel Foucault* for conflating three things: "a popular notion of the 'death wish'", "the psychoanalytic notion of a 'death drive'", and "the notion introduced by Georges Bataille of 'the death of the subject'", and adds: "it is precisely **the metaphorical nexus of the two that focuses his analysis** and which has occasioned the appearance of 'level-headed' reviews in which **a certain heterosexual prurience becomes free to express itself under the rubric of sober criticism**."

**An asymmetry insisted on.** **Chapter 4, note 5**: "I consider it a cynical and homophobic use of that insight to claim that homosexuality is nothing other than repudiated heterosexuality . . . **This is also why the analysis of homosexual melancholy cannot be regarded as symmetrical to the analysis of heterosexual melancholy. The latter is culturally enforced in a way that the former clearly is not**, except within separatist communities which cannot wield the same power of prohibition."

**Her own reading flagged as possibly too much.** **Chapter 1, note 47**, in full: "**What follows may be an overreading, as some of my classicist readers have suggested.**" (It precedes the "to have or take a wife" reading of *eilephen*.) And in the same chapter, **note 46** warns against her own direction: "It is important to raise a cautionary note against too quickly reducing sexual positions of active penetration and passive receptivity with masculine and feminine positions within the ancient Greek context", citing Halperin against the conflation.

**Haraway, in a hot tub.** **Chapter 1, note 44** is the book's most quoted footnote and it is worth having exactly:

> "**Donna Haraway, responding to an earlier draft of this paper in a hot tub in Santa Cruz**, suggested that it is crucial to read Irigaray as reinforcing Plato as the origin of Western representation. Referring to the work of Martin Bernal, Haraway argues that **the 'West' and its 'origins' are constructed through a suppression of cultural heterogeneity, in particular, the suppression of African cultural exchange and influence. Haraway may be right**, but Irigaray's point is to expose the violent production of the European 'origins' in Greece and so is not incompatible with the view Haraway outlines . . . **What becomes stored in that receptacle?**"

**A sympathetic disagreement, fully set out.** **Chapter 3, note 10** quotes Kaja Silverman at length on prying the incest prohibition loose from the Name of the Father ("**the Law of Kinship Structure is not necessarily phallic**"), says "I am in clear sympathy with Silverman's project", and then declines her solution: "I am not sure, however, that **saying 'no' to the phallus** and, hence, to what symbolizes power . . . **is not itself a reformulation of power, power as resistance.**"

**And a note on how good work actually gets done**, which is a claim about form rather than argument. **Chapter 3, note 19**: "Significantly, **it is less often individual authors or works that succeed in this kind of complex work, but rather volumes which promote the consideration of different perspectives in a dynamic relationship to one another**", with Toni Morrison's *Race-ing Justice, En-gendering Power* given as "an excellent example of this kind of collective authorial event."

## 5. The historical and archival finds, mostly in chapter 5's notes

The Cather notes contain more primary material than the chapter does.

**A letter from Sarah Orne Jewett to Cather, 1908** (**ch5 n. 11**), objecting to Cather writing as a man, quoted nearly whole:

> "**The lover is as well done as he could be when a woman writes in the man's character; it must always, I believe, be something of a masquerade.** And you could almost have done it yourself: a woman could love her in that same protecting way, a woman could even care enough to wish to take her away from such a life, by some means or other. But oh, **how close, how tender, how true the feeling is! The sea air blows through the very letters on the page.**"

Butler adds the structural parallel: "the relation between the anonymous narrator of Cather's *My Ántonia* and Jim Burden parallels the narrator who receives the story and the teller of the story in Jewett's *The Country of the Pointed Firs*", and that both Jewett's novel and "Tommy the Unsentimental", **published the same year**, "interrogate the narrative and erotic dynamics of gift-giving and sacrifice."

**Havelock Ellis on blindness** (**ch5 n. 18**), which gives the chapter's "blindness that may not be cured" a period source: "**Havelock Ellis evidently linked blindness to sexual inversion in the 1890s and Cather may have known of his theory.** He also claimed that blind people were prone to sexual 'shyness' and 'modesty,' suggesting a link between inhibited desire and failing eyesight."

**The bicycle** (**ch5 n. 19**), which is why Tommy's pedalling is readable at all: "**Public debates on the appropriateness of women riding bicycles were highly publicized throughout the 1890s in the press**, raising the question of whether too much bicycling was harmful to women's health and **whether it might not excite women's sexuality in untoward ways**", sourced to Patricia Marks, *Bicycles, Bangs, and Bloomers: The New Woman in the Popular Press*, and to Virgil Albertini, "Willa Cather and the Bicycle" (1987).

**The "W" as Shakespeare** (**ch5 n. 10**): "It appears that Cather is here miming Shakespeare. Not only did she call herself '**Will**' and '**William**' when she was a young woman, but in these texts **she invokes the abbreviated 'W' as Shakespeare himself was wont to do**", with Joel Fineman, "Shakespeare's Will: The Temporality of Rape" (1987), alongside.

**Charlotte Brontë's coinage** (**ch5 n. 15**): Brontë "evidently used '**Shirley**' as a woman's name for the first time in her novel *Shirley* (1849). **Cather appears to be continuing and reversing that 'coining'** in this story, first, by establishing 'Tommy' as a girl's name and, second, **by establishing Shirley as a patronym**."

**And a judgment on Cather that the chapter does not make** (**ch5 n. 21**): "**Cather's misogyny effectively renders 'Tommy the Unsentimental' implausible as a narrative of love and loss. That Jessica is degraded from the start makes the final 'sacrifice' appear superfluous.**" She sets Toni Morrison's reading of *Sapphira and the Slave Girl* in *Playing in the Dark* beside it: Morrison "argues that the credibility of Cather's narrative is undermined by a recurring and aggrandizing racism . . . **because Cather, like Sapphira, has produced the slave girl in the service of her own gratification**", and Butler draws the link: "Such a displacement resonates with the displacements of Cather's cross-gendered narrations as well, **raising the question of the extent to which fictional displacement can be read as a strategy of repudiation**."

**From the Larsen notes:** **Jewelle Gomez** "suggests that **black lesbian sexuality very often thrived behind the church pew**" (**ch6 n. 8**); and **Du Bois reviewed Larsen and Claude McKay together in *The Crisis***, praising *Quicksand* as "a fine, thoughtful and courageous piece of work" while finding McKay's *Home to Harlem* so "nauseating" in its "drunkenness, fighting, and sexual promiscuity" that it made him feel "like taking a bath" (**ch6 n. 10**, quoted from McDowell), which is the evidence for the chapter's claim about the cost of uplift.

**From chapter 1:** **note 31** reports Irigaray's rereading of the *fort-da* scene in *La Croyance même*, where the substitute for the maternal is not the spool but "**the curtains, the folds of the bed linen that receive, hide, and return the spool**", so that "like the *chora*, 'she', the dissimulated maternal support for the scene, is the absent but necessary condition for the play of presence and absence."

**And a philological aside with a consequence**, **chapter 2 note 20**, buried in a long technical note on translating "The Mirror Stage": Lacan calls primary identification "**la souche**" of the secondary ones, which Butler glosses as "the trunk of a tree which, it appears, has fallen or has been cut down but which serves as fertile ground", and then connects to chapter 1: "**This wood fallen or chopped, ready for use, resonates with the meanings of matter as 'hyle'** considered in chapter one. In this sense, for Lacan, **primary identifications are indissociable from matter.**"

**Also from chapter 2:** **note 27** reads Lacan's own French for its prosthetics, "**une forme que nous appellerons orthopédique de sa totalité**" and "l'armure enfin assumée", and draws the conclusion: "insofar as a certain phallic potency is the effect of the transfigured body in the mirror, **this potency is purchased through artificial methods of phallic enhancement, a thesis with obvious consequences for the lesbian phallus**." And **note 30** is a page of careful work showing that the phallus does not symbolise penis and clitoris in the same way: "the clitoris is symbolized as penis envy (not having), whereas the penis is symbolized as the castration complex (having with the fear of losing) . . . **The clitoris can never be said, within this view, to be an example of 'having' the phallus.**"

## 6. Two small notes with current reach

**Chapter 8, note 1**, in full, attached to the sentence about why "queer" could be reclaimed and a racial slur could not: "**This is a question that pertains most urgently to recent questions of 'hate speech.'**" (It is the seed of *Excitable Speech*, 1997, which is not in this folder.)

**Chapter 3, note 1**, a one-line methodological rule: "Here one might follow a Wittgensteinian way of thinking and consider that **it is very possible to assert that sexuality is constrained, and to understand the sense of that claim without taking the added and unnecessary step of then offering a metaphysics of constraint to secure the meaningfulness of the claim.**"
