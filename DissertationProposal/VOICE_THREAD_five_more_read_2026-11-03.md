# PART ONE: THE VOICE THREAD, TWO MORE STATIONS

## 1. Mazzei and Jackson 2017: the garage door and the footsteps

**What is added to the 2013 position.** The 2013 articles decoupled voice from the subject. This one gives voice to the assemblage as a whole, via Jane Bennett: "we move toward positing voice in qualitative educational research as a thing that is entangled with other things in an assemblage that acts with an agential force." And the refusal that organises the article: "we do not 'calibrate' voice to the human, nor do we attend to voice as 'either pure cause or pure effect' via human intentionality."

**The research artifact**, printed with the stage direction they wrote into it, and it is the heart of the article. Amelia, a Baptist church member, contrasts the new preacher with the old:

> "We went from Tommy to Steven. . . . Tommy was very outgoing and very involved. . . . Steven is the complete opposite. His children are home-schooled, and he doesn't go to school events where people from the church would be. He's not visible at all. Outside the church, he's not visible. Our kids need that . . ."
>
> [As Amelia speaks, the faint sound of the automatic garage door opening is followed by the sounds of a turning door knob, a squeaky opening of a door, a shutting sound, and footsteps. Will, Amelia's husband and deacon at the church, enters the kitchen, interrupting the interview.]
>
> Amelia continues: "Our new preacher Steven is a very godly man. He's very close with God. He is a fine addition to our community. [Amelia pauses and looks at the interview guide] Do you want me to talk about what we do after church now?"

**Their reading, and the care in it is the point.** "we assert that an individual subject, in this case, Amelia, does not merely 'change her mind' about what she is saying but rather such 'dynamism is agency'." And the territorial account: "the territory of critique is formed, until the material force of footsteps reclaim the territory of patriarchy. . . . The force of Will's footsteps organize to make a new territory, the territory of small-town conservative patriarchy where she is now no longer critic, but docile."

**The sentence that keeps the claim honest, and which should be quoted whenever this article is summarised:** "We do not claim that the garage door 'has' a voice, nor do a husband's footsteps 'have' a voice—**anymore than we claim that Amelia 'has' a voice.**" And: "Certainly, that is one way to think about it. But from a posthuman perspective that necessitates a stance of distributive agency, those things are all part of an agentic assemblage that has an ontological force."

**And the counterfactual they explicitly refuse**, which is the hardest part of the argument to hold: "the elements in the assemblage (or entanglement) are not single sources of knowledge or agency—**the footsteps did not cause Amelia to act anymore than Amelia rushed to get her voice/critique 'heard' before her husband arrived home**."

**Three implications they list for methodology**, each one a demotion. First, attention to "the agential cuts that are made, in the assemblage", so that "The artifact or voice is no longer a story of Amelia, or of her experience—but a mutual constitution produced by material and discursive forces." Second, analysis is no longer representational. Third, and this is the practical one: "thinking a posthuman voice in educational inquiry also requires a **rethinking of interviewing**. . . . Such a scenario certainly requires a de-centering and de-privileging of the method of interviewing in posthuman educational research." They quote Mazzei 2013 for it: "research as the machine that is a hub of connections and productions, with interviewing being just one of those connections."

**The analytic questions they offer in place of interpretation**, printed as a pair: "How does Amelia intra-act with the materiality of her world in ways that produce different becomings?" and "What are the points on the spectrum (of motherhood; small-town living; being a 'good' Christian, etc.) that co-exist and intra-act?"

**They close on Braidotti asking for new words**: "The collapse of the nature-culture divide requires that we need to devise a new vocabulary, with new **figurations** to refer to the elements of our posthuman embodied and embedded subjectivity."

**And the Pickering question from the previous file is resolved.** This reference list carries **both** Pickering 1993 (the *American Journal of Sociology* article) **and** Pickering 1999 (the reprint in Biagioli's *Science Studies Reader*), listed separately with identical titles. So the two 2013 articles were citing the same text from two locations, and Mazzei and Jackson themselves list both. Either is correct; cite the 1993 original.

**Kirby is load-bearing here**, quoted three times from *Quantum Anthropologies* (2011), including "entanglement suggests that the very ontology of the entities emerge through relationality: the entities do not preexist their involvement", and the closing "in the sheer wonder of the spacetime entanglement at work". *Quantum Anthropologies* is already read in this folder.

## 2. Jackson 2010: "Deleuze and the girl", and the backflip

**This is the most formally experimental article in the folder**, and the experiments are declared rather than smuggled.

**It is written in the second person, to Jesse**, and she says why: "This usage allows Jesse to be closer to the reader and move away from the molar objectivity/distance of third-person convention." And the first person is deliberately withheld: "I refrain from any first person narrative 'I' in the representation. I do this not to remain hidden but to disrupt the traditional ethnographic, even confessional 'I' that haunts interpretive and critical qualitative research traditions. I refuse the narrative 'I' – or the molar 'I' – that is expected to give a full representation for the listener to easily consume and comprehend; **the narrative or molar 'I' tricks readers into thinking that they have the full picture.**"

**She also breaks the citation style on purpose and explains it**: "I also avoid the molar form of citational power that is demanded of APA style; I integrate Massumi's language as a source without the disruption of copious quotation marks and page numbers embedded in the text. I create/experiment with a molecular strategy of citing differently by using endnotes with page numbers only." Eleven of her sixteen endnotes are nothing but "Massumi 1992" and a page number.

**She puts four questions to her own reader**, which are a fair test of whether the experiment works: "Are readers caught in the molar machine of representation and do they want/need/desire to 'hear' Jesse's voice, her words? Might readers live molecularly with the text and not depend on the writer (me) to tell them what to think? . . . might Jesse's becoming grasp readers so that they themselves are becoming-Jesse?"

**The fieldwork, stated plainly**, and it is worth having because it is unusually specific: a year at Garner High School in the rural southern USA, "arriving at Garner High School at 7:30 in the morning, parking my 1986 BMW in the senior parking lot . . . and searching the halls for the senior girl whom I was shadowing that particular day", following the same schedule as the girl, sitting "in a student desk either beside or behind the girl I was following", with days ending "sometimes at 10:00 pm."

**How Jesse surfaced, and this matches the 2024 account exactly**: "Rather than begin with data to analyze using Deleuze, I remained open to allow new connections to irrupt. **You could say that I read in the threshold.** As I read, in the threshold between Deleuze and my experiences as an ethnographer, the becoming-data that surfaced was Jesse."

**Molar and molecular, defined for use**, which is the clearest short statement of the pair in this folder: "Molar is something that is well-defined, massive, and governing – such as large structures or identity categories. For example, cheerleading can be considered a molar form in the southern USA; cheerleading exists within discursive and material fields saturated with patriarchy, masculinity, and historically laden ideals of dainty, feminine southern girlhood." And the molecular: "micro-entities, processes, creations – tiny things (singularities) that destabilize the perception of a whole."

**The wasp and the orchid, as she uses it**: "The becoming is in the relationship between the wasp and the orchid. The becoming is the something else, the newness that is created." Then the move she has to make to get from there to a cheerleader: "How do we go from the natural world of the wasp and orchid to the social world of the girl and cheerleading?"

**The narrative itself.** Jesse arrives in black baggy sweatpants, black tank top and flip-flops on a game day when she ought to be in uniform, with "NO DOUBT" printed across her windscreen. She lifts weights with the football players in an elective class where the other two girls ride stationary bikes. Then the pep rally, where the molar cheer ends with the other girls lifting their skirts to show the mascot paws, three of them rotating their hips "as would a stripper on a pole". And then, set alone on its own line in the original:

> You perform a standing backflip.

Followed by: "The Molar Gaze goes off in multiple directions, not knowing where to look or how to synthesize the irresolvable contradiction. Your becoming is a visible incoherence, an undoing, a fracture. You transform; the cheer transforms; the gaze transforms. For a moment everyone and everything become something else. **Fleeting success.**"

**And she will not call it a victory.** "It will not be the last time. You will do it again, again, again. To achieve the goal that has no end means ceasing to be what you are in order to become what you cannot be: supermolecular forever. **The goal is a limit approached, never reached; what is important is the process.**"

**So the backflip is now read in its original.** It appears here in 2010, is returned to in "An ontology of a backflip" (2016), and underlies "Thinking without method" (2017). It is the same material Kotzé's method chapter refers to, and the 2024 article says it was never captured as data or written into Jackson's dissertation.

**Her epigraph for the whole method, from Massumi's translator's foreword**: "The question is not: is it true? But: does it work?" The same line Mazzei and McCoy's special issue closes on and the same line Mazzei's 2010 article reaches.

## 3. Jackson 2003: the hometown interview, and the Trickster in her head

Five pages, no notes, no references. A short experimental companion to "Rhizovocality" in the same issue, and the piece where the Trickster figure that "Rhizovocality" theorises actually appears.

**What it is.** Jackson interviewing Jeri, a childhood friend, for her dissertation, in the hometown they both come from, pseudonymised as Manders. They had not seen each other in fifteen years. The text interleaves Jeri's words, Jackson's present-tense narration, and italicised childhood memories, with no analysis section.

**The arrival, which sets the problem**: "I turn into her driveway and park but don't get out of my car. I fret that Jeri, too, is thinking of our waxing and waning childhood friendships, rife with uneven and fickle moments of jealousy, competition, sadness, and abiding love for each other. . . . How heavy their truth-telling will be, weighed down with the history of us." And her own admission of what she wants instead: "At these times I desire a hostile objectivity that will distance us all from our psychic excess so that there will reside, in some space somewhere, a clear, pure Signified that I can easily access and write up. **That's where I go when I'm scared.**"

**The description of Jeri that she then withdraws**, which is the article's method in two lines: "She's popular, friendly, confident, happy, accepting of others. / **At least, that's what her voice tells me.**"

**The passage the article is built around, quoted in full and not adjudicated.** Jeri on teaching in Manders: "We have more Hispanics moved in than we ever have. . . . And the school system is going to force us to learn Spanish. But I refuse. They can go back where they came from if they can't learn English. But I love my little Hispanics. I have three in my classroom, and they are sweeter than any children that I teach. . . . So I really shouldn't be bitter about them moving in. I just don't want our school system to be taken over by them."

**Jackson's response, recorded as behaviour rather than as interpretation**: "I smile and nod. Do I communicate understanding? **The Trickster in my head opens her mouth and laughs aloud.** Can Jeri hear the laughter in my head during this awkward silence? I try to quiet her laughter by looking down at the interview guide, desperately figuring where to go next. The interview guide seems absurd, a failure." And the Trickster's own question: she "wants to know how Jeri rescues her own racist beliefs by (conditionally) 'loving' Hispanic children in her classroom but not the presence of their extended families in the community."

**She does not ask it.** Instead: "I want to tell Jeri this. I want to tell her how, in other cultures, it is acceptable and expected that extended family live together under one roof. I fantasize about myself coming back to Manders . . . as a teacher in the small-school system. I am a maverick. I deconstruct. I teach about racism, classism, and sexism. **I romanticize myself** – my liberation, my actions, my choices. But, I trip and stumble on this seduction. The Trickster laughs again."

**The childhood memory she sets against Jeri's account**, which does the work an analysis section would: aged nine, the only white child on the school bus to her grandmother's house, staring at houses "that seem to be rotting and sinking into the earth" with "shiny, late-model cars" parked outside. "I am to find out more than 20 years later, from one of the Black women I will interview for my dissertation, that Black people in this community do not have the choice to move outside the bounds of this segregated section of Manders. But they can buy new cars and show that poverty lines are something imposed on them through housing discrimination."

**How she leaves**: "I leave 3 hours after I arrived with my bag of interview equipment haphazardly packed. Unlike Jeri's life as she presents it, the pieces of my dissertation are anything but falling together perfectly. **She sends me away with a nice, tidy ending, but I leave her a clumsy thief, a non-innocent intruder**, with stories that are not woven together but unraveling at the so-called seams."

**And her statement of where the analysis actually lives**: "Yes, I listen to her voice within my theory and my agenda, but it is **the texture of our voices together** that produce contradictions, tensions, losses of meanings and understandings, and the difficulties of expression. The moments of subtle discomfort point to each of the vulnerabilities and betrayals in our tellings."

**The ending is eight unattributed lines under two headings**, printed without punctuation marks of attribution so that it is unclear who speaks which:

> Where is the power? / I'm not going to tell you this. / I'm going to direct you to tell me this. / I will take you far from where you want to go. / I will not interfere – not now, anyway, but perhaps later.
>
> Where is the knowledge? / Please give it to me so that I will know. / You can't know, but I'll tell you what I think you can/want to hear. / Will I have the courage to ask? / Will you have the courage to tell?

**One small observation that connects three articles.** A garage door opens in this 2003 piece too: "The sound of the automatic garage door opening interrupts my anxiety. Jeri is waving me in, smiling." Fourteen years later a garage door opening is the hinge of the Amelia reading in Mazzei and Jackson 2017. Same fieldwork region, same domestic architecture, two different functions. Recorded as a coincidence of place, not a claim.

# PART TWO: TWO OUTSIDE THE LINEAGE

## 4. Somerville 2004: the body that keeps disappearing, and pit sense

**The problem she names, and she names it through other people's texts rather than in the abstract.** Feminism has produced a great deal of theory about the body and very little research into lived bodies, because "the specter of essentialism" makes the corporeal body dangerous ground. She quotes Vicki Kirby's 1991 naming of it: "The increasingly sterile debate between essentialism and antiessentialism has inadvertently encouraged this **somatophobia**."

**Her move is to catch the body disappearing in texts about the body**, and she does it three times.

*Kirby.* "Kirby's article is remarkably lacking in the bodily presence/absence she names. . . . The one lived body that is present in her text is the pinched body of an apologist." In a forum debating Irigaray's essentialism, Kirby asked about the anatomical body and the respondent "pinched herself and commented 'Well, I certainly don't mean this body'". Kirby's own reply, quoted: "it is not the question that should produce consternation but rather an answer that presumes to engage the problematic of feminism and corporeality by denying the relevance of this particular, lived body. **Just where should we suppose that other body might be located?**"

*Probyn.* She opened a paper with a discussion of how her own body led her to her work on anorexia, and found that her "body can do no right: it makes postmodernists nervous and certain feminists angry at my supposed betrayal of the empirical weight of the female body". Somerville: "This is the only point at which any lived body appears in a text about the body."

*St. Pierre.* And this one is a direct audit, of the same author whose afterword is in the previous batch. In St. Pierre's 2000 overview of feminist poststructural theory, "There are only two references to 'body' in her paper. In the first, the body is an **abstract metaphor**, 'bodies of thought and practice' and in the second . . . the subject of liberal humanism is '[u]nique, unitary, unencumbered, the self escapes all forms of embodiment'." Her verdict: "This version of feminist poststructural theory does not offer any alternative possibilities for embodiment."

**And the sharpest line on metaphor in this batch is one she quotes**, from Bibi Bakare-Yusuf, who critiques contemporary theorising as "**mere flirtation with the idea of the lived body where the experience of lived bodies is constituted as a metaphor that is 'good to think with'**". Which gives the argument: "we need to make connections with matters of the flesh on the lived body."

**Three methodological gestures she extracts from the literature and then uses**, named and credited: "naïve accounts of experience" (Marshall), "using the body (at the scene of work) as a strategy" (Simmonds), and "unearthing bodily and embodied experiences in memory and diary" (Simmonds). Plus her own addition, "the body-at-the-scene-of-writing". And the reason "naïve" is not an insult: Marshall's point that "it is precisely because it is so difficult to name our bodies and what they do that corporeal feminist theory needs to pay more attention to naïve accounts of experiences."

**The article is interleaved with dated journal entries in her own body.** It opens on a treadmill: "Walking at a steady pace on the treadmill; five minutes walk, five minutes run. Walking, thinking about body, (my) body. Its outline, feel the shape with my hand over shoulder and breast, hip and thigh, tracing a bodyline. . . . **My body at the scene of thinking, writing; your body reading, body connection, bodylines.**" Later, 24/11/01, her daughter leaving home: "Stretch of body, like loss of limb, umbilical cord still attached, temporarily." And 10/12/01, her own heart: "This thing that is wrong with my heart—it makes strange leaps and hurts in my chest, not much, a little, but the leaps are strange, like the expression 'your heart in your mouth'. . . . My friend, who has it too, says that the trouble is, your heart can just stop beating." With the self-question attached: "Is this something I should reveal? It is dangerous territory."

**Where the writing came from**, and this is unusual provenance for a method: "The body writing grew out of a crisis of the body in academic work. I was suffering from exhaustion and trying to write a paper about relationship to place. The more I thought about the problem, the more distressed I became." Her response was a series of massages with a body practitioner, Cathy Carmont, who was herself theorising massage and who recorded their talk for her own research: "she provided a space of trust where I could risk madness, speaking images that surfaced from tissue, organs, joints, and bone, **without the need to make sense**." The journal entries that came out of it are printed, including: "A tight place in my tummy, a hard, black rock about fist-size, round and flat. As Cathy massages, the rock is washed with water which wells up into throat and eyes."

**The passage most worth having for the body-and-place thread**, a picnic entry: "Warm and sleepy, I lie in shallow dip on lichen-dimpled surface of big flat rock, face into brightness. . . . My skin becomes flecked with grey and light apple green lichen-lace and I am a lizard, grey scales and flecks, lying on a rock in the sun. I become so flat I am the rock, body blends into its surface, tufts of soft green moss around my edges and voices of children playing over me. **I am the surface of the earth and they are playing on my edges.**"

**The empirical half: safe bodies in mine work.** She analysed *Pit Yarns*, two booklets of mine workers' stories published by a workplace communications trainer in the Hunter Valley in 1997, then interviewed **20 mine workers** on site, then went down a mine.

From the textual analysis, a category she names "**degradation of the flesh**", with the story that produced it: "Ian dropped a prop on old Tommy's finger and it was hanging off by a bit of skin. Old Tommy was real hard on him and said 'Ah don't worry about that' and pulled it off. Well, Ian's fainted. They put Ian in the back of the machine and old Tommy in the front with the finger off." And the gendering of the mine itself, quoted from a worker: "As you drive your sixty ton machine into the coal, you can hear mother nature protest, the coal will crush out of the face and ribs, the roof will roar and groan. She will explode the coal out of the rib at you, stinging your body if you are in the wrong place." Her reading: "body/place relations are represented in this story in metaphors of sexual violence where the body/matter of the mine and the mine worker's body are both damaged. . . . **The flesh of body and the flesh of place are subject to the same discursive rules of violent degradation.**"

**Two negotiations she had to make before the interviews**, and both are transferable. First, parity: "to acknowledge that we shared a common problem of risking our bodies in unsafe work practices. I had to acknowledge the academic body at the site of work, a return to the (dis)appearing body in my own earlier work. . . . **I was not researching mine workers who were in some way differently negligent.**" Second, the workers' own condition: "the mine workers' requirement that I experience the mine first hand. A visit down the closest mine had been organized on the day after the interviews and **without this I would have had no credibility with the mine workers.**"

Her journal from underground is printed: "Over the lip of light, trees grass sky, and smell of eucalypts, disappear; down a black tunnel, moths flutter on skin of face in small narrow light of a lamp attached to my head. . . . On the pit floor gumboots stick in slush." And what it bought her: "My listening was intense because the world they described was so foreign but **my sense of place was only established by my bodily presence in the pit**."

**And what the interviews did to her own prior analysis.** "Face to face, the tall, muscular, idealized bodies of the mine workers' yarns gave way to small, medium, thin, fat, fit, and unfit bodies." And: "**The interviews shattered any certainties that might have resulted from that analysis** and opened up whole new possibilities for understanding."

**Pit sense**, the workers' own term and the article's best finding: "how mine workers inhabit the mine as place and how they know whether the body/mine is safe at any particular moment. It is a profoundly embodied knowledge and can only be learned through body/place relations." In a worker's words: "They know that the roof's bad, they know by hearing it, they know by smell, they know by the sense of just being there and being uncomfortable, the heaviness of the air, that you're in a place where you shouldn't be, lack of oxygen or gas. You'll feel the hairs move up on your legs, y'know, with black damp or something there."

**And the thing pit sense turns out to depend on**, which nobody would predict: ritual sameness, which the workers call homeliness. "Blokes will get dressed the same way every day. They will get undressed the same way. They will go to their own shower, every day . . . They fill their drink bottle the same way, you can watch them. They're just creatures of habit." Somerville's reading: "It is only because of this acute and intense sense of things being-in-place and out-of-place that the mine workers can know when they are in danger." And the workers' own simile for noticing danger: "Whether it be like walking into the lounge room and seeing bloody chips all over the floor. They go, 'Bloody hell, what a mess, I don't like that', or they see a crack in their roof."

**Three other Australian researchers she maps for method**, with the analytic categories she takes from each: Grosz on *chora*, for the discursive ground of body/place; Bronwyn Davies's collective biography, for "being-in-place, being out-of-place, and learning bodily competence in place"; Erica McWilliam on the teacher's body, for "pain and pleasure", "the senses" and "the face". Plus her own borrowing of "slanted" or "coming at it obliquely" from Power, for "certain indirect analytical categories [that] may enable more access to lived body experience."

## 5. Kairienė, Mažeikienė and Rupšienė 2026: the lineage applied, two decades on

**Why this one is here.** It is a 2026 empirical study that runs explicitly on Jackson and Mazzei's "thinking with theory", citing the **2023 second edition** specifically, plus Masny's rhizoanalysis and Deleuze and Guattari. So it shows what the previous five articles look like when a research team in another country applies them to a real sample. That makes it a check on the lineage rather than a contribution to it.

**The study.** **21 participants**, all early-career education researchers who had completed a doctorate, "from different universities and living in different regions of Lithuania". Of the 21: 12 researching only in education and 9 across education plus philology, philosophy or history; none under 21 or over 60, with 9 aged 21-40 and 12 aged 41-60; work experience ranging from 1-5 years (3 people) to 21-30 years (3); publication counts from 1-5 articles (8 people) to over 30 (3). **About 20 hours of interviews in total**, semi-structured, run "until conceptual saturation was reached". Ethics reviewed by the university's committee.

**What they claim not to have done**, following St. Pierre: "in post-qualitative inquiry, we did not employ coding, categorisation, or thematic analysis. We were engaged in thinking through Deleuze and Guattari's theory." Their procedure instead: read the transcripts slowly and repeatedly "with the theory-arising question: **what would Deleuze and Guattari say?**", then draw rhizomatic maps on Deleuze and Guattari's own four principles, then apply Masny's rhizoanalysis.

**Their positionality statement is a small model of the form**: three female researchers, one in a postdoctoral fellowship and two in senior roles, who describe themselves as "liquid in-betweener" after Barnes, "shifting between roles of insiders and outsiders", and who name their three disciplines as education, philology and philosophy, "amalgamating our disciplinary backgrounds . . . into a rhizome that grows in unpredictable directions."

**The findings, as a frequency claim** (which is itself a slightly odd thing for a rhizoanalysis to produce, and is recorded here as theirs): "The most common assemblages are art-exploration assemblages, more-than-human assemblages, and work-related practice assemblages. The less common assemblages are material-led assemblages and affective (body and emotions) assemblages." The becomings they name: becoming-explorer, becoming-artist, becoming-expert of research methods, becoming-AI user, and becoming readers and writers.

**Five participant accounts worth having.**

*The methodology lecture.* A researcher told on 28 August that she would teach a methodology module starting 4 or 5 September: "all my former lecturers became my colleagues. One of my colleagues said, 'teach from intuition'. Oh, my goodness, how can you teach research methodology based on intuition? There is no intuition here. I had a lecture on Thursday, and for half a year, every Thursday, I would get up at 5:00 a.m. and prepare for the lecture."

*The dance at the conference.* A feedback session where "who wants to talk, who wants to write, and here is the moment when one person stood up and did a modern dance to express this. It was very unexpected."

*Lithography as method.* A researcher who visited a lithography school with artists from Hong Kong, New York and Los Angeles: "Lithography as a process is very similar to research methodology. . . . when you do research, and when you write a discussion, or describe it, a second time, or rewrite your conclusions, that moment is already the methodology of writing conclusions, it's like lithography: first, second, third. . . . Since the number is limited, and they are all different. I sometimes think that we write conclusions in the same way."

*The floor covered in printouts*, which is the one affective-assemblage account in the paper and the best of the five: "When I conduct thematic analysis, I print out all the material and cover the entire floor. And then I make what I call branches, subthemes, because I need to organise the picture in my own mind. . . . I have to write things down the old-fashioned way, mark them with colours, **sleep in that room**, get up at night, and physically immerse myself in the study. So, you know, I am such an out-of-schooler." Their reading: "the researcher engages in relation to the material" when she gets up at night, and "her body interrupts the linear time of biological rest."

*The bread city.* A Deleuze and Guattari conference where the lunch break featured an art project made of Swedish crispbread: "it was an entire city built from these breads, and there were butter towers on the sides, there were also apple and jam spreads, but the most interesting thing was that on the table there was a description that provided a philosophical description of Deleuze and Guattarian concepts, such as affect, body, etc." Their reading: "The entire city of Swedish bread is presented as a **body without organs**, with many exits and entrances."

**The AI material**, which is the newest thing in the paper and the reason it is dated 2026. Participants use ChatGPT for letters and searching, and Elicit for systematic literature reviews. One: "when AI develops so rapidly, scientists will need to increase the volume, conducting not 5, not 10, but **100 interviews**. I would very much like the scientific world to go deeper and look for why and how these problems can be solved, rather than just stating some facts." The authors read this as multiplicity and as a move "to a smooth space". Others name YouTube lectures and bought Udemy courses as their real methodological training.

**And they declare their own AI use**, under a heading of its own: "**AI Use for Research Purposes.** Deepl and Grammarly tools helped authors to proofread the manuscript text and enhance academic writing."

**Their conclusion is prescriptive**, which the lineage they draw on generally refuses to be: "education researchers should strengthen their learning of research methodologies by engaging in more material-led practice, establishing and working in research laboratories, conducting innovative research, and creating new methods themselves."

## 6. What these five change in the folder's picture

**The voice line is now eight texts and complete through 2017.** Jackson 2003 ("Rhizovocality" and this companion piece), St. Pierre 2009, Mazzei 2010, Jackson 2010, Mazzei 2013, Jackson 2013, Mazzei and Jackson 2017, Jackson and Mazzei 2024. The one clear gap is "An ontology of a backflip" (2016) and "Voice without a subject" (2016), neither held.

**One participant per theorist, across five articles.** Anne, read through Deleuze's cinema books (Mazzei 2010). Jeri, read through the Trickster and left unresolved (Jackson 2003). Jesse, read through becoming and the molar/molecular (Jackson 2010). Fran, read through the body without organs (Mazzei 2013). Cassandra, read through Pickering's mangle (Jackson 2013). Amelia, read through Bennett's agentic assemblage (Mazzei and Jackson 2017). Six people, six frames, one pair of authors. **Anyone citing this lineage should know which participant belongs to which article**, because the names are not interchangeable and the readings do not transfer.

**Kirby is the link to a read already in the folder.** *Quantum Anthropologies* is quoted three times in the 2017 article and Kirby's 1991 and 1997 work is the spine of Somerville's problem statement. Both the Kirby collection reads and *Quantum Anthropologies* are already in `todo October 26`, so they can be checked against these two uses.

**The metaphor thread gains one quoted position and one audit, and neither is a new author position.**

The quoted position is **Bakare-Yusuf**, via Somerville: theorising that treats "the experience of lived bodies . . . as a metaphor that is 'good to think with'" is "mere flirtation". That is the same complaint as McKnight and Whitburn's, made in 1999 about the body rather than in 2017 about the lens, and it is worth recording because it is the earliest version of the complaint now in the folder.

The audit is **Somerville on St. Pierre**: in St. Pierre's 2000 overview, the only two appearances of "body" are an abstract metaphor and a quotation. That is a precise, checkable observation about a text by an author whose afterword is in the previous batch, and it is the kind of count this folder's metaphor thread runs on.

Mazzei and Jackson 2017 uses "figuration" twice and "metaphor" not once, consistent with Jackson's 2003 endnote preference and with Mazzei and McCoy's 2010 heading. So the lineage's practice is stable across fourteen years: figuration, never metaphor. **Seven positions still stand; nothing is added or revised.**

**Somerville is the closest thing in the folder to a method for the body-and-place reading.** Her three borrowed gestures plus her own fourth, her dated body journals, her massage provenance, and the mine fieldwork together make a worked example of bringing a lived body into relation with theory without either essentialising it or losing it. That sits beside Neimanis on water, Fries and Fahey on art practice, Donnarumma on the performing body and Shildrick on the prosthetic one. **Pit sense** in particular is a term for embodied place knowledge taken from the people who have it, which is what Fries's and Fahey's close reads keep circling without a word for.

**Kairienė et al is the folder's one case of the lineage being applied by other people.** Two things in it are worth holding without resolving. They cite the 2023 edition of *Thinking with Theory*, which is the edition that retired "data" in favour of "performative accounts", and they then write throughout about "raw data", "data analysis", "data saturation" and "data bias", and defend their sampling with Creswell and Maxwell on validity and bias. And they say "we did not employ coding, categorisation, or thematic analysis" while reporting which assemblages were "most common" and "less common". Both are tensions between the vocabulary they adopt and the vocabulary they keep. **Recorded as a tension in the article, not adjudicated**, because the lineage's own texts say repeatedly that a complete departure is not possible and that "traces remain, despite our cartographic intentions."

## 7. Citation faults and things to check before citing

**Mazzei and Jackson 2017.**
- Braidotti's publisher is printed "**Polilty**" for Polity.
- "humans themselves are assessed not as **autonoms**" in a quotation from Bennett, for autonomies or autonomous.
- Pickering is listed twice, 1993 and 1999, as the same title in two places. Not an error, but see section 1.
- Livesey's entry has the year inside the page reference: "(Livesey, p. 19, 2010)".

**Jackson 2010.**
- **A reference-list entry with the author names mangled**: "Robert, C.B., and K.B. Sari. 1998. *Qualitative research for education*". This is Bogdan, R.C. and Biklen, S.K., with surnames replaced by given names and initials reversed. The body text cites it as "(e.g., see Robert and Biklen 1998)", mixing the mangled name with the correct one.
- "**Althone** Press" for Athlone Press in the Deleuze *Logic of Sense* entry.
- Sotirin's chapter is in *Gilles Deleuze: Key Concepts*, published by McGill-Queen's, but the place is given as "Ithaca, NY", which is Cornell's city.
- Stagoll's "Becoming" entry gives *The Deleuze dictionary* as "New York: Columbia University Press"; it is Edinburgh University Press.
- The contributor note dates *Voice in Qualitative Inquiry* to "September 2008"; it is 2009 everywhere else, including in Mazzei's own 2010 reference list.

**Jackson 2003.**
- No reference list at all, so the Trickster, which comes from Haraway via Visweswaran in "Rhizovocality" in the same issue, is unattributed here.

**Somerville 2004.**
- The PDF's production block gives the author as "**MargaretSomervilleDr.**" and the email as "**e**msomervi@metz.une.edu.au" with a stray leading character; the correct address appears in the footnote.
- St Pierre is printed without the full stop ("St Pierre") throughout, where the author's own usage is "St.Pierre".
- The *Feminist theory and the body* collection is cited as "(Benton Rushing, 1999)" for the whole volume and also as a single-author chapter in the same year, so the editor and a contributor share one citation form.
- "Price & Shildrick, 1999" is given for *Feminist Theory and the Body*; the Price and Shildrick volume dates from 1999 and the related *Vital Signs* from 1998, and the two are easy to confuse. Check which before citing.

**Kairienė et al 2026.**
- The article's "Atkinson (2020)" in the body is "Anne-Marie Atkinson"; the reference list gives "**Atkinson, L. M.** (2020)". Two different initials for one author.
- Two different years are given for the same Deleuze and Guattari text: they cite *A Thousand Plateaus* as "(2004)" throughout, which is the Continuum reissue, while every other text in this folder cites the 1987 Massumi translation. The page numbers therefore will not match the other articles' citations of the same passages.
- "Cole (2022)" appears in the body with no first name and is the only citation in that form.
- Several sentences carry grammatical slips that survived the declared Grammarly pass, for example "it shows how early-career researchers, holding a doctoral degree, learning research methodologies".
- The table is headed "Please read the data in the Table from left to right", which suggests a column misalignment in the typeset version.

Quotations above are given with spacing repaired, production blocks removed and nothing else changed.
