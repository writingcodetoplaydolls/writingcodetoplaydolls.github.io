# *Contemporary Materialism*: chapters 4 and 5

2 Nov 2026. New file. The companion is `ROMERO_ContemporaryMaterialism_read.md` (29 Oct), which recorded chapters 4 and 5 as not read. **This file reads them both in full.**

- **Chapter 4, "Quantum Matter", Gustavo E. Romero** (pp. 155-176)
- **Chapter 5, "Spacetime Is Material", Luciano Combi** (pp. 177-197)

**Read this first.** These two chapters give a sustained, technical, physics-internal position on quantum mechanics, measurement, the uncertainty relations, entanglement and spacetime that **reads the same formalism very differently from the Barad texts in this folder.** Both are set out below as positions, with their own arguments and their own citations, and **no attempt is made here to settle which reading is right.** Section 7 lays the divergences side by side so the difference is visible and usable rather than buried.

---

## 1. Chapter 4 in one paragraph

Romero's stated method is "to stay close to the formalism, and to apply the tools of modern semantics to the analysis of this formalism", and specifically: "**I will pay attention not to what scientists say, but to what they do when they research.**" His route is axiomatization: cast the theory into axiomatic format so that the assumptions linking formal constructs to extra-linguistic objects are made explicit. From that he argues four things. **There is no observer in the theory**; **the Heisenberg inequalities are theorems about quantum properties, not statements about knowledge**; **entanglement is non-local correlation, not causal action**; and **the ontology is fields, not particles** ("quantum objects are quantum fields over spacetime"). His summary line: "**Quantum mechanics is strange, surely, but it is so because reality is strange to us, not because it doesn't exist or because it is immaterial.**"

## 2. What he is arguing against, in the physicists' own words

The chapter is useful partly as a collection of the primary quotations, with page numbers, that the idealist readings rest on.

## 3. The four claims, with his reasons

**No observer.** From the axiomatization: "**the direct referents of the theory are quantum systems and their environments, not observers or instruments, much less minds or conscious states.**" On collapse, he denies that anything collapses: "**The probability has not 'collapsed' after the measurement** from the initially predicted value to a value pk = 1. The a priori probability remains the same, **exactly as the a priori probability before a roll of dice does not change or collapse when the dice finds a final state.**" What does the work instead is the environment: "the only thing that can affect the state is the interaction with the environment", and the account of that belongs not to quantum mechanics but to the quantum theory of measurement, "which rests on the concept of **decoherence**". He concedes the gap: "**A general theory of quantum measurement does not exist, and it is dubious whether it can be consistently formulated in all generality** since it should depend on the specific experimental device."

**The uncertainty relations are theorems.** "the Heisenberg inequalities **are theorems and have nothing to do with the effect of any observer**. They can be derived from the non-commutation of the corresponding operators and the so-called Schwartz inequality, that is purely mathematical." He also corrects the energy-time form: "**Since there is no time operator in quantum mechanics, the forth inequality (4.13) does not strictly hold.**" His conclusion: "**They have nothing to do with our knowledge, uncertainty, or our observations. They just say that some quantum properties are not defined simultaneously in a sharp way.** Such properties are not classical properties, but **sui generis** properties of quantum systems."

**Entanglement is correlation, not action.** The experimental record is given (Bell 1964, 1966; Freedman and Clauser 1972; Aspect et al. 1981, 1982): "There are not hidden variables in the theory." But: "**Causality is relation among events, not among things** . . . when we determined the state of one of the components of the entangled system, **there is no change in the state of the other component** . . . **There is no causal connection between components at all; there are just non-local correlations**", and "it is because of no actual work is done that information cannot be transmitted faster than light through entanglement."

**Fields, not particles, and the wave-particle duality dissolved.** "**The so-called wave-particle duality actually does not exist. Quantum systems are neither waves nor particles.** They can display under some conditions a behavior that might resemble that of a wave and under other circumstances that of a classical particle, but they are neither of them: **they are sui generis entities.**" The argument for fields is the frame-dependence of particle number: the expectation value of the number operator differs between mode decompositions, so "**different observers detect a different number of quanta (particles) . . . They are essentially a frame-dependent feature of the field**", and under the principle that "**what there is cannot depend on our way to describe it**", particles lose their claim to be basic. What is invariant is energy-momentum, defined locally by a tensor, so "**the ontological import is in the quantum field, not in the particles. And it is not neither in the structure, since the structure emerges from the relations of the fields.**" The vacuum is a state of the field, not its absence: it "**should not be confused with actual vacuum or absence of field. It has properties, such as energy fluctuations and is susceptible of polarization.**" Unruh and Hawking radiation are used as the worked cases. His test of materiality is change: "**If we understand as material entities those capable of changing**, this implies that the quantum fields are the true material constituents of the world **because energy is just the capability of changing.**"

**The count, stated plainly**: "**12 fundamental fields for fermions** (6 quarks and 6 leptons) and **13 fundamental fields for bosons** (8 gluons; 3 for W+, W- and Z0; 1 for the photon and 1 for the Higgs). All these fields exist on a background spacetime." And the conclusion about the furniture of the world: "**Matter, then, seems to come in two flavors: spacetime and quantum fields**", with Weyl's title borrowed for the answer: "**space, time, matter. Or better, material spacetime and material quantum fields.**"

He also marks his own position as one among several: "This is **not the only** realist and objective interpretation that can be proposed for quantum mechanics. The Many-Worlds interpretation, for instance, adopts the collapse postulate and interprets it at face value accepting an ontological inflation. **The overabundant ontology that results is perfectly compatible with materialistic views.**"

## 4. Chapter 5 in one paragraph

Combi argues that **spacetime is material because it can change and act**, and that this follows from General Relativity's background independence, but he insists it is "**a different kind of matter**" from fields. The chapter is organised around the old substantivalism/relationism dispute, which he declines to settle in those terms, and then works through events, change, energy, entropy and cosmological scale. The frame is Wheeler's line, quoted: "**Spacetime tells matter how to move, matter tells spacetime how to curve.**"

## 5. The pieces most likely to be useful

**He refuses the two-camp framing and says why.** He quotes Rynasiewicz: "**Present-day physicists do not employ a language that conforms with the original contrast, and the current controversy is fueled by so much squabbling over how to appropriate modern terminology to one's own doctrinaire advantage**", and then: "**Instead of analyzing what category is more appropriate, let us start from some formal grounds**", namely the axiomatic basis, where one can say which elements are substantial (things) and which relational (emergent structures or properties). His own verdict keeps both: "**the metric field represents a dynamic entity called spacetime (substantial), while the absence of prior background, highlighted by the Hole Argument, renders the theory relational.**"

**Einstein changing his own mind is quoted** (1956): "**On the basis of the general theory of relativity space as opposed to 'what fills space' has no separate existence.** If we imagine the gravitational field, i.e., the functions g to be removed, there does not remain a space of the type (1) (Minkowski spacetime), but **absolutely nothing**, and also no 'topological space'. **There is no such thing as an empty space, i.e., a space without a field.**"

**The ontological shift is named**: in GR "Einstein promotes the entire kinematical structure of Special Relativity to a dynamical structure", which "implies a **profound ontological shift** since the kinematical structure, the underlying way to describe the physical laws is now a representation of a dynamic physical system: spacetime. **In short, there are no more fixed structures in the theory, relativity becomes general.**"

**Events: common nature, no individuality.** This is the piece most likely to matter here. Combi gives a sample event in full ("spacetime has a Ricci scalar curvature of value R0 when a particle has a position X and its proper time is τ0"), and then:

> "we take the physical fields, we solve the dynamics, and **only then it makes sense to talk about the properties, or existence of a particular event**." (With a footnote: "**In this sense, there is a holistic flavor to General Relativity.**")

> "Stachel has shown that the picture where the elements of the ontology are the fields is better described in the fiber bundle formalism of GR, **where we do not need an underlying manifold to formulate the theory**. In this approach, he also describes events as **having a common nature (what he calls quiddity) but lacking individuality (what he calls haecceity)**. In this sense, events seem to have **some kind of ontological vagueness** that reminds us of similar issues for identical particle states in quantum physics."

**Change without an external clock.** "**there is no external time in GR**"; spacetime can be dynamic with nothing else present ("black holes can merge in a vacuum"); what cannot be had alone is a scale: "**what is impossible to determine in the absence of other physical systems is an intrinsic length scale between these two events, i.e., it is not possible to measure a rate of change** . . . a scale (e.g., clocks and rods) can only be established by massive systems". The asymmetry is real but the description is not: "**This asymmetry between the past and future is an invariant fact of spacetime, but the concrete description of the evolution depends on the frame we choose**", footnoted with Curie: "l'asymétrie crée le phénomène."

**Existence as a four-dimensional worm, and the commitment it carries.** "a material system exists as a **4-dimensional 'worm' occupying a hypersurface D** . . . **In a Parmenidean sense, since this state cannot change, relativity commits to Eternalism.**" With the qualification: "**this does not mean that time or change is altogether an illusion for entities** . . . Contrary to a Newtonian ontology, what exist is not attached to a particular moment." And a limit on his own ontology: "**events mark the interaction between two things. As we said, a pure ontology of events would have to look essentially different from our thing-based ontology.**"

**A definition of energy worth having.** "**Energy, as an ontological property, represents the changeability of a thing**" (Bunge 2010), and "the concept of energy cannot be conceived without an underlying of spacetime" (Lehmkuhl 2011), since the energy-momentum tensor is defined by differentiating the Lagrangian with respect to the metric. Also: "**no matter the intrinsic nature of matter, spacetime is only concerned with its energy-momentum.**"

**Gravitational energy cannot be local.** "locally, spacetime looks flat. This shows that **a local notion of gravitational energy is elusive. Gravitational energy must be, at least, a quasi-local concept**, a quantity that characterizes an extended region and not a local region." Two constructions are given with what each is for: the **ADM mass** (surface integral at infinity, which is what "the mass of a black hole" usually means, and which keeps the radiated energy in the total) and the **Bondi-Sachs mass** (measured on a null surface, so "any radiated energy remains in the past", which is what connects to a gravitational-wave detector).

**Entropy, and why the second law is a problem.** "If the second law of thermodynamics is globally valid, then **there seems to be a contradiction on how the Universe evolved from a thermal equilibrium state that maximizes the entropy and the current state of the Universe that is colder and structured.**" The usual answer is that spacetime carries its own entropy and started low; Penrose's proposal is to build it from the Weyl tensor; black-hole entropy goes as area (Bekenstein 1973). Wallace's qualification is kept: "**spacetime acts mainly as a catalyst for entropy increasing processes**", and "in most local scenarios, e.g. to develop life on earth, the gravitational entropy itself is not relevant".

**The cosmological constant, deflated.** "Physicists often speak about a fundamental mystery surrounding this constant. Although this has motivated alternative explanations for and new theories, **the character of the problem is greatly exaggerated**"; on the 55-orders-of-magnitude discrepancy, "**it is a mistake to apriori identify this constant as the vacuum energy, since the mechanism of quantum gravity is still unknown**"; and "**The fuzz is indeed unjustified.**"

**Scale, in both directions, as an open dispute.** The **backreaction problem**: "**does the presence of inhomogeneities change the global solution?**" Green and Wald (2011) say backreactions are negligible; Buchert et al. (2015) say their scheme is not general; Green and Wald reply (2015). **Combi leaves the dispute open and names both sides**, noting that inhomogeneous cosmologies "could explain current observations of the accelerated Universe as an alternative to the standard model". And the top-down direction: "**the mere identification of a local gravitational system is problematic in an expanding Universe** . . . in an expanding universe, it is hard to find black hole solutions." His own summary of the two-way traffic: "**understanding our surroundings might give us information about the entire Universe, and the other way around**", with the emergence problem that follows: "**If the Universe is unique, we cannot distinguish boundary conditions from physical laws locally.**"

**The chapter ends with a dictionary**, which is its most immediately usable part: formal definitions of **spacetime model**, **affine connection**, **equation of motion** and **absolute structure**, and then the four distinctions that are routinely conflated: **covariance**, **invariance** ("invariance is much more restrictive than covariance"), **general covariance** ("the equations of any theory can be written, in principle, in a general covariant way"), **general invariance**, and **background independence** (Anderson 1967: "a theory is background independent if their dynamical EOM are free of absolute structures"), with the warning that "**a precise definition of background independence is very subtle and is not equal to general invariance**" and that even GR retains arguably absolute structures (dimension, signature, volume elements).

## 6. Where both chapters state their own limits

Worth noting as a habit, since both authors are arguing for a strong position and still mark the holes:

- "A general theory of quantum measurement does not exist, and it is dubious whether it can be consistently formulated in all generality" (Romero)
- "Whether these two kinds of matter can be reduced one into the other, is something to be found" (Romero, closing sentence)
- "The description of spacetime given by general relativity, however, is far from satisfactory: many physically realistic models are singular" (Romero)
- "Both ways have problems and it is not even clear whether they can be formulated consistently" (Romero, on routes to quantum gravity)
- "If events can be ultimately described as things or not will depend on whether a consistent ontological theory of events compatible with GR can be formulated" (Combi)
- "we do not have a microscopic theory of spacetime to obtain a self-consistent measure of entropy" (Combi)
- "An understanding of these issues is necessary to get a clear picture that can lead the way to a quantum theory of gravity **if such a theory could be formulated**" (Combi, closing sentence)

## 7. The divergence, laid side by side

Set out so the difference is visible. **The two columns are what each source says, not a judgment between them.**

| On | Romero / Combi (2022) | The Barad material already in this folder |
|---|---|---|
| **the observer** | "the direct referents of the theory are quantum systems and their environments, **not observers or instruments, much less minds or conscious states**" | the apparatus is constitutive, and the agential cut is enacted within the phenomenon |
| **collapse** | nothing collapses; "the a priori probability remains the same, exactly as . . . before a roll of dice"; the environment and decoherence do the work | the measurement is where determinacy is produced |
| **the uncertainty relations** | "theorems", from non-commutation and the Schwartz inequality, "nothing to do with our knowledge, uncertainty, or our observations" | read as indeterminacy rather than uncertainty, which is a third position again, and not the same as either column |
| **wave-particle duality** | "actually does not exist"; quantum systems are "sui generis entities", neither | the two-slit apparatus is the standing case for complementarity |
| **superposition** | a consequence of the linearity of the dynamical equations, holding "at the level of states", which "leads naturally to entanglement" | taken up as an ontological figure, and (per her own note of 2026) read divergently across sources on purpose |
| **entanglement** | "There is no causal connection between components at all; there are just non-local correlations" | entanglement as the ontological primitive, with relata not preceding relations |
| **what there is** | fields and spacetime, both material, on the test that both carry energy and can act | phenomena, intra-actions, and the apparatus |
| **structure** | "it is not neither in the structure, since the structure emerges from the relations of the fields" | relations before relata |
| **Heisenberg's own philosophy** | quoted (1962: 145) and argued against | available in the folder in *Physics and Philosophy* |

**The thing to notice is that the two columns disagree about the same equations, and both work from the formalism.** Neither is a misreading of the mathematics; they are different semantic interpretations of it, which is exactly what Romero says his chapter is: an interpretation, and explicitly not the only realist one available.
