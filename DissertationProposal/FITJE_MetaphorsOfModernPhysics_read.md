**Why this chapter matters beyond the question it was fetched for.** It was located while looking for a discussion of metaphor's standing. It is that, but it is also **the single most useful text found in this folder for section 1.1**, because it is an artist writing about scientific images and why the central objects of modern physics cannot be pictured at all. It carries **five etymological arguments and one deliberate anti-etymology**, an eighth instance of measurement producing its object, a traces argument from particle physics, the empirical numbers on the reproducibility objection, and a definition of **transposition** by distinction from metaphor.

## 1. The chapter's actual position, which is not the one the editor's summary gives

**A correction to what I wrote earlier today.** I quoted Schwab's introduction, where the chapter is summarised as arguing that metaphors in cutting-edge science "occupy the places of emergent knowledge over time, **becoming the reality physicists work with rather than a reality 'out there' that they aim to represent**". That sentence is in the chapter's argument, but **the summary is a partial reading**. Fitje distinguishes **two characters of metaphor** and spends most of the chapter attacking the second.

**The active character**, which is the one the summary reports:

> "modern physics also exhibits **a more active character of metaphor** than the forthright comparative quality: an evocation of the inner connection between things, an awareness of duality, and a semblance of two different senses as one. In these terms, **the metaphor may be regarded as a transpositional act that hints at the very creation of things, blurring the gap between them and even between them and their names.** . . . At best they represent a mental fertility, a creative capacity to reinvent an old idea in the light of something new."

**The stagnant character**, which is most of the chapter:

> "There is a case to be made that the transpositions of physics do not necessarily signify the dynamic movement mentioned earlier . . . **They may as well represent a kind of reproduced regression.**"

> "Here, the models seem to have been fostered into exact duplicates; **the analogies have become equivalent to the thing, as if they were derived from it. But they are solely derived from the faculty of imagination.** This operation is rather a semiotic device that routinely conceals that **there is no thing to derive the model from. Curiously, the models come into existence because there is no base for modelling.**"

> "**The problem is not that metaphors are used, but rather that the same inaccurate ones are used and reused repeatedly without much consideration, until they become entangled with the elements they represent, entrenched in the so-called commonplace.**"

So his position is nearer to this: the figure is not a copy of a thing, because **there is no thing it could be a copy of**; and the danger is not metaphor but the forgetting that it is one. His closing formulation:

> "**Metaphors merely pose problems when they are not recognised as such; they also represent a fertility of language and a comparative capacity of mind.** The latter should be viewed as a benevolent feature **allowing us to identify similarities where, at first glance, they seem least likely to occur**."

**And he grades them.** This is not a position he holds in the abstract:

> "A generous view of the metaphor, one that is extended from the department of language, does not imply that all metaphors bespeak the same thing. There are clear differences connected to intentions as well as to practical usage and implications. **It may even be useful to point out some variations in quality, or at least in degrees of aptness.** In my opinion, **the billiard ball is an inapt metaphor**. This is because it makes assumptions about the properties and visual attributes of the atom that not only are inaccurate but also **undermine the core essence of the theories they represent**. **Dark matter**, on the other hand, is a metaphor merely expressing the fact that its constituents, still unknown to astrophysicists, do not interact with radiation of any sort. **It does not make any claims about the material composition, except that it is physically present and yet unseen.**"

**He also states the objection to his own method and does not resolve it:**

> "Does not the hyperbolic attention to the metaphor deprive us of any useful distinction between the 'real' and the 'depicted'? Are we not then running the risk of advocating a position from where everything may be regarded metaphorically? **This is exactly what quantum theory has already done, but without providing any replacement to fill the semantic vacuum it has left behind.**"

**And he audits his own text against his own criticism**, in the first person, in the last paragraph:

> "I have frequently resorted to various kinds of metaphors when assembling my arguments. In some cases this has been done intentionally, in others not. **I have habitually employed Reddy's conduit metaphors, practically in every sentence. I have even made use of popular illustrations resembling those that I most blatantly denounced.** The primary objective of this text has not been one of accusation."

For the first-person lane that last passage is a precedent of a particular kind: not a theory of the first person, but **a researcher closing by naming where his own text fails his own standard**, which is the Bordo structure (knowledge arriving through a practice done badly) in an artistic-research chapter.

## 2. What it gives 1.1, which is the biggest payload

The chapter opens on the limit of the image and never leaves it.

> "**No one has ever seen an atom.** This is due neither to a technological restriction nor entirely to a sensory one. There is, some would argue, a fixed lower limit of what we can see, namely the limit of light. In taking this view it is important to remember that **optical technology merely enables us to enlarge an image, never to improve it.**"

An ultraviolet microscope reaches about ten nanometres and stops; no light lets us see human genes, because, in Bronowski's phrase, "**they simply won't cast a shadow**". The governing limit is named and citable: the **Abbe diffraction limit**, that "the smallest objects you can single out have to have a size of about half the wavelength of the light employed in the microscope" (Born and Wolf 1997: 63). **Diffraction, as the limit of what an apparatus can resolve, rather than as a figure.**

What is done instead is to fire particles and read what they leave:

> "By employing, for instance, electron beams as the source of illumination, we can fire the particles towards an object to observe **the traces the particles leave** . . . we here trace details by penetrating inwards, probing underneath the visible surface. **This information is then, in a secondary process, converted into what is often called a shadow image.** Today such images are usually **constructed through computer simulations**, digitally converting the penetration data obtained from the particles. **We can map out atoms and prove that they exist, but what the images represent are merely their traces transposed.**"

He then refuses the clean distinction between real and false images, and the refusal carries the apparatus sentence:

> "even our most primitive photographic technology, detecting and redirecting light falling on a piece of plastic or glass, depends on a certain transposition from input to output. **Our visual perception itself involves interpretation and processing of the information contained in light. Each of these apparatuses possesses an individual limitation, and to claim that one in particular sets the standard of authenticity may turn out to be a deceitful prospect.**"

With the counterweight, which keeps it from collapsing into "all images are equal":

> "**Still, treating electron microscope images exactly like any other photography may lead to worse tragedies**, particularly as this seems to entrust us with the scientific virtue of looking objectively at nature, even at a subatomic level. It might be more constructive **to differentiate between input and output, or between signal and depiction**."

**Transposition is defined in photographic terms** before it is defined in any other: "The notion of transposition in photography often refers to the process of reversing the tonality of an image, as from negative to positive, from latent to manifest. . . . Image processing based on data from electron beams or X-radiation depends upon another, more concealed, form of transposition, **as a movement from information to figure**."

Four more items for this section.

**The undepictable, stated outright.** On the electron: the properties are known exactly (identical mass, charge and spin), and no picture survives contact with them. Oerter's **ice skater** fails, because for an electron to have the known spin and obey our laws of rotation "the 'ball' would have to be moving faster than the speed of light" (Oerter 2006: 95), "**Nor does it help replacing the circle with any other geometrical shape**." Then: "**What has charge and spin without being a spinning charge?**" and "how can something with no size have mass?" His conclusion is the sentence for 1.1: "**The electron puzzle reveals how deeply the impossibility of picturing these particles is embedded in the theories about them. They are undepictable by definition.**"

**Bohr refusing visualisation, with a date.** "In 1925, Niels Bohr, who himself had only two years earlier explained photon collisions in terms of the classical billiard ball picture, felt obliged to withhold the possibility of any meaningful visualisation of such events. He concluded that the complications caused by the light quantum's unavoidable fluctuations in time made it impossible to apply what he referred to as ***anschauliche Bilder*, or 'intuitive images'**" (Miller 1986: 173). And the consequence: "**the intuitive images remain; only the admission regarding them as such seems to have vanished.**"

**An image that is a picture of something other than its subject.** "Electron cloud pictures, for instance, represent a probability distribution of the electron in the space of the atom. These figures are **designed graphically to mimic information** about the electron's probable location. Where the clouds are most intense, the probability is high, and where they are diffuse, it is low. **But these are not pictures of electrons; they are pictures of probability.**"

**A convention of notation, which is a grating of a kind.** "in the written formulation of acoustic signals . . . there is a convention of **picturing harmony vertically**, as it emerges in the parallel layering of tones, **and melody horizontally**, unfolding in a causal sequence, **like an image of time**. A similar visual language is applied in most statistical graphs, even those designed to represent the mentioned probability distribution of particles." Musical notation and statistical graphs described as the same visual convention, and the only music material found in any of these texts.

## 3. What it gives 1.5: five etymologies, one refusal of etymology, and a measurement sentence

**The etymology row goes from five precedents to eight on this chapter alone**, and then gets its best counter-case.

| | |
|---|---|
| ***allotrios* and *kurion***, in footnotes | Aristotle's definition of metaphor as the transposition of an "alien" name: *allotrios*, "belonging to another person, belonging to others," "foreign, strange", against *kurion*, "properly," "from *kurios*, he to whom a person or thing belongs" (*Poetics* 1457b31, 1457b7) |
| ***imago*** | "The word *image* is derived from Latin *imago*, meaning 'copy' or 'likeness,' and for Roland Barthes, **the origin of the word reveals the most significant problem related to the semiology of images**", namely whether "analogical representation, the copy, can produce true systems of signs and not merely simple agglutinations of symbols" (Barthes 1977: 153) |
| ***theoria***, with *theatre* | Bohm: scientific theories "never give us a final true knowledge, but rather provide us with **an apparatus for looking at something**", since "the word *theoria* in Greek means 'contemplation,' 'speculation,' or 'looking at'". Footnote 3: "From late Latin *theoria*, from Greek *theoria*, from *theoros* 'spectator,' from *thea* 'a viewing' + *oros* 'seeing.'" **And the English word *theatre* comes from the same root**, so "scientific theory functions as a sort of **theatre of mind**". Fitje then turns on his own point: "such a statement itself relies on a spatial metaphor, one that pictures knowledge as a process moving inwards" |
| ***atom* and *proton*, the dead-etymology case** | "Terms like **atom**, derived from the Greek word for 'indivisible,' and **proton**, meaning 'first' or 'fundamental,' **remain in use although their etymological derivation has long since been proved contradictory**. . . . And, once a specific term has gone into circulation, **it becomes virtually immortal**." The counter-case the row did not have: the etymology is false and the word survives anyway |
| **the *re* in representation** | The method sentence: "**One aim of artistic research should be to emphasise the *re* in representation, acting as a reminder that knowledge is always created, never simply revealed.**" Built the way *ac/counting* and *re-turning* are built |

**And then the refusal, which is the strongest single item for that row because it is a physicist deliberately avoiding etymology:**

> "In 1963, when American physicist **Murray Gell-Mann** was to propose a name for a material constituent yet to be discovered, he started with a sound. The sound he had in mind was something like /ˈkwɔrk/, **mainly because it meant nothing and thus would never turn obsolete. To avoid the loss of meaning associated with Greek and Latin terms, Gell-Mann wanted to make one that was purely rooted in phonetics.** But he was unsure of how to spell it. Then, while leafing through James Joyce's *Finnegans Wake* he came across the word *quark* in a phrase, and decided to use that spelling."

Fitje prints the Joyce stanza ("Three quarks for Muster Mark!", Joyce 1975: 383). Set beside the *atom* and *proton* case, the pair makes the argument: a name built from a meaning dies when the meaning is disproved and goes on being used; **a name built from a sound cannot die that way.** Alongside it, **dark matter**, named *dunkle Materie* by Fritz Zwicky in 1933 after he estimated that galaxy clusters must hold more mass than was visible, replacing the earlier term **missing mass** once the mass was argued to be physically there.

**An eighth instance of measurement producing its object**, and it is about edges, which puts it in the skin and boundary lane as well:

> "To determine the position of an atom, observers agitate the atom so severely that its location only an instant later will be completely unknown. **This is the contradiction of any measurement: to measure precisely we first need to define the edges of what we are measuring, and to do this we first need to measure.** As matter today is defined, somewhat loosely, as molecules in motion, **these edges are elusively unstable.**"

With it, the plainest statement of operationalism in any held text: "**all physics does is to provide recipes for measuring. None of its concepts are really ever defined, only in terms of what is called *operational definitions*, the bare essentials of what is needed for conducting a specific measurement.** To build a rocket and travel to outer space, we do not need to define space, only give a prescription for how to measure it", and Jones's line that this "may make it possible for us to navigate in it, but **it does not tell us what space is**" (Jones 1982: 16). He notes the asymmetry with the humanities, where "each term and concept needs to be meticulously defined for the research to be regarded scientifically valid", while "**the department of particle physics, with all its emphasis on accuracy and precision, operates without defining many of its central concepts, such as space and matter**."

**He makes the Heisenberg-against-Bohr distinction the plan already values**, from the art side: "**This observer effect is, however, not to be confused with the uncertainty principle**, because the latter has proven to be inherent in all wave-like systems. Thus, it does not describe a situation in which an observer takes part but **any interaction between classical objects and quantum objects**." And he quotes the Stanford Encyclopedia on where the real disagreement lies: "**Do Heisenberg's relations express restrictions on the experiments we can perform on quantum systems, and, therefore, restrictions on the information we can gather about such systems; or do they express restrictions on the meaning of the concepts we use to describe quantum systems?**" (Hilgevoord and Uffink 2016).

**And a Bohr sentence that belongs in 2.6 and in the art-and-physics layer**, from a letter to Heisenberg:

> "**When it comes to atoms, language can be used only as in poetry. The poet, too, is not nearly so concerned with describing facts as with creating images.**" (quoted in Bronowski 2011: 256)

With Fitje's guard against reading it as resignation: "such statements were not purely made in desperation. **Accepting the imperfections of human knowledge is not simply a matter of reaching for second-best solutions; it is what the quantum world demands from us.**"

## 4. What it gives 2.2: a programme for artistic research, and the reproducibility objection answered with numbers

**The programme**, which is the clearest statement of what an artistic researcher does with scientific data found anywhere in this folder:

> "**transpositions are always needed to make scientific data readable.** However, **where physicists tend to cover up these processes by minimising their impact on measures and results, artistic researchers may instead want to accentuate the erratic nature of the data.** In this respect, the notion of transposition joins a growing tradition that engages with scientific data, **not necessarily to arrange or resolve anything, but to reveal and exhibit its natural obscurity**, as if by looking obliquely at Bohm's theatre."

> "These activities do not necessarily prescribe recipes but can **allow the transposition of each recipe, the metaphorical movement itself, to be the focal point**."

**The reproducibility objection, named as the standing objection to artistic research and then answered empirically.** The plan's 2.5 cluster already holds "reproducibility itself" as an instance of measurement producing its object, by way of Kirby. This is the version with numbers:

> "The criterion of reproducibility has been the cornerstone of the scientific method since the seventeenth century. **It has also formed the basis of a recurring criticism against artistic research in recent years.** . . . Looking closer though, reproducibility emerges as a more problematic principle than what is usually declared. A recent survey of **more than 1,500 researchers conducted by *Nature*** showed that **more than 70 per cent of the participants had tried and failed to reproduce another scientist's experiments, and more than 50 per cent had failed to reproduce their own** (Baker 2016). Half those researchers surveyed agreed to the claim that we are seeing a crisis of reproducibility, but far fewer found that the published results in their field were in any way untrustworthy. **Most confident in their published literature were the physicists.**"

And then the stronger point, that in particle physics the norm does not apply in the first place: at **COMPASS**, the CERN case study he uses, "scattered traces of muons are observed by several detectors, interpreting the 'path' of the particles as they travel through the collider. Since their whereabouts in between the different detectors are theoretically uncertain, **the path, stretched out in classical space and time, functions as a form of transposed probability.** . . . **experimentalists will never be able to reproduce any one of the incidents occurring**, and consequently they will need millions of events for the data to become useful. The set-up for the experiment is certainly reproducible, as is the equipment for interpretation and the structuring of the data. **But the particular results may never manifest themselves again.**" His verdict on the standard: it "**in time may turn out to be an outdated norm even for the natural sciences**".

**The concealment of failure, on both sides**, which bears on the first-person lane and on practice-as-research:

> "Many scientists strive to remove all traces of trial and error when presenting ideas and achievements to others. **Artists and writers may do this too, by concealing their failures, even destroying sketches and studies, releasing only the finished work.** Many creators want to exhibit a polished surface for others to admire **without leaving any evidence of the personal struggle that got them there** . . . Modern physics, or at least its popular offspring, reflects an attempt to convince people that the theories presented are objective **by eliminating all traces of the human minds that produced them**."

**And the art-and-science claim, with two names in one sentence:**

> "There is a popular metaphor claiming that **science is pure analysis, like taking the rainbow to pieces, while art is pure synthesis, like putting it back together**. Modern physics, as much as modern art, has proven this to be an inaccurate distinction. **Pablo Picasso took the world to pieces while Niels Bohr puzzled it back together.** In doing so, however, neither of them relied solely on one of these generalised attributes. **Discovery requires both imagination and examination.**"

> "**There is an underlying affinity between the natural sciences and the fine arts, one that transcends the binary oppositions of hard and soft, brain and heart, analysis and synthesis, logos and mythos.** The standout achievement of modern physics has been neither the exploration of galaxy clusters nor the probing of subatomic particles but **the disclosure of the shortcomings of the human mind**."

The *mythos* and *logos* division he is working against is attributed to Jones: "While dream, myth, literature, and art, in Jones's view are relegated to the realm of imagination and subjectivity, what philosophers often refer to as ***mythos***, science is celebrated as the discipline that deals with ***logos***; what can be controlled, measured, and predicted; **what we can know without considering that we are fallible**." Against which, after Bohr and Heisenberg, "physics undoubtedly became a discipline concerned with **what we can know although we are fallible**".

## 5. What it gives 2.5: transposition defined against metaphor

This is the only place in the folder where **transposition** is defined by distinguishing it from metaphor, and the distinction is clean enough to use.

> "Perhaps **transposition could serve the cause as a more dynamic umbrella term**. This is partly because the term [metaphor] is already **stained by its long history of proposed definitions, in linguistics, semiotics, and hermeneutics**."

> "While **the metaphor relates to the whole process of relocation**, its overall narrative and the resulting implications of taking something out of its familiar sphere and imposing its current meaning onto something else, **the transposition merely refers to the movement itself. In other words, the transposition says nothing about the meaning created by the metaphor, and nothing about the initial meaning before the metaphor entered the equation.**"

> "if a metaphor can be transposed from one level to another, as mentioned earlier with the case of Rutherford's atom model, **the transposition does not cultivate two separate metaphors but rather explains the movement of one**."

And the extension that reaches the body: "**Transposition, then, could also be suited to describing the technological conversion of a signal, such as the mental translation from a nerve stimulus to a sound, or the attachment of meaning when this sound is interpreted as language.**" That is the sensory-substitution case in Chiew's chapter described in transpositional terms, from the other direction.

His closing formulation of why it matters: "**After all, movement of meaning is what discovery is all about.**"

**The transposed-metaphor case he builds this on** is worth having for 2.5 in its own right, and it is Jones's: Newton's mechanics "had been so successfully used to analyse the solar system . . . that this approach becomes **the prototype for all physical theories**. The attack was the same at all levels and the size scales whether one thought of planets revolving around an attracting sun, colliding billiard balls, or the tiny molecules of a gas bouncing against each other . . . **It was only natural that people began to believe in the reality of the models.** The particle metaphor was transposed from the macroscopic to the microscopic level, replacing, rather than rejuvenating, the ancient Greek *átomos*" (Jones 1982: 115). Rutherford's solar-system atom, Fitje adds, follows **Aristotle's rule of proportion**, "old age is related to life as evening is related to the day" (*Poetics* 1457b22).

**And an alchemical case that is still in use.** The alchemists moved properties from the large world to the small, "**for them, a volcano was an up-scaled boil, and a rainstorm a fit of weeping**", and "**these symbols are in fact still used in medicine. For the female we apply the alchemical sign for copper, everything that is soft; for the male we use the sign for iron, all that is hard.** Furthermore, these are also the signs for the planets Venus and Mars." The gender signs are surviving alchemical metaphors, which is a measurement-and-classification item as well as an art-history one.

## 7. New to get hold of, from this chapter

**Jones, R. S. (1982), *Physics as Metaphor*, University of Minnesota Press.** The book-length version of the position, and Fitje's main source. Jones's **four cardinal metaphors, space, time, matter and number** (p. 52), with the claim that the framework "is, despite how unassailable and absolute it may appear to us, a human construct emanated from the restraints of our consciousness", that "**mind and matter should not be thought of as distinct, and the metaphor . . . is ultimately inseparable from physical theory**", and the five-word version of the whole question: given that relativity makes space and time the same as matter and energy and geometry the same as gravity, "**Which is really a metaphor for which?**" (p. 5). **The first thing to get.**

**Miller, A. I. (1984/1986), *Imagery in Scientific Thought: Creating 20th-Century Physics*, MIT Press.** A whole book on scientific imagery, and the source for Bohr's 1925 refusal of *anschauliche Bilder*. **For 1.1 this is the book the section has been looking for outside the folder.**

**Ricoeur, P. (1977), *The Rule of Metaphor*.** Eight studies, and Fitje's summary of it is "a generous depiction of the metaphor as **a process of interrelating concepts, a cognitive phenomenon that should not be confined to the department of language**" (p. 307). He notes that **Ricoeur, unlike Lakoff and Reddy, does accentuate "the fertility and inventive aptitude of such operations"**, so Ricoeur is the one of the three whose account is positive.

**Barthes, R. (1964/1977), "Rhetoric of the Image"**, in *Image, Music, Text*, 32-51, for the *imago* argument at p. 153. **Bronowski, J. (1973/2011), *The Ascent of Man*** for the Bohr letter at p. 256 and the seeing-limit material at 269-270, 295. **Beveridge, W. I. B. (1957), *The Art of Scientific Investigation***, for the Kekulé snake-dream passage at p. 56, which ends "**Let us learn to dream, gentlemen**", and for its method, which is quotation from scientists' diaries and personal notes on intuition, chance and serendipity: **a first-person-evidence book about scientists, from 1957.** **MacKinnon, E. (2008), "The Standard Model as a Philosophical Challenge", *Philosophy of Science* 75(4), 447-57**, where the standard model "is not even a theory, but rather a huge collection of disjointed rules modified to accommodate experimental results", and nobody asks **Feynman's forbidden question, "what must (might) the world be like if a theory is true of it"** (pp. 448, 456). **Baker, M. (2016), "Scientists Lift the Lid on Reproducibility", *Nature*, 25 May**, free, the 1,500-researcher survey. **Oerter, R. (2006), *The Theory of Almost Everything*** for the ice-skater argument at p. 95. **Reddy, M. (1993), "The Contemporary Theory of Metaphor"** and **Lakoff, G. (1993)**, both in Ortony (ed.), *Metaphor and Thought*, 2nd ed., for the conduit metaphor at pp. 282-286. **Hilgevoord and Uffink (2016), "The Uncertainty Principle"**, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, free. **Born and Wolf (1980/1997), *Principles of Optics***, p. 63, for the Abbe diffraction limit. And the **Heisenberg 1971 CBC Radio interview**, "Physics and Beyond", which Fitje cites as a YouTube video.
