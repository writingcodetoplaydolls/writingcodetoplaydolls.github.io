## 2. Chapter 3. Florence Chiew, "Sensory Substitution: The Plasticity of the Eye/I", pp. 48-69

The chapter asked for, and the strongest single chapter in the folder for the camera thread.

### What it is about

Cross-modal plasticity, read through prominent neuroscience case studies, with two aims: to question deficiency understood as a lack of normal function, and to argue that the malleability of sensory function evokes what she calls an eco-logic of sensibility, which lets the plasticity of the eye be thought as the plasticity of the "I".

### The camera that writes to the skin

**Tactile-visual sensory substitution (TVSS)**, invented by the neuroscientist Paul Bach-y-Rita. A blind person wears a device that converts visual images taken by a **mobile camera** into vibrating tactile stimulators placed on parts of the skin, the back or the abdomen. More recent designs use a head-mounted camera and a tongue display unit: a video cable feeds electrode arrays in the mouth, which convert the image into sequences of pulse patterns.

With training, users report the sensation changing its modal quality and its origin: it moves from a sensation of touch on the patch of skin receiving stimulation to the visual experience of images projected in front of them (Bach-y-Rita and Kercel 2003: 543). Within a short time the device is integrated into the user's corporeal schema, which Chiew says challenges both the boundaries of the body and of biology.

Also from the same source, on the walking cane as transducer: experience is "externalized to the point of contact between the object and the cane" (2003: 542).

**Why this matters here.** For 1.1 this is a camera whose output arrives on skin, and whose user relocates the image away from the skin to in front of the body. It is the inverse of the event camera in `NEUROMORPHIC_CAMERA_lens.md`: that one records only change and is blind to a still body, this one writes a continuous image onto a still body. For 1.5 it is an apparatus that visibly redraws the boundary of the body, and the redrawing is reported in the first person by the person wearing it. And it is first-person disabled testimony about a camera, which is what Heiredal and Manning's masked Hasselblad was wanted for.

### Merleau-Ponty, and a page map of *The Visible and the Invisible*

Chiew's section "Merleau-Ponty and the Sensible Universe" works from the late, unfinished book, principally the essay "The Intertwining, The Chiasm". The chapter gives enough page references to go straight to the passages:

| Page | What is there |
|---|---|

| **1968: 136** | "we are the world that thinks itself", and the world "at the heart of our flesh" |
| **1968: 137** | The body participates in things because it is of their family, visible and tangible itself; the body belongs to the order of things "as the world is universal flesh" |
| **1968: 141** | **intercorporeity** |

| **[1958] 2006: 94** | *Phenomenology of Perception*, on phantom limbs. "To have a phantom arm is to remain open to all the actions of which the arm alone is capable" |
| **1968: 102 and 149** | From the 2011 monograph, chapter 6, not this chapter: the porous being that a question is consonant with, and "a being by porosity, pregnancy, or generality" |

**Chiasm**: from the Greek *khiasma*, a placing crosswise. The body as at once sentient and sensible, and the flesh of the body as the flesh of the world, reversible.

Chiew also cites Kirby's monograph at **2011: 119**, inside the Irigaray and Merleau-Ponty chapter, for the claim that Merleau-Ponty's phenomenology is so unqualifiedly open to the world that even closure is intrinsic to it.

### Superposition: a fourth reading, and this one is a refusal

The reading plan now holds three readings of the term. **This chapter is a fourth, and it is the only one that says no.**

Chiew argues that understanding sensory substitution as a kind of "superposing" is something Merleau-Ponty would likely disagree with, because it neglects the complexity of time and space in suggesting that touch and vision can simply be overlapped, placed before, above, or in the place occupied by the other. Merleau-Ponty's own words at 1968: 134 are that the two parts "are not superposable". Chiew's alternative is to call cross-modal plasticity an **intra-modal plasticity**, which is not a union of two or more discrete modalities.

So: Manning's superposition as Whiteheadian mutual inclusion, Colman's as overlapping states converting Fraser's two lenses into one, Merleau-Ponty's as the thing the senses specifically cannot do, and Chiew's substitution of intra-modal for cross-modal in order to avoid it. Four sources, four jobs, one of them a refusal, each with a traceable genealogy. Note also that Chiew's **intra-modal** is formed the way Barad's **intra-action** is, and the collection uses intra-action 27 times, so the two words are in the same book.

### Disability, and an etymological argument

Merleau-Ponty's treatment of the phantom limb is read as a reinterpretation of disability as an expression of life's tenacity, not as lack: losing a limb or a modality does not mean the person no longer has the arm or the sight, because the flesh of the body is an intercorporeity. Chiew pairs this with Weeks and Tsao (2010: 464) on phantom limbs, which she glosses as an "intra-social limb" that calls into question the containment of the body as an atomic entity separate from its environment.

Then an **etymology**. Deficiency, in its root, does not mean lack. From Latin *de*, away from, down, aside, and also the undoing or reversing of a verb's action, plus *facere*, to do or make. *Defacere* as an unmaking rather than a lack (citing Barnhart 2006). Her point is that undoing is not failure, because plasticity is precisely life making and unmaking itself.

**This is the same move the proposal makes on *agencement* and on stim** (see `STIM_etymology_and_root.md`). A published precedent, in a Braidotti and van der Tuin series, for running an etymological argument on a term as the hinge of a chapter.

She closes on "chiasmatic plasticity", and on the perceiver as the world's experience of itself.

## 3. Chapter 4. Michelle Jamieson, "Allergy as the Puzzle of Causality", pp. 70-89: a grating printed on skin

Not asked for, but it is the second place the skin count concentrates, and it is the closest thing in the folder to an apparatus that measures permeability.

The chapter works on the **skin prick test**. A grid is printed on the patient's skin. Drops of glycerinated allergen extract are placed on the skin at the centre of each square, and a prick is made through each drop. The result is read as a series of red welts, a wheal and flare response. Jamieson's reading: the grid printed on the skin numerically and rationally organises the response, so the test provides a precise visual and temporal representation, and it compartmentalises by a logic of separability which "manufactures" the circumstances it then reads. She argues the skin prick test demonstrates the impossibility of the very distinction it is built on, between the biological and the environmental.

**For 1.5 and for the permeability material this is the find.** The comps definition has a grating co-created with its material. The skin prick test is a grid drawn directly onto skin in order to make the skin's permeability legible and countable, and it produces the response it measures. Set beside the 500-dalton rule in `PERMEABLE_BODIES_research_notes.md`, which is the question of what crosses intact skin, this is the clinical instrument for that question, and a critical reading of it already exists.

## 4. Chapter 9. Astrida Neimanis, "Nature Represents Itself", pp. 179-198

Neimanis wrote *Bodies of Water: Posthuman Feminist Phenomenology*, which is the standing book on bodily permeability, and she is in this collection.

Her chapter asks whether there can be a posthuman representation without representationalism. She reads Barad on representationalism and the ontological cut, then takes up Alaimo and Catriona Sandilands, both of whom insist on still asking after the representability of nature. Sandilands (1999: 180): any claim to speak of or for nonhuman nature is to some extent a misrepresentation, yet the perpetual failure of representation does not absolve ecofeminist politics of producing alternative conversations. Alaimo (**2010: 23**, which is *Bodily Natures*) on environmental politics demanding we speak for nature because of, not in spite of, the impossibility.

Her conclusion, which is worth having in full from the book: the failure is not a problem to be overcome but a crucial part of a radical politics whose promise lies in its unfinishedness.

She then turns to Kirby's monograph and the question "What If Culture Was Really Nature All Along?" as offering that possibility.
