## 1. The preface, which is about losing the subject

The first paragraph is the most directly usable page in the book for anyone whose material will not hold still, and it is worth having in full:

> "I began writing this book by trying to consider the materiality of the body only to find that **the thought of materiality invariably moved me into other domains. I tried to discipline myself to stay on the subject, but found that I could not fix bodies as simple objects of thought.** Not only did bodies tend to indicate a world beyond themselves, but **this movement beyond their own boundaries, a movement of boundary itself, appeared to be quite central to what bodies 'are.' I kept losing track of the subject. I proved resistant to discipline. Inevitably, I began to consider that perhaps this resistance to fixing the subject was essential to the matter at hand.**"

**She then reports the question that was actually put to her, including the name she was called:**

> "in the recent past, the question was repeatedly formulated to me this way: '**What about the materiality of the body, Judy?**' I took it that the addition of 'Judy' was an effort to dislodge me from the more formal 'Judith' and to recall me to a bodily life that could not be theorized away. There was a certain exasperation in the delivery of that final diminutive, a certain patronizing quality which **(re)constituted me as an unruly child**, one who needed to be brought to task."

**And the concession the whole book turns on**, made before any argument is made:

> "For surely bodies live and die; eat and sleep; feel pain, pleasure; endure illness and violence; and these 'facts,' one might skeptically proclaim, cannot be dismissed as mere construction . . . **And surely there is.** But their irrefutability in no way implies what it might mean to affirm them and through what discursive means."

> "**What are we to make of constructions without which we would not be able to think, to live, to make sense at all, those which have acquired for us a kind of necessity?** . . . Thinking the body as constructed demands a rethinking of the meaning of construction itself."

She closes by refusing the programmatic: "this text is not intended to be programmatic. And yet, as an attempt to clarify my 'intentions,' it appears destined to produce a new set of misapprehensions. **I hope that they prove, at least, to be productive ones.**"

## 2. The introduction: five claims, and what performativity is not

The book's own numbered list of what is at stake, abbreviated: **(1)** the matter of bodies recast as the effect of a dynamic of power; **(2)** performativity understood "not as the act by which a subject brings into being what she/he names, but, rather, as **that reiterative power of discourse to produce the phenomena that it regulates and constrains**"; **(3)** "sex" as a cultural norm governing materialization rather than a bodily given; **(4)** assuming a sex as a process not undergone by a subject but through which the speaking "I" is formed; **(5)** that process linked to identification and to which identifications the heterosexual imperative forecloses.

**The terms worth lifting:**

| | |
|---|---|
| **"sex" as regulatory ideal** | "'sex' is an ideal construct which is **forcibly materialized through time**. It is not a simple fact or static condition of a body, but a process whereby regulatory norms materialize 'sex'" |
| **why reiteration matters** | "**That this reiteration is necessary is a sign that materialization is never quite complete, that bodies never quite comply with the norms by which their materialization is impelled**" |
| **the abject** | "those 'unlivable' and 'uninhabitable' zones of social life which are nevertheless **densely populated by those who do not enjoy the status of the subject**, but whose living under the sign of the 'unlivable' is required to circumscribe the domain of the subject" |
| **the constitutive outside** | "there is an 'outside' to what is constructed by discourse, but this is not an absolute 'outside,' an ontological there-ness that exceeds or counters the boundaries of discourse; as a constitutive 'outside,' it is that which can only be thought, when it can, **in relation to that discourse, at and as its most tenuous borders**" |
| **the refusal of linguisticism** | "**the point has never been that 'everything is discursively constructed'**; that point, when and where it is made, belongs to a kind of discursive monism or linguisticism that refuses the constitutive force of exclusion" |
| **the sentence to keep** | "**In philosophical terms, the constative claim is always to some degree performative.**" |
| **matter as process** | "a return to the notion of matter, not as site or surface, but as **a process of materialization that stabilizes over time to produce the effect of boundary, fixity, and surface we call matter**" |
| **power without a subject** | "**There is no power that acts, but only a reiterated acting that is power in its persistence and instability.**" |
| **the girling of the girl** | the medical interpellation that "shifts an infant from an 'it' to a 'she' or a 'he,' and in that naming, the girl is 'girled'"; and "**that 'girling' of the girl does not end there** . . . that founding interpellation is reiterated by various authorities and throughout various intervals of time" |

**She also pre-empts the two standard objections in their own words**, which is useful as a way of handling an objection: "If everything is discourse, what about the body?" and "If gender is constructed, then who is doing the constructing?"; and her answer to the second is grammatical: "**it takes a certain suspicion toward grammar to reconceive the matter in a different light.**"

**And a limit she sets on her own book, stated as a limit:** "any analysis which pretends to be able to encompass every vector of power runs the risk of a certain epistemological imperialism . . . **No author or text can offer such a reflection of the world, and those who claim to offer such pictures become suspect by virtue of that very claim.**" Followed by: "**it may be precisely the partiality of a text which conditions the radical character of its insights.**"

## 3. Chapter 1: the matter of matter, and Irigaray's catachresis

**The etymological finding that the chapter runs on**, which is also the pun in the book's title:

> "The classical association of femininity with materiality can be traced to a set of etymologies which link **matter with *mater* and *matrix* (or the womb)** . . . In Greek, **hyle is the wood or timber** out of which various cultural constructions are made, but also a principle of origin, development, and teleology . . . *Materia* in Latin denotes the stuff out of which things are made, **not only the timber for houses and ships but whatever serves as nourishment for infants**."

> "to be material means to materialize, where the principle of that materialization is precisely what 'matters' about that body, its very intelligibility. In this sense, **to know the significance of something is to know how and why it matters, where 'to matter' means at once 'to materialize' and 'to mean.'**"

**Aristotle supplies the word she keeps**: *schema*, which in the Greek carries "form, shape, figure, appearance, dress, gesture, figure of a syllogism, and grammatical form", so that "**if matter never appears without its schema**, that means that it only appears under a certain grammatical form". **Foucault supplies the historicized version of it**: in *Discipline and Punish* the soul is "an historically specific imaginary ideal . . . under which the body is effectively materialized", and the prison "is materialized to the extent that it is invested with power". Her correction to Foucault is the chapter's hinge: "we need to ask **what constrains the domain of what is materializable** . . . Does Foucault's effort . . . fail to account for not only what is excluded from the economies of discursive intelligibility that he describes, but **what has to be excluded for those economies to function as self-sustaining systems**?"

**The catachresis passage, in place at last** (the companion file read it as a window):

> "**No wonder then that the feminine appears for Irigaray only in catachresis, that is, in those figures that function improperly, as an improper transfer of sense, the use of a proper name to describe that which does not properly belong to it, and that return to haunt and coopt the very language from which the feminine is excluded.** This explains in part the radical citational practice of Irigaray, **the catachrestic usurpation of the 'proper' for fully improper purposes**."

And the reason it is catachresis rather than metaphor: for Irigaray "one cannot interpret the philosophical relation to the feminine through the figures that philosophy provides, but, rather . . . **through siting the feminine as the unspeakable condition of figuration**, as that which, in fact, can never be figured within the terms of philosophy proper, but whose exclusion from that propriety is its enabling condition." Jane Gallop is credited for the method: her reading of Irigaray's two lips "as both synecdoche and catachresis, a reading which offers an interpretation of Irigaray's language of biological essentialism as rhetorical strategy."

**On the *chora*** she does the Greek herself, with the dictionary senses: *dechesthai*, "to receive, take, accept, welcome, include, and even comprehend"; *eisienai*, which "denotes a going toward or into, an approach and penetration", and also, she notes, "**being brought into court**, i.e., subject to public norms, and '**coming into mind**' or 'beginning to think'"; and the prohibition on resemblance, which Plato breaches in the act of naming: "**Plato posits that which he claims cannot be posited. And he further contradicts himself when he claims that that which cannot be posited ought to be posited in only one way.**"

**She names the limits of her own method as she uses it**, which is worth noting as a model: "The largeness and speculative character of Irigaray's claims have always put me a bit on edge, and I confess in advance that although I can think of no feminist who has read and reread the history of philosophy with the kind of detailed and critical attention that she has, **her terms tend to mime the grandiosity of the philosophical errors that she underscores**"; and of her own: "To the extent that I replicate that speculative excess here, I apologize, **but only half-heartedly, for sometimes a hyperbolic rejoinder is necessary when a given injury has remained unspoken for too long**."

**Then she turns on Irigaray**, and this is the chapter's own best paragraph:

> "There are good reasons, however, to reject the notion that the feminine monopolizes the sphere of the excluded here . . . **Plato's scenography of intelligibility depends on the exclusion of women, slaves, children, and animals**, where slaves are characterized as those who do not speak his language . . . **Irigaray does not always help matters here, for she fails to follow through the metonymic link between women and these other Others** . . . But what is the 'elsewhere' of Irigaray's 'elsewhere'?"

**And the warning against making exclusion into a mood**, which is the most transferable sentence in the chapter:

> "whereas this can appear as the necessary and founding violence of any truth-regime, **it is important to resist that theoretical gesture of pathos in which exclusions are simply affirmed as sad necessities of signification**. The task is to refigure this necessary 'outside' as a future horizon, one in which the violence of exclusion is perpetually in the process of being overcome. **But of equal importance is the preservation of the outside**, the site where discourse meets its limits . . . **radical and inclusive representability is not precisely the goal**."

**Her stated reason for not simply affirming matter and not simply negating it**: "To call a presupposition into question is not the same as doing away with it; rather, **it is to free it from its metaphysical lodgings** in order to understand what political interests were secured in and by that metaphysical placing . . . **a loss of certainty is not the same as political nihilism**."

## 4. Chapter 2: the phallus as a transferable property

The chapter is a close read of Freud's "On Narcissism" (1914) for the point at which his examples get away from him, and its first move is to take pain seriously as the thing that produces a body part:

> Freud, quoted from *The Ego and the Id*: "**Pain seems to play a part in the process, and the way in which we gain new knowledge of our organs during painful illnesses is perhaps a model of the way by which in general we arrive at the idea of our own body.**"

> and: "**The ego is first and foremost a bodily ego; it is not merely a surface entity, but is itself the projection of a surface.**"

**The figure she builds the reading on is a toothache**, from a line of Wilhelm Busch verse that Freud quotes: "concentrated is his soul . . . in his molar's [jaw-tooth's] aching hole". Butler reads the figure as a collision: "**a punctured instrument of penetration, an inverted vagina dentata, anus, mouth, orifice in general, the spectre of the penetrating instrument penetrated** . . . This wounded instrument of penetration can only suffer under the ideal of its own invulnerability."

**The contradiction she catches Freud in**, stated plainly: the male genitals appear first as the cumulative example of body parts made knowable by investment, and then as "**an originary site of erotogenization**" for which other parts substitute; "In the first case, they are the effect and sum of a set of substitutions, and in the second, they are an origin for which substitutions exist." And then Freud's own concession undoes both: "**We can decide to regard erotogenicity as a general characteristic of all organs**", which Butler takes at its word: "**To be a property of all organs is to be a property necessary to no organ**, a property defined by its very plasticity, transferability, and expropriability."

**On Lacan the method is the same**: she sets "The Mirror Stage" (1949) beside "The Signification of the Phallus" (1958) and asks why the second denies what the first requires. Lacan's denial, quoted: "the phallus is not a fantasy . . . Nor is it an object . . . **It is even less the organ, penis or clitoris, which it symbolizes.**" Her reading of that sentence is grammatical and it is the chapter's sharpest passage: "**The phallus symbolizes the penis; and insofar as it symbolizes the penis, retains the penis as that which it symbolizes; it is not the penis** . . . Symbolization depletes that which is symbolized of its ontological connection with the symbol itself", and therefore "if the phallus must negate the penis in order to symbolize and signify in its privileged way, then **the phallus is bound to the penis, not through simple identity, but through determinate negation** . . . **the phallus would be nothing without the penis**."

**What follows is her list**, which is the thing the chapter is remembered for:

> "**'having' the phallus can be symbolized by an arm, a tongue, a hand (or two), a knee, a thigh, a pelvic bone, an array of purposefully instrumentalized body-like things.**"

**And the two sentences on the body and the mirror that are the chapter's conclusion**, which bear directly on anything about reflection, surface or image:

> "**The body in the mirror does not represent a body that is, as it were, before the mirror: the mirror, even as it is instigated by that unrepresentable body 'before' the mirror, produces that body as its delirious effect, a delirium, by the way, which we are compelled to live.**"

> Lacan, quoted as an epigraph to the chapter's last section: "**Consciousness occurs each time there is a surface such that it can produce what is called an image. That is a materialist definition.**"

She is explicit that she is not offering a new body part: "what is needed is not a new body part, as it were, but **a displacement of the hegemonic symbolic** of (heterosexist) sexual difference and **the critical release of alternative imaginary schemas for constituting sites of erotogenic pleasure**." And she names the chapter's own failure in its first line: "**I knew that I could not possibly offer a satisfying essay** . . . I would like, then, to acknowledge that failure from the start and to work that failure for its uses."

## 5. Chapter 2's answer on language and materiality

This section of chapter 2 ("Are Bodies Purely Discursive?") is **Butler's own position on the question the metaphor files were built around**, and it is not the same as any other candidate in this folder. It should be read beside `METAPHOR_and_the_real_search2.md` and beside section 4 of `NEIMANIS_2017_ch2_ch4_read_2026-11-02.md`.

> "**To posit a materiality outside of language is still to posit that materiality, and the materiality so posited will retain that positing as its constitutive condition.**"

> "it is not that one cannot get outside of language in order to grasp materiality in and of itself; rather, **every effort to refer to materiality takes place through a signifying process which, in its phenomenality, is always already material**. In this sense, then, **language and materiality are not opposed, for language both is and refers to that which is material, and what is material never fully escapes from the process by which it is signified.**"

> "**But if language is not opposed to materiality, neither can materiality be summarily collapsed into an identity with language.**"

> "**Language and materiality are fully embedded in each other, chiasmic in their interdependence but never fully collapsed into one another** . . . Always already implicated in each other, always already exceeding one another, **language and materiality are never fully identical nor fully different.**" (She compares this to **Merleau-Ponty's flesh of the world.**)

> On the signifier's own matter: "**what allows for a signifier to signify will never be its materiality alone** . . . The materiality of the signifier will signify only to the extent that it is **impure, contaminated by the ideality of differentiating relations** . . . Conversely, the signifier will work to the extent that it is also contaminated constitutively by the very materiality that the ideality of sense purports to overcome."

> And her replacement for "the materiality of the body": "**what persists here is a demand in and for language, a 'that which' which prompts and occasions** . . . not the blank slate or passive medium upon which the psyche acts, but, rather, **the constitutive demand that mobilizes psychic action from the start**."

**She also concedes the list**, explicitly: "It must be possible to concede and affirm **an array of 'materialities' that pertain to the body**, that which is signified by the domains of biology, anatomy, physiology, hormonal and chemical composition, illness, age, weight, metabolism, life and death. **None of this can be denied.** But the undeniability of these 'materialities' in no way implies what it means to affirm them."

## 6. Chapter 3: constraint as the condition, not the limit

The chapter's single most quotable move, and the correction of the commonest misreading of her:

> "**it is not only that there are constraints to performativity; rather, constraint calls to be rethought as the very condition of performativity. Performativity is neither free play nor theatrical self-presentation; nor can it be simply equated with performance.** Moreover, **constraint is not necessarily that which sets a limit to performativity; constraint is, rather, that which impels and sustains performativity.**"

**And she lists what the constraints actually are**, in a sentence that refuses to make construction sound easy: "in the domain of sexuality these constraints include **the radical unthinkability of desiring otherwise, the radical unendurability of desiring otherwise, the absence of certain desires, the repetitive compulsion of others, the abiding repudiation of some sexual possibilities, panic, obsessional pull, and the nexus of sexuality and pain**." Plus: "**A construction is, after all, not the same as an artifice.**"

**The law as citation**, which is the argument she will use again in chapters 7 and 8, worked out here through the figure of a judge: "As one who efficaciously speaks in the name of the law, **the judge does not originate the law or its authority; rather, he 'cites' the law** . . . And yet the already existing law that he cites, from where does that law draw its authority? . . . **it is precisely through the infinite deferral of authority to an irrecoverable past that authority itself is constituted** . . . **The pointing to a ground which is never recovered becomes authority's groundless ground.**" And therefore: "**Since the law must be repeated to remain an authoritative law, the law perpetually reinstitutes the possibility of its own failure.**"

**On identification she says what it never is:** "it never can be said to have taken place; **identification does not belong to the world of events** . . . identification is **the phantasmatic staging of the event** . . . Identifications are never fully and finally made; they are incessantly reconstituted."

**And the political argument the chapter ends with**, which is about the cost of coherence:

> "**a radical refusal to identify with a given position suggests that on some level an identification has already taken place**, an identification that is made and disavowed."

> "**it may be only by risking the incoherence of identity that connection is possible.**"

> "The question here concerns **the tacit cruelties that sustain coherent identity, cruelties that include self-cruelty as well** . . . if identity is constructed through opposition, it is also constructed through rejection. **It may be that if a lesbian opposes heterosexuality absolutely, she may find herself more in its power** than a straight or bisexual woman who knows or lives its constitutive instability."

> "**the pluralist theoretical separation of these terms as 'categories' or indeed as 'positions' is itself based on exclusionary operations** that attribute a false uniformity to them and that serve the regulatory aims of the liberal state."

> And the questions she substitutes for the list: "**How is race lived in the modality of sexuality? How is gender lived in the modality of race?**", at Gloria Anzaldúa's **crossroads**, "a crossroads that is not a subject, but, rather, **the unfulfillable demand to rework convergent signifiers in and through each other**."

She also includes her own earlier work in the charge: "**some feminist positions, including my own, have problematically prioritized gender as the identificatory site of political mobilization at the expense of race or sexuality or class or geopolitical positioning/displacement.**"

## 7. Chapter 4: the correction to the drag reading, and the camera

**The retraction is explicit and it is in the chapter's second section:**

> "Although many readers understood *Gender Trouble* to be arguing for the proliferation of drag performances as a way of subverting dominant gender norms, I want to underscore that **there is no necessary relation between drag and subversion**, and that **drag may well be used in the service of both the denaturalization and reidealization of hyperbolic heterosexual gender norms**. At best, it seems, drag is a site of a certain ambivalence."

She separates the kinds: *Victor/Victoria*, *Tootsie*, *Some Like It Hot* are "**drag as high het entertainment**", which she "would be reticent to call . . . subversive", since they provide "a ritualistic release for a heterosexual economy that must constantly police its own boundaries". And she answers the Frye/Raymond/hooks charge of misogyny with a structural point rather than a denial: that argument "**figures male-to-female transsexuality, cross-dressing, and drag as male homosexual activities, which they are not always**", and "makes male homosexuality about women", so that "at its extreme, this kind of analysis is in fact **a colonization in reverse**".

**"Realness" is defined by its unreadability**, which is a precise and portable formulation: "**this is a performance that works, that effects realness, to the extent that it cannot be read.** For 'reading' means taking someone down, exposing what fails to work at the level of appearance . . . **when what appears and how it is 'read' diverge, the artifice of the performance can be read as artifice; the ideal splits off from its appropriation.**"

**The chapter's weight is Venus Xtravaganza**, and Butler refuses the symmetry: "**There is passing and then there is passing, and it is, as we used to say, 'no accident' that Willi Ninja ascends and Venus Xtravaganza dies.**" Her reading of the death: "**this is a killing that is performed by a symbolic that would eradicate those phenomena that require an opening up of the possibilities for the resignification of sex** . . . If Venus wants to become a woman, and cannot overcome being a Latina, then **Venus is treated by the symbolic in precisely the ways in which women of color are treated**." And the general conclusion: "**the denaturalization of sex, in its multiple senses, does not imply a liberation from hegemonic constraint.**"

**On the film's own politics she quotes bell hooks at length** rather than summarising her, including "Livingston does not oppose the way hegemonic whiteness 'represents' blackness, but rather assumes **an imperial overseeing position that is in no way progressive or counterhegemonic**", and hooks's observation that "**Such a stance obscures the substantial rewards she has received for this work.**" **Butler then names her own position inside the charge**, in a parenthesis: Livingston, "a white lesbian (in other contexts called '**a white Jewish lesbian from Yale**,' an interpellation which **also implicates this author in its sweep**)".

**Her own addition to hooks is the camera as an instrument**: "**the camera acts as surgical instrument and operation, the vehicle through which the transubstantiation occurs** . . . Livingston thus becomes the one with the power to turn men into women who, then, depend on the power of her gaze to become and remain women." And the question she says she wanted the film to ask of itself: "**I would have liked to have seen the question of Livingston's cinematic desire reflexively thematized in the film itself, her intrusions into the frame as 'intrusions.'**"

**And what she finds that hooks's two-trajectory reading leaves out is the kinship**, which is the part of the chapter that has outlasted the rest:

> "**These men 'mother' one another, 'house' one another, 'rear' one another, and the resignification of the family through these terms is not a vain or useless imitation, but the social and discursive building of community, a community that binds, cares, and teaches, that shelters and enables.**"

> "*Paris Is Burning* documents **neither an efficacious insurrection nor a painful resubordination, but an unstable coexistence of both.**"

## 8. Chapters 5 and 6: two literary readings, and what the name does

**Cather (ch. 5)** is read for the name as a site of transfer rather than of identity. The method is stated against two biographers and against Sedgwick: "**The postulation of an original 'truth' of lesbian sexuality which awaits its adequate historical representation presumes an ahistorical sexuality constituted and intact prior to the discourses by which it is represented**", and in its place: "**It is perhaps less that the legibility of lesbianism is perpetually endangered in Cather's text than that lesbian sexuality within the text is produced as a perpetual challenge to legibility**", quoting Adrienne Rich: ". . . for Willa Cather, lesbian, the marker is mute."

The readings themselves are patient and verbal. In *My Ántonia* the anonymous narrating "I" of the introduction is handed over to Jim Burden, who then writes the book's own title on his portfolio, which Butler reads as "**a fraudulent gift, an apparent sacrifice, in which a feminine authorship appears to yield in favor of a masculine one**"; Ántonia's repeated "Name? What Name?" becomes "**a figure for an unmasterable excess produced by the conceits of nomination**"; and the snake in the gravel bed, lying "in loose waves, like a letter 'W'", is read as the truncated **Willa**, "the undulating movements of writing and, in particular, the winding, doubling, and falling back on itself that constitutes that abbreviated token of her signature". In "Tommy the Unsentimental" she gives the whole lexical history of "Tom" and "tomboy" from the OED (including Tommy shops, where "wages for labor were paid in goods rather than money") and reads Tommy's signing in her absent father's place as "**not a simple identificatory loyalty of daughter to father, but an aggressive appropriation as well**". The phrase she builds the chapter's ending on is one of the Old Boys' lines, "**the blindness that may not be cured**", which she sets a year after the Wilde trial against "the love that dare not speak its name" and reads for its one word of difference: "**This is a blindness that may or may not be cured.**" And the general claim: "**substitutability is a condition for this sexuality**", so that the prohibition "is, I would argue, precisely the occasion of its constitution and exchange."

**Larsen (ch. 6)** is the chapter in which the book's argument about race is actually made rather than asserted.

> "**to claim that sexual difference is more fundamental than racial difference is effectively to assume that sexual difference is white sexual difference, and that whiteness is not a form of racial difference.**"

**Her reading of the word "queering" in Larsen's 1929 novella is the chapter's best piece of close work**, and she does the lexicography first: in 1929 "queer" "did not yet mean homosexual, but it did encompass an array of meanings associated with the deviation from normalcy . . . **of obscure origin, the state of feeling ill or bad, not straight, obscure, perverse, eccentric**", and as a verb "**to quiz or ridicule, to puzzle, but also, to swindle and to cheat**". She then tracks every occurrence in the novella (the aunts, Gertrude's "queer little suppressed sound, a snort or a giggle", Brian's "old, queer, unhappy restlessness", Irene's voice that "had gone queer", Felise's "I've queered that") and draws the conclusion:

> "**queering is what upsets and exposes passing; it is the act by which the racially and sexually repressive surface of conversation is exploded, by rage, by sexuality, by the insistence on color.**"

**On how blackness becomes legible in the novella**: "Blackness is not primarily a visual mark in Larsen's story . . . because what can be seen, what qualifies as a visible marking, is a matter of being able to read a marked body in relation to unmarked bodies, where **unmarked bodies constitute the currency of normative whiteness**" and "**'race' itself is figured as a contagion transmissable through proximity**". And on the husband, whose name she reads as a sound: "His name, **Bellew, like bellow, is itself a howl**, the long howl of white male anxiety in the face of the racially ambiguous woman whom he idealizes and loathes. . . . But 'Bellew' is also **the instrument that fans the flame**." His racism requires what it denies: "**he cannot be white without his 'Nig,' without the lure of an association that he must resist**."

**Butler is careful about the choice the criticism had set up and refuses it**, naming both sides (Christian, Hull, Carby, Singh, Washington on the historical side; Tate, Wall, Youmans, McDowell on the psychological), and quotes Carby on the bind Larsen was writing inside: "**the representation of black female sexuality meant risking its definition as primitive and exotic within a racist society.**" Her own conclusion is about the gap in the narration at the moment of Clare's death, which she treats as the thing to be read rather than a defect: "**the narrator, who is usually able to say what Irene cannot, appears drawn into Irene's nonnarrativizable trauma, blanking out.**" And she follows the logic out of the novel: "the logic of 'passing' and 'exposure' came to afflict and, indeed, to end Nella Larsen's own authorial career", when she was accused of plagiarism over "Sanctuary" in 1930, "that is, **exposed as 'passing' as the true originator of the work**", after which "**she recede[d] into an anonymity from which she did not emerge.**"

## 9. Chapter 7: the argument against the rock

This is the chapter the companion file read one section of. Read whole, it is a sustained refusal to let a theory exempt its own founding claim from its own method, and the move is repeatable on any theory.

**The self-refuting structure, stated as a two-line proof:**

> "**To claim that the real resists symbolization is still to symbolize the real as a kind of resistance.** The former claim (the real resists symbolization) can only be true if the latter claim ('the real resists symbolization' is a symbolization) is true, but **if the second claim is true, the first is necessarily false**."

**The figure she catches, and why it matters that it is a rock:** the real appears in Žižek as "a 'rock' or a 'kernel' that not only resists symbolization and discourse" and yet is also, "sometimes within the same sentence, '**a loss**' a '**negativity**'; as a figure it appears to slide from substance to dissolution, **thereby conflating the law that institutes the 'lack' and the 'lack' itself**". She traces the rock to two places, which is the sort of sourcing worth copying: the Hebrew prayer in which God is "my rock and my redeemer", and the last page of Freud's "Analysis Terminable and Interminable", where women's resistance to the suggestion of penis envy reaches "bedrock" (*der gewachsener Fels*). Her gloss on Freud's own figure: "**this is a figure of a ground that is nevertheless sedimented through time, and so not a ground, but an effect of a prior process covered over by this ground.**"

**And the objection that is not about theory at all.** Žižek's text sets the patriarchal family, concentration camps and the Gulag side by side as instances of the same traumatic kernel; Butler's response is the ethical centre of the chapter:

> "**what is historical and what is traumatic are made absolutely distinct; indeed, the historical becomes what is most indifferent to the question of trauma** . . . But insofar as the real secures this lack, it postures as a self-identical principle that **reduces any and all qualitative differences among social formations . . . to a formal equivalence**."

> "**Even if every discursive formation is produced through exclusion, that is not to claim that all exclusions are equivalent**: what is needed is a way to assess politically how **the production of cultural unintelligibility is mobilized variably** to regulate the political field, i.e., who will count as a 'subject,' who will be required not to count."

> "**The production of the unsymbolizable, the unspeakable, the illegible is also always a strategy of social abjection.**"

> "**To freeze the real as the impossible 'outside' to discourse is to institute a permanently unsatisfiable desire for an ever elusive referent**: the sublime object of ideology . . . produc[ing] a prepolitical pathos."

**She also notices who the threats in the text are, and what that implies**, which is a model of reading a text's own rhetoric: "the 'real' appears within his text as that which needs to be protected or safeguarded from Foucaultian, feminist, and poststructuralist challenges . . . **If the 'real' is under threat, but is itself understood as the threat of castration, to what extent can Žižek's text be read as an effort to protect the 'threat' of castration against a set of further 'threats'?** . . . **The threat is protected in order to safeguard the law, but if it is in need of protection, the force of that law is already in a crisis that no amount of protection can overcome.**" And where Žižek has the rock speak ("Is not this effort to historicize the family triangle precisely an attempt to elude the 'hard kernel' . . ."), she notes the oddity: "**If the real of the law is precisely what cannot speak** . . . then it is with some interest that the real speaks here . . . and that it is Žižek who, it seems, **receives the word from the rock, and brings it down the mountain to us**."

### The aardvark named Napoleon

**This is the chapter's gift, and it is Kripke's own example, turned.** Kripke, needing all speakers to intend the same reference for a proper name to designate rigidly, concedes a case where that fails: "**If I hear the name 'Napoleon' and decide it would be a nice name for my pet aardvark, I do not satisfy this condition.**"

Butler's reading: "**this improper usage, however, inheres in the possibility of the proper usage**, indeed, remains that over and against and through which the proper reiterates itself as proper . . . **by virtue of the very reiterability of the name** . . . **this risk of catachresis is continually reproduced**." And therefore "**what is called 'the referent' depends essentially on those catachrestic acts of speech that either fail to refer or refer in the wrong way**", so that the question becomes "whether the permanent risk of catachresis does not '**unfix**' the referent."

**She then shows what the "initial baptism" in Kripke actually is**: a naming that "takes place at no time and place", that "cannot take place in private", and that is "**the fixing of a referent to a person through the interpellation of that person into a religious lineage**", a citation of Adamic naming; so "**the 'fixing' of the referent is thus a 'citation' of an original fixing**". From which she draws the consequence for the patronym: a rigid designator "continues to 'fix' a person through time only on the condition that there is no change of name", which requires exogamy, which requires that "**women, in their roles as wives and daughters, relinquish their name** . . . **The exchange of women is thus a prerequisite for the rigid designation of the patronym.**"

**And her own question, which is the one to carry away:** "**Is not the defilement of sovereignty, divine and paternal, performed by calling the aardvark 'Napoleon' precisely the catachresis by which hegemony ought to proceed?**"

**The chapter ends on the term "women" rather than on Žižek.** Her position: "the term marks **a dense intersection of social relations that cannot be summarized through the terms of identity**"; "if women are positioned as that which cannot exist, as that which is barred from existence by the law of the father, then there is a conflation of women with that foreclosed existence, that lost referent, that is **surely as pernicious as any form of ontological essentialism**"; and the double movement she recommends in place of either: "**to invoke the category and, hence, provisionally to institute an identity and at the same time to open the category as a site of permanent political contest** . . . **That the category can never be descriptive is the very condition of its political efficacy.**"

She also quotes Lauren Berlant for the affirmative version of failure: expecting full recognition "leads to a necessary scene of '**monstrous doubling**' and '**narcissistic horror**'", and in its place, "**the affirmation of that slippage, that failure of identification is itself the point of departure for a more democratizing affirmation of internal difference**."

## 10. Chapter 8: the distinction she insists on, and melancholia

**The distinction that most of the secondary literature misses, stated in one sentence:**

> "**performance as bounded 'act' is distinguished from performativity insofar as the latter consists in a reiteration of norms which precede, constrain, and exceed the performer and in that sense cannot be taken as the fabrication of the performer's 'will' or 'choice'** . . . **The reduction of performativity to performance would be a mistake.**"

**And she takes responsibility for the confusion:** "One of them I brought on myself by citing drag as an example of performativity, a move that was taken then, by some, to be **exemplary** of performativity. **If drag is performative, that does not mean that all performativity is to be understood as drag** . . . **I never did think that gender was like clothes, or that clothes make the woman.**"

**On the word "queer" the chapter is about timing**, and it refuses to generalise the reclamation:

> "**When and how does a term like 'queer' become subject to an affirmative resignification for some when a term like 'nigger,' despite some recent efforts at reclamation, appears capable of only reinscribing its pain?**"

> "it will have to remain that which is, in the present, **never fully owned, but always and only redeployed, twisted, queered from a prior usage** . . . This also means that **it will doubtless have to be yielded in favor of terms that do that political work more effectively**."

> "**it may be that the conceit of autonomy implied by self-naming is the paradigmatically presentist conceit**", and against it, a list of questions to ask of any identity term: "**For whom is outness a historically available and affordable option? Is there an unmarked class character to the demand for universal 'outness'?**"

**The melancholia argument is the book's last substantive move**, and it inverts the usual reading of drag:

> "**gender performance allegorizes a loss it cannot grieve**, allegorizes the incorporative fantasy of melancholia whereby an object is phantasmatically taken in or on as a way of refusing to let it go."

> "**drag allegorizes heterosexual melancholy**, the melancholy by which a masculine gender is formed from the refusal to grieve the masculine as a possibility of love; a feminine gender is formed . . . through the incorporative fantasy by which the feminine is excluded as a possible object of love, **an exclusion never grieved, but 'preserved' through the heightening of feminine identification itself**."

> "In this sense, the '**truest**' lesbian melancholic is the strictly straight woman, and the '**truest**' gay male melancholic is the strictly straight man."

> "**it may well be that what constitutes the sexually unperformable is performed instead as gender identification.**"

> and the cause she names is an absence rather than a refusal: "**This is, then, less the refusal to grieve** (a formulation that accents the choice involved) **than a preemption of grief performed by the absence of cultural conventions for avowing the loss of homosexual love.**"

**The chapter reads collective grieving as survival infrastructure**, with one named example: "**the NAMES Project Quilt is exemplary, ritualizing and repeating the name itself as a way of publically avowing the limitless loss**", and "The emergence of collective institutions for grieving are thus **crucial to survival, to the reassembling of community, the reworking of kinship, the reweaving of sustaining relations.**" It also keeps a history of theatricality that is worth having as a list: "traditions of cross-dressing, drag balls, street walking, butch-femme spectacles, the sliding between the 'march' (New York City) and the parade (San Francisco); **die-ins by ACT UP, kiss-ins by Queer Nation**; drag performance benefits for AIDS".

**And the book's last two paragraphs are about losing control of one's own writing**, which is the preface's problem returning as a conclusion:

> "**How will we know the difference between the power we promote and the power we oppose? Is it, one might rejoin, a matter of 'knowing?'** For one is, as it were, in power even as one opposes it . . . **The incalculable effects of action are as much a part of their subversive promise as those that we plan in advance.**"

> "**They continue to signify in spite of their authors, and sometimes against their authors' most precious intentions.** . . . It is one of the ambivalent implications of the decentering of the subject to have **one's writing be the site of a necessary and inevitable expropriation** . . . **speaking is always in some ways the speaking of a stranger through and as oneself, the melancholic reiteration of a language that one never chose**."

The acknowledgements record who read it (Joan W. Scott read the whole text; Drucilla Cornell, Elizabeth Grosz and Margaret Whitford read drafts; Wendy Brown is credited with "the careful persuasion which helped me to see how revisions of some of my earlier positions might better suit and clarify my aims"), and the dedication records the losses the last chapter is about: "**This book is written in the memory of those friends and family I have lost in recent years: my father, Dan Butler; my grandmother, Helen Greenberger Lefkowich; my friends, Linda Singer and Kathy Natanson.**"
