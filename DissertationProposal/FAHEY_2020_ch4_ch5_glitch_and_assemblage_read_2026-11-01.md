**The short version.** Chapter 4 turns out to rest on a biographical fact the earlier read did not reach: **Fahey was a radiographer before she was an artist, and she has delivered image-guided robotic radiosurgery.** The chapter is built on the structural resemblance she recognised between the drone footage she was watching and the medical images she used to make. That makes it a **medical-imaging apparatus chapter**, which puts it in 1.1 and 2.5 as well as the permeability lane. Chapter 5 is a **materials and voice chapter**, and its "Stuttering" section is built from a Skype call with a speech therapist.

## 1. The radiographer, and the apparatus argument that follows

> "Early on in the research, I became aware of the complex ways **the body is exploited in drone warfare through medical terminology** and the use of medical language to legitimise drone warfare. A conversation with a former colleague about her research into image-guided radiation treatment planning reminded me of **the resemblances between the technical visualising practices spanning my former career as a radiographer and my current practice as an artist-researcher.** Image-guided robotic radiosurgery is a complex procedure used to treat patients with inoperable malignant tumours, **the delivery and planning of which I have been involved in on a number of occasions.** As I watched RAF thermal drone footage on YouTube, I thought about the similarities of this visual experience to my own experience as a radiographer. **This is why I was drawn to the images in the first place; they are technologically comparable visual practices operating to enact very real material effects on bodies.**"

**The reconstruction passage is a measurement-produces-its-object instance with a number in it**, and it is told by someone who ran the machine:

> "in many types of medical scanning, including computed tomography (CT), **the sensor collects radio-density data at 2 to 3mm intervals. It performs an interpolation between these image slices, to reconstruct the visualisation of the body. So, the reconstructed body is partially sensed, partially generated by an algorithm computed by the machine and visualised by computations that turn this data into pixels on a screen.**"

> "**Both practices decorporealise the body; the unpredictable fleshy matter of the body is mapped and calculated, captured on a microscale through algorithmic analysis and on a macroscale through the generation of grey-scale images designed to be technically read and potentially actioned in some way.**"

**Two sources behind it that 1.1 should have.** **Friedrich and Queisner (2014), "Automated Killing and Mediated Caring: How Image-Guided Robotic Intervention Redefines Radiosurgical Practice"**, MEMCA-14, AISB50: putting medical and thermal drone images side by side "is problematic, but 'it nevertheless points to a **structural resemblance of the technological conditions between military and medical interventions**'", and, at p. 4, that **"it is the image of the patient's body, rather than the patient's body that becomes the central reference."** And **Jeremy Packer (2013), "Screens in the Sky: SAGE, Surveillance, and the Automation of Perceptual, Mnemonic, and Epistemological Labour", *Social Semiotics* 23(2): 189**, for the "**radiographic episteme**", which concerns "scanning systems and practices that **produce images without light**". **Images produced without light is the event-camera and Abbe-limit question arriving from medicine**, and it belongs beside `NEUROMORPHIC_CAMERA_lens.md` and Fitje's chapter.

**And she worked with a phantom rather than human data.** A "Multipurpose Chest Phantom N1 'LUNGMAN'", which is "essentially **a non-human body that functions similarly techno-visually to human bodies through machine vision**", chosen so that she could experiment without using human data. "Phantoms used in both medical research and teaching **produce very real effects on corporeal bodies**, be it through the testing of treatment plans for applications, or the pedagogical implications in clinical practice, on patients."

Her reason for using it is the permeability argument: it let her "**problematise the reduction of the body to its decorporeal surface, ignoring the physical dimensions of bodies, 'the reproductive organs, lungs and heart, glands and capillaries'**" (Kathy Davis 2007: 54, quoted via **Josef Barla, *The Techno-Apparatus of Bodily Production*, 44**).

## 2. Metaphor doing lethal work, which is a seventh position for the metaphor cluster

The chapter's opening section is about metaphor, and it is the only place in anything read for this project where a metaphor's standing is a matter of who dies.

> "We discussed our shared concerns and **the problematic ways the medical field invokes metaphors of warfare and violence and how medical terminology such as 'surgical precision' is co-opted by the military.**"

> "**Medical rhetoric depoliticises warfare; bio-medical metaphors work to render military violence 'intrinsically therapeutic' where 'counter-insurgency becomes chemotherapy, killing insurgent cells' and 'sometimes even innocent bodies to save the body politic'.**" (Gregory, "From a View to a Kill", 205)

> The rhetoric is, per **Chad Harris**, a "**mass media construct**" to create an appearance of "smooth interoperation between humans and machinery" using the language of scientific technicality.

> Positioning those they term "insurgents" as "**an alien force that invades the social body**" (Colleen Bell).

> And Fahey's own: "**This metaphor of cancer is arguably racially inscribed through the lens of colonialism in how it positions certain bodies as mutations to be controlled, disciplined and eradicated. This medical, curative language reinforces the ideology of technical, surgical precision conveyed linguistically in the operative images online.**"

**The question the metaphor file has been asking is whether a metaphor is less than the thing it describes. Here it is the instrument.** Recorded alongside the other positions, not adjudicated.

## 3. "Leaking", and fictioning as a method

Chapter 4's second half is called **Leaking**, and the work in it is *If/Then*.

> "**Fictioning as a methodology allowed me to imagine conversations and write a script with other characters.** I began writing *If/Then* as **a dialogue between a scanning machine and a human**. It developed through many iterations, eventually into **a recollection of a conversation, rather than a dialogue**."

**What the fiction was made out of was real and heterogeneous**, and she lists it: "the recollection of an imaginary dialogue I had written became entangled with other fragments of research into animal and machine vision, **a Skype conversation with a scientist researching the use of motion detection suits for lie detection**, patient experiences of medical scanning and **personal reports in online forums and blogs about disassociated psychological states**."

**And a craft finding reported as a finding**, which is the kind of thing practice-as-research is for: "I collected the technical sounds of machines online and in the studio, and **recorded the voice-over using an app on my phone. It struck me how recording in this direct way afforded many of the qualities I had struggled to achieve in *Repetitive Strain* using multiple layers of effects.** The sonic qualities of the voice were both embodied and recognisably human yet also dislocated, distant."

**John Law on ANT** (2009: 141), which is the same Law the methods lane runs on for mess: ANT is "the enactment of materially and discursively heterogeneous relations that produce and reshuffle all kinds of actors including objects, subjects, human beings, machines, animals, 'nature', ideas, organisations, inequalities, **scale and sizes**, and geographical arrangements." With **Bennett, *Vibrant Matter*, viii** on machines that "act as quasi agents […] with trajectories, propensities and tendencies of their own", and, at **xvi, Bennett "argues for a careful and strategic approach to anthropomorphism"**, which is a third held position on that question beside Barad's criterion and the anthropocentrism file's own conclusion.

**And a footnote worth the whole section.** Footnote 315 is Fahey's own summary of Tuana, with the page range and the reason for the word:

> "In her discussion of the impact [of] Hurricane Katrina, Nancy Tuana uses **the metaphor of 'viscous porosity'** to describe the relational interaction between humans, matter and the social, akin to Donna Haraway's material-semiotic actor . . . She states that '**attention to the porosity of interactions helps to undermine the notion that distinctions […] signify a natural or unchanging boundary**'. **She uses the term 'viscosity' to imply a resistance, rather than the free flowing implications of 'fluidity'** . . . See Nancy Tuana, 'Viscous Porosity: Witnessing Katrina', in *Material Feminisms*, 188-214. **Leaky and porous bodies are also reclaimed by Elizabeth Grosz and Margrit Shildrick not as irrational and unpredictable but affirmatively disruptive and embodied subjects.**"

**Note that Fahey, like Kotzé, calls viscous porosity a metaphor.** Two independent readers of Tuana, in two unrelated theses, both use that word for it.

**The footnote also names two porosity sources the permeability notes do not have:** **Grosz, *Volatile Bodies: Toward a Corporeal Feminism*** (Indiana, 1994), and **Shildrick, M. (2015), "'Why Should Our Bodies End at the Skin?': Embodiment, Boundaries, and Somatechnics", *Hypatia* 30(1): 13-29.** That Shildrick article is the permeability lane's own question as a title, by the author whose 1996 monstrous-body chapter is already held.

**Three more things from Leaking.** **Butler on instruments**, which is a constitutive-exclusion claim and belongs in 2.5: "persons use technological instruments, but **instruments surely also use persons** (position them, endow them with perspective, establish the trajectory of their action); they **frame and form anyone who enters into the visual or audible field, and, accordingly, those who do not**…", with the consequence that some lives are cast as "**ungrievable**", "framed as 'threats to human life as we know it rather than as living populations in need of protection'". **Catherine Waldby** on the "**cybernetic turn**" as the "**calibration of living entities according to the logics of the computer**". And **Fahey's objection to her own sources**: Friedrich and Queisner claim a "hybrid agency" resulting in a "loss of individual autonomy", "**Yet they omit any reference to the complex programming and algorithmic computations input by humans at research, development, and quality assurance stages of technical development.**" The labour behind the algorithm, which is the same move as the privacy-is-an-algorithm-not-a-sensor point in the camera notes.

## 4. Chapter 5 opens on her own burst blood vessel

The chapter's first words are set as short lines:

> "**I burst a blood vessel in my eye. The white was**
> **pink**
> **for days.**
> **All the world looked pink through that eye until the redness dissipated.**
> It happened when I was polishing a piece of textured aluminium that I had created, a process which demanded intense looking and concentration on the surface. The workshop was hot, and I was inexperienced.
> **A tiny blood vessel haemorrhaged.**"

**A chapter of an examined doctoral thesis opening on the researcher's own injury, caused by her own making, with the eye that does the looking as the thing that fails.** For a project whose own starting point is a body's marks from expansion and contraction, this is the nearest formal precedent in the folder: no theory first, no apology, and the line broken where the colour is.

## 5. Materials, and a new Barad page for the permeability lane

The making is cast glass and aluminium into plaster moulds she and a technician made: "**The forms were never in a resting state, but in a constant flow of temperature and moisture variation; at times it was essential to keep them moist, at others to dry them out, only to coat them in wet plaster again.**"

Her evidence is what the metal did: "**The infinite particles of the material of the molten aluminium flowing and leaking into the ruptured, emergent cracks and splits within the mould** could be understood as a tangible marker of this liveliness of materials and the congealing of the multiplicities of relational bodies and forces involved in the making of the work."

**Tim Ingold, *Making: Anthropology, Archaeology, Art and Architecture*** (2013: 21, 25), which is a major practice-based source the plan does not have: making is "**not the imposition of form onto matter, but the contraposition of forces**", extending to "the **variability of matter, its tensions and elasticities, lines of flow and resistances**", and, via Deleuze and Guattari, "whenever we encounter matter '**it is matter in movement, in flux, in variation**'".

**And a Barad page the permeability lane should have, which is the strongest one-sentence version of its own claim.** *Meeting the Universe Halfway*, **p. 153**:

> "**All bodies, not merely 'human' bodies, come to matter through the world's iterative intra-activity, its performativity. This is true not only of the surface or contours of the body but also of the body in the fullness of its physicality, including the very 'atoms' of its being.**"

With **"Posthumanist Performativity", *Signs* 28(3), 2003: 815, 822** (held in `Comps\Used texts`) for co-constitution and for agency as "**congealed**".

**Her statement of the Butler limit is clean and citable**, and it is the distinction 2.5 needs: Butler's performativity comprises "speech acts and bodily acts" after Merleau-Ponty, and although she argues language and materiality are embedded in one another, "**her theorisation of the matter of the body is one that is shaped and shapes primarily through language**", where matter is "**a process of materialisation that stabilises over time to produce the effect of boundary, fixity, and surface**", positioning bodies "primarily as subject to processes of regulatory power structures".

## 6. Stuttering, and the glitch

**The etymology first**, which is a thirteenth precedent for the 1.5 row: "**The etymology of the word glitch is related to slipperiness.**" And the technical definition she builds on: "**Buffering is a type of glitch in digital processes where the program reading content from the buffer must pause until the 'buffer refills'.**"

**The Stuttering section is built from one conversation, and she prints her notes as a list:**

> "I began further research into **vocal stutters / stammers (the terms are used interchangeably depending on the geographical location)** and their effect on the voice. From Berlin I Skype video called through a bad connection with **a friend of a friend who is a speech therapist**. **My understanding of the stammer was that of a bodily artefact, a mutation that cannot be predicted or controlled.** My notes from our conversation read;
> **-when someone is stressed**
> **-highly emotive states**
> **-when linguistic demand outweighs capacity**
> **-breakdown**
> **-cannot cope**
> **-blocking, repetition, prolongation**"

Note the method: **the research instrument and the subject are the same thing**, a bad Skype connection used to talk about broken speech, and the work that results is "a fictional Skype conversation over a poor connection" generating "**a mode of intimate attention, rather than representation or interpretation**". She describes the voice-over as "**a speculative mutation of this original script, its minor language / stutter**", after **Simon O'Sullivan** on **Deleuze and Guattari's minor language**, and the text carries the line "**You're breaking up, can you hear me?**"

**And she turns on her own work.** Having argued for unruliness, citing Deleuze's "**what can a body do?**" (*Nietzsche and Philosophy*, 1996: 39):

> "**However, the practice-based research I had created was undeniably instructional and concerned primarily with the control and regulation of the bodies and more specifically, stutters and artefacts.**"

A researcher stating that her own piece did the thing she objected to. **That is the self-audit move Fitje makes in his last paragraph and Kotzé makes about her own prescriptiveness; three held texts now do it.**

**Mattering** then limits Butler a second way: "**Butler's notion of vulnerability is limited in that it depends on recognition for an ethical encounter to occur. It is primarily based on asymmetrical power relations that 'we' or 'I' recognise 'you'.**" Against which Butler herself has recognition depending on "**an exchange that dislocates us from our positions**", so that to ask for or offer recognition is "**to elicit a becoming, to instigate a transformation**". And **Puar**, "Becoming-Intersectional in Assemblage Theory", 55, with an objection that cuts at this whole lane: the attempt to attend to difference and the specificity of others is paradoxically "**beholden to the self-referentiality of the 'centre'**".

## 7. What the thesis claims, in its own words

From the conclusion's statement of aims and contributions:

> "**Technical vulnerabilities appeared through investigating error, spoofing and digital artefacts in the practice-based methods, whilst bodily vulnerabilities emerged through leaks, stutters, ticks and narrative inconsistencies; both modes acted to disrupt the ideology of seamless interactions with technology.**"

> "the research aims to **challenge the eschewal of encounter with absented bodies whilst avoiding both re-inscribing the body through colonial modes and disavowing the impact of drone strikes**."

> a "practice-based research methodology that generates an embodied art practice and **non-corrective body of work** based on a (new) **materially adjusted Butlerian vulnerability**, **and the limits of this claim**."

**"Leaks, stutters, ticks" is worth noticing on its own account.** Her category of bodily vulnerability is built from three things, and two of them, the stutter and the tic, are the vocabulary of the stimming lane. **A drone-art thesis and an autistic-rhetoric monograph arrive at the same list from opposite directions**, and nothing in the plan connects them.
