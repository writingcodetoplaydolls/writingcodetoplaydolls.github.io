## 1. Chapter One: the term, and the four reasons the videos are online

The chapter's object is the **operative image**, Harun Farocki's term from "Phantom Images" (2004) for images "that have a role in automatic targeting through pattern recognition and pixel matching". Farocki's claim was that such images "went beyond representation to be part of an operation, actively targeting, '**doing things in the world**'".

**What Fahey is actually looking at, stated as a dated record**: strike videos uploaded to YouTube by the RAF (playlist *Operations*, Iraq, Afghanistan, Libya, available to her from Sept 2016 until Jan 2020), the MoD (playlist *#DefeatingDaesh*, Operation Shader) and US CentCom (Operation Inherent Resolve), from as early as 2009; uploading ceased by US CentCom from 2015, by the RAF in 2015 and the MoD in 2017, after which the footage moved to the DVIDS website. **She notes in a footnote that YouTube was bought by Google in 2006 and that Google worked on Project Maven.**

**Her reason for choosing YouTube is a reason about context, not content:** the videos sit "alongside other forms of propaganda, leaked military operative content, soldiers' video diaries, victim testimony, news reports and music videos, **makeup tutorials, children's entertainment, home do-it-yourself demonstrations, comedy sketches**".

**She asked someone and quotes the answer.** Chris Cole, director of Drone Wars, by email, gave four reasons, all of which "can all be understood broadly as propaganda": to show the RAF is effective against ISIS and so keep public and parliamentary support; "that the strikes are accurate (to try to contrast narratives around civilian casualties and 'accidents')"; "that they are deadly (to deter potential enemies)"; and "**that it is exciting, hence the music soundtrack (to aid with recruitment)**". **A short, sourced, four-point answer obtained by writing to a person who would know.**

**Three senses of "operative" are distinguished**, after Pantenburg: an image made by and for machines to perform an operation; an image that is visually interpretable and part of a technical operation; and the performative sense, where "techniques such as montage or voice-over commentary inject a sense of agency that the images themselves would not have". Hoel adds the reciprocal reading (Manovich), and Fahey keeps all three because all three are at work in her material. Also useful: Flusser's **black box**, with the distinction between "technical images" (computed from concepts) and "traditional images" (generated from observing objects), and the warning that "**the 'objectivity' of technical images is an illusion**"; and Latour's extension of the black box to any machine whose success shifts attention to inputs and outputs.

## 2. "Absenting": the verb, and the list of mechanisms

She makes a verb of it and says why: "I use the verb '**absent**' to describe the multifaceted technical, corporeal, material, linguistic and visual ways operative images act to remove bodies, but also to consider the ways bodies are removed from the operative images online", and defines her other key phrase: "By '**ideology of the technical**', I mean that the videos promote an ideology of technical precision and infallibility that contribute to the appearance of a bodiless war."

**The chapter is then an itemised list of how the removal is actually done**, each item sourced. This is the part worth keeping as a model, because it is a mechanism inventory rather than an argument:

| Mechanism | How it works |
|---|---|
| **the aerial view** | "the view from above as a scientific, rational and calculative space" (Adey); Virilio's battlefield "composed of nothing"; Gregory's "ground truth vanishes in the ultimate 'God-trick', whose terrible vengeance depends on **making its objects visible and its subjects invisible**" |
| **duration cut** | drone crews follow targets for hours or days; "the films themselves are usually less than a minute to four minutes long" |
| **thermal rendering** | grey-scale values "eradicate[] variation, difference, and noise [that] may impede action or introduce moral ambiguity" (Wall and Monahan) |
| **resolution** | optical resolution is restricted information; Forensic Architecture estimate leaked 2015 Italian drone footage at **1 to 2 cm per pixel**, while public satellite imagery is degraded "to remove the human from representation" (Weizman), to "mask[] the human figure within the square of a single pixel" |
| **substitution of bodies** | where a body is needed, it is the pilot's: "it is often the image of an apparently white western body such as a pilot that acts as a substitute" (Stahl); and the trauma discourse follows the operators, with Pinchevski's caution that this "may 'obscure the suffering of those targeted'" |
| **editing** | clips end "just after the explosion cloud"; delayed-detonation weapons mean buildings "remain and appear relatively intact, unlike the bodies inside" |
| **the caption** | objects not people: "bridges", "strongholds", "bunkers", "compounds", "tunnels", "fighting positions", "vehicles", "trucks", "armoured tanks"; people only as "targets", "fighters", "ISIL", "DAESH" |
| **the word "precision"** | "**accuracy and precision are not interchangeable**"; Zehfuss challenges "more precision is better" and notes that "however precise the weapon may be, non-combatant protection will only ever be at best as good as the information used by those operating it" |
| **pattern of life analysis** | human movement, conversation and interaction converted "algorithmically into a patterned sequence of numerals", producing "**decorporeal data-doubles**" (Pugliese) |

**Elaine Scarry supplies the three conditions**, which Fahey then shows are all met: the language of killing stops being morally resonant when the rhetoric shifts from bodies to results, when the military itself becomes the wounded body, and when "bodies themselves are transformed into the objects they inhabit". Scarry's claim that "**injury to the body is the 'content' of war**" and is "often the issue that is most concealed" is the chapter's hinge.

**And the counts are given, with ranges and sources rather than single figures**: the Stanford/NYU *Living Under Drones* report on how missiles kill ("incineration, shrapnel, and the release of powerful blast waves capable of crushing internal organs"); the Bureau of Investigative Journalism's estimate that since 2004 "**8,459 to 12,105 people have been killed in a minimum of 6,786 US led airstrikes in Pakistan, Afghanistan, Yemen and Somalia, 769 to 1,725 of whom were civilians and 253 to 397 were children**"; the MoD admitting its first civilian casualty in Iraq and Syria only in March 2018; and the **"double tap"**, a second strike on survivors and rescuers.

**Her own closing report of what she felt is two words long, and she distinguishes it from the words she might have been expected to use:**

> "Watching the videos online, my overall sense is one of detachment . . . **However, I do not feel relieved or complicit; I feel numb.**"

**And she states the problem her practice then has to solve, including the objection to her own solution:** foregrounding the senses can be "a physical distraction of the look" (Guerin), but "**these images are already censored; there is nothing of the body to be sensed, they are desensitised**", while "**the suffering of victims cannot be represented mimetically without violating their integrity as corporeal and individual human beings**".

## 3. Chapter Two: three bodies of work, three failures, named

**The chapter's own frame is unusual and should be noted first**: the three bodies of work "as of yet . . . remain without titles, analysed instead as experiments with materials, images and surfaces", and are referred to collectively as *Material Translations*. **Untitled, because they did not arrive.** The starting hypothesis was haptic visuality (Laura U. Marks: "the eyes themselves function like organs of touch"; "haptic looking tends to rest on the surface of its object rather than to plunge into depth, not to distinguish form so much as to discern texture"), tested against Steyerl's question, quoted as a question: "**what if the truth is in its material configuration?**"

**Travelling** records the archive trip: RAF Chicksands, the Military Intelligence Museum holding the RAF Medmenham archive, where her passport was confiscated at the gate, she was chaperoned by a retired aerial analyst, photography was banned on the base but allowed inside the museum, and "**I quickly learned that going off-script was not an option**". What she went for was the embodied technique, and she lists what she found: stereoscopy, photo-mosaics, terrain modelling, painted and draped vertical surfaces as camouflage; and at the British School at Rome, that "**sensitive parts of aerial images were cut out by hand and removed from the image as a form of censorship**", plus a terrain-model method of carving plaster with a pantograph from an aerial image and then "**draping of an image skin (a soft and pliable aerial photograph) over the top**". **The comparison she draws is between two archives and how each feels**: Chicksands "felt surveilled . . . sites where specific narratives are played out", against YouTube's "appearance of an uninstitutional, user-generated archive", which is also not one, since "YouTube censors both promote and conceal content through algorithms and user-generated operations".

**Scrolling** is written partly as its own object, with the keystroke on its own line ("Command + Shift + 4") and the scroll broken across lines. The frame is Steyerl's **poor image** ("a ghost of an image, a preview, a thumbnail, an errant idea") and her line that "**Their poverty is not a lack but an additional layer of information, which is not about content but form**", plus Eder's **cold** and **hot** images: these are cold on first look and hot on second, given the captions ("protecting our people") and the "energetic and powerful electro-rock music", which Fahey tracks down to a named stock track. **Her report of zooming in is a null result, stated as one:** "With the magnifier tool I zoom in and back out, travelling over the surface of the image. Yet investigating the image in this way **reveals nothing but grey pixels**."

### The three failures

| Work | What she did | Why it failed, in her words |
|---|---|---|
| **plaster** | Stripped plasterboard of its paper, sanded it, converted stills to **bitmaps** so the laser engraver could read them (so "the appearance of the engraved image . . . was dependent on two levels of depth, and nothing in between"), engraved them, later cut the forms by hand to escape the screen's proportions | "**I recognised that the engravings on plaster were illustrating an idea, rather than embodying an exploration of the question.** They did not consider how the techniques employed and the materials used are woven through the nature of the investigation." And the broader failure: translating the image "onto and into a tactile, haptic material does not interrupt its visual signification towards an ideology of a technical and bodiless war **and arguably perpetuates it**" |
| **latex** | Stills printed on fine silk, submerged in medical-grade liquid latex by a specialist producer, then buffed with silicone so the surface rippled and "disrupt[ed] the legibility of the image" | "**Because the still images I was using were heavily desensitised and redacted, in my choice of stills I had in fact created a series of graphic, albeit abstracted and visceral skin-like objects**" . . . "my encounter with them reveals nothing of the bodies absented from the images **beyond problematic registers of disgust and discomfort**". Representing absented bodies this way "**objectifies and marks them as other**" |
| **faux marble tile** | Removed the image in Photoshop and kept only the redaction marks; cut a floor inlay from black, white and grey faux marble tile with a waterjet from a vector file; grouted it bright yellow | "I had to work with the demands of the machine, rather than my own or the material's" . . . "**I didn't explore the materiality of the tile itself, and its unpredictability, bodily and generative potential**" . . . "the removal of the content resulted in a disembodied, graph-like and technical image", and "**focusing on mechanisms was itself a form of black-boxing**" |

**And the double bind she arrives at, stated as a bind rather than resolved:**

> "a sole focus on corporeality can erase the structures drone warfare employs to make bodies invisible . . . **Nevertheless, focusing solely on the mechanisms and structures that absent, redescribe and disappear bodies can serve to paradoxically erase them.**"

**The comparisons are used to locate her own failures, not to rank artists.** Trevor Paglen's *Drone Vision* (2010) "conceals the embodied processes involved in its original production and its production as an artwork"; Romeo Alaeff's *Air Strikes* charcoal drawings raise "the ethics of reproducing the drones' eye, techno-scientific, weaponised view"; Thomas Hirschhorn's *The Incommensurable Banner* "says little of the political frameworks and power structures that actually make the bodies invisible", with Dawney's point that the wounded body "can 'do powerful political work'" for propaganda and Sobchack's that "**the horror of the corpse is precisely that it is not perceived as a subject**"; James Bridle's *Everyday Redaction* is "almost inaccessible without its text-based introduction", which raises the question of "what it means to accompany this data visualisation with a text that ensures that the work is read in a specific way by the viewer". **Her reading of Hirschhorn's title is generous and specific**: "Hirschhorn uses the title text, **not to make us understand but to potentially allude to what we cannot understand**", with the etymology of *incommensurable* given (in, com, mensurare).

**Doing** turns to Jenny Holzer's Tate Artist Rooms (2018) as the counter-example that works, with Holzer quoted on the standard: "'**I hope the text and the visuals are inseparable [now]. It shouldn't be either / or**'". What Fahey takes from the room is spatial and bodily: looking up to read LED text in time with its movement, a sleeping bag embroidered with a British Army nurse's first-person testimony "the form and placement of which conveys the sense of an absent body", people sitting on the engraved marble seats, the LED "appeared to be glitching, moving so fast that it was blinding and impossible to read".

**The chapter's closing list of what to do next is a list of questions she then actually answers in chapter three**, including: "how I can account for my position as a white artist, editor, producer, researcher, looking at these images through my laptop screen"; "how text could be used **without operating correctively**"; and "**Finally, I questioned if my own body could be used as a material in this way.**"

**One more finding, about her own method rather than the work**: reducing moving images to stills "problematically intensified their ideology . . . **this gesture was complicit with the militaries' reductive compression of drone warfare into bite sized ideologically constrained readily consumable content**". The still was the error.

## 4. The glitch arrives as an accident, and she keeps it

Worth isolating, because the glitch is the thesis's eventual key term (chapters 4 and 5) and **this is where it enters, as a material defect she did not plan**:

Plasterboard holds "tiny invisible fiberglass particles which made their way into the skin on my hands, temporarily producing a red reaction"; air bubbles left "tiny holes . . . defects produced from air bubbles in the casting process which gave the sheets an **osseous and organic quality**". Then, set out on its own lines in the thesis:

> "These holes were a **glitch** in my system. Tiny pits confounded the laser, shifting its focus. They disrupted communication between **sender** and **receiver**. They interrupted the image, generating multiple levels of immeasurable depth in the surface of the plaster instead of the two that we had programmed."

**And she recognises it from her previous profession, which is the first appearance of her radiography training in the argument:**

> "I had seen this phenomenon in my work as a radiographer. Interruptions from inside and outside the body, or the body moving during the procedure, **leave their mark as artefacts in the digital image**, disrupting the analyses and visual reading of the scan. **I once saw a patient's lungs scattered with shrapnel, appearing as multiple bright white circles on the x-ray.**"

## 5. Chapter Three: fictioning, and what it is not

The turn is stated as a response to the previous chapter's failures: "my thoughts turned to more imaginative modes of practice . . . **A writing practice that incorporates a more fictional approach to materials and bodies, I speculated, could provide the opportunity to work with questions of positioning, the multiplicity of bodies and disruptive, rather than corrective narratives.**"

**The residency is described as a condition of work**, including the room: British School at Rome, Sept 2017, a double-height studio with a loft over it, so "each evening I began laying out the images, materials and work I had brought on residency on the studio floor so that each morning I would wake up and **look down on them from above** for a couple of moments before descending the stairs". Among Roman antiquity she saw her plaster works for what they were: "**how static the plaster works I had previously made were, reminiscent of museological archaeological specimens**."

**On the aerial point of view she states the limit of her own access to it, in one sentence:** "My intention was **not to inhabit this point of view, but to be sensitive to this perspective and my inability to wholly access it**."

**Haraway carries the chapter**, and the quotations she chooses are the hard ones rather than the flattering ones: situated knowledge "allows us to become answerable for what we learn how to see"; the warning that "there lies a serious danger of romanticising and / or appropriating the vision of the less powerful"; the question "**With whose blood were my eyes crafted?**"; and the figure she adopts, a "'split and contradictory self' that is 'partial in all its guises, never finished, never whole . . . **able to join with another, to see together without claiming to be another**'". On fiction: "like facts, fiction refers to action, but fiction is about the act of fashioning, forming, inventing, as well as feigning or feinting", and fiction as "in process and still at stake, not finished, **still prone to showing something we do not know yet to be true but will know**".

**Fictioning is given a working definition by contrast with lying**, through Reeves-Evison: deceptive practices "may subsequently be revealed to be false, for example propaganda, whereas fictive practices come with a disclaimer that 'relinquishes their truth claims'" and so "**Both fiction and deception peddle untruths, but fiction scuppers its own ability to deceive**". And through O'Sullivan: fictioning works "when it is no longer clear where the fiction itself ends and so-called reality begins", requires "the introduction of something random; a rupture or point of collapse . . . **or else it risks just presenting more-of-the-same**", and produces "a myth to hold together these ruptures". Fictioning as "the sign of something . . . that **will not pander to the demand to make sense**".

**She also says what she is declining, and names the exhibition where she decided it.** At *Evidentiary Realism* (Nome Gallery, Berlin, Dec 2017, with Bridle, Farocki, Haacke, Holzer, Keenan, Weizman) she "left with an overwhelming feeling that evidentiary realism was not relevant to my research practice", and she names what she would count as evidence instead: "**the evidence of my interaction with the screen itself** . . . and the evidence of editing in my practice". **Also recorded on that trip, in two sentences, is an institution choosing to stay small**: at the Harun Farocki Institute, asked whether she would like it to become a bigger foundation, the coordinator said no, "that it would go against Farocki's belief system in challenging institutional power structures and that **the trustees preferred their peripheral status, unstable as it was**."

**Her diagnosis of the field is the chapter's sharpest sentence:**

> "much of the art practice concerning military warfare and violence has a **corrective** approach . . . they seek to uncover, make visible, demonstrate and reveal information, **even if what is revealed is the frameworks of invisibility or invisibility itself**, or the politics of looking and spectatorship."

And on the hackers specifically (Bridle, Paglen, Mosse), via Crary's "myth of the lone hacker": "**In the revelatory modes of their works there is an underlying corrective stance and apparently little consideration of their privileged status as white western males.**"

### How *Repetitive Strain* was actually made

Each decision is given with its reason, which is what makes the chapter usable.

- **The camera**: a handheld digital **microscope**, pointed at her own laptop screen while the drone footage played, at varying focal lengths, so that the recording catches "sometimes . . . the materiality of the screen itself, focusing on the level of the pixels, sometimes on the video playing on screen, sometimes on **the dirt, hair, dust and finger prints on the surface of the screen** and other times on the reflection of my hands". **She sawed off the microscope's protective case** to make it mobile rather than stand-mounted, and dust got inside the lens, "creating a grey floating shadow in the screen captures", which stays.
- **Why the microscope is not forensics**: "one can never truly read the image, as the microscopic camera never wholly zooms out; **only partial glimpses are revealed**", and this "was not, as Eyal Weizman terms 'critical forensic practice'". The parallel she draws is to her own eyes: "Even without the microscope, or my glasses for that matter, **my visual experience of the world is mediated and partial, interrupted by blinking, the limits of my peripheral vision and tiny floaters, like dark specks of dust in my biological lens.**"
- **The texts**: all three in the first person, "focussed on the points at which vision is occluded and overwhelmed by other forms of sensory experiences and memories", **drawn from real accounts of living under drones** (explosions, insomnia, injury, post-trauma) "interspersed with other accounts of the psychological and sensory impact of physical and mental trauma, drawn from online forums, news reports, blogs and personal experiences and real and imagined memories".
- **The voice**: read as multiple overlaid voice-overs with delays and reverberation, with a telephone filter applied "to convey the sense of both physical intimacy and distance within the sound", after Barthes's **grain of the voice**, so that "the materiality of the voice implies the bodily presence in the spoken words, rather than the intelligibility of language, **altering the significance of what is said, from the intelligible to the expressive**".
- **The other sound**: contact microphone on the laptop's internals, her own typing and mouse clicks, open-source **ASMR** recordings of fingernails on screens, and "the intermittent high pitched buzz of mosquitos", because "**People living under drones also describe the repetitive strain of looking up every time they hear the sound of the drone which they describe colloquially as like a mosquito.**"
- **The title**, doing two jobs: "the bodily implications of prolonged interaction with technology, but also **the persistent presence of drones in certain areas**, impacting on bodies sonically, physically and psychologically".
- **The buffer screen** between episodes: "a type of glitch in digital processes where the program reading content from the buffer must pause until the 'buffer refills'", and "in a sense buffering is **the processing of data yet to come, images yet to be looked at**". She notes the etymology of *glitch* is "related to slipperiness".

### The install, and the reports from it

Two installs, described with what went wrong and what viewers said.

**Embassy Gallery, Edinburgh, *Between Us And...*, June 2018**: three **Lenovo ThinkPads** chosen for their screens, "low resolution, matte and non-reflective", so that "there is sense that no matter how close one looks, it is impossible to see past a certain depth". **The viewer had to lie on a black daybed underneath and look up.** "Many reported a feeling of uncertainty, having never encountered a laptop screen in this way . . . Some viewers reported that the feeling of being physically **grounded** while others revealed that the flipping of the conventional spatial experience of the screen felt **unsettling**."

**Lewisham Arthouse, July 2018**: single-channel projection with four-channel sound, under a ceiling-hung sculpture made over one weekend from **thermal foil** (chosen as an emergency material and as "a potential thermal imaging-proof material", exploiting "a vulnerability in the camera's sensor"), driven by six fans, with four more speakers hidden above it playing the mosquito, five speakers in the room, three giant beanbags, and a ThinkPad flat on the wall playing a screen capture of scrolling through YouTube.

**Four things she had not predicted, listed as such:**

1. "digital sound and images (and the technology that plays them) have complex lives of their own which had to be worked with in a generational and almost collaborative way within the space"; the projector "overheated occasionally and spontaneously shut down"
2. the thermal ceiling "acted almost as a mirror, reflecting the projected image in a much more focused way than I had envisaged", rather than dispersing the light
3. the projection's own light "creating long periods of darkness, coloured light and intense flashes in the room", so that with their backs to the door "many reported how unsettling it felt to be in the space in those dark moments and how conscious they became of **the potential presence of someone behind them**"
4. "**Many viewers missed the ThinkPad in the exhibition altogether**", which she then treats as a finding about peripherality rather than a fault

**And she does not let her own solution off:** the texts "risked an **over-identification** in which bodies were reinscribed and represented according to a white western colonialist logic of which both Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak and Haraway are cautious. Thus, it could be read as approaching a problematic and corrective stance, through arguably **the linguistic equivalent of representation or representing the failure of representation, both of which I have been critical of previously**."

**The reference set for fictioning** is given with what each one contributes: **Walid Raad's *The Atlas Group*** (1989-2004) and *Secrets in the Open Sea*, where in performance he told an audience that monochrome blue slides were portraits of missing people whose bodies went into the Mediterranean, "us[ing] language not to explain the work, but to reveal a complex relationship between language and images **in which they are both performing a fictioning together**", and where he appears "no longer . . . the spokesperson, instead appearing as a conflicted and unstable, a split and contradictory version of himself"; **Omer Fast's *5000 Feet is the Best*** (2011), three slipping storylines and a metafictional exchange quoted in full ("'But you're not a real pilot' . . . 'So what? . . . You're not a real journalist'"), with Fahey's objection that it still centres the pilot, "as is often the case with filmic works", which is "arguably deceptive strateg[y] to humanise the inhumanity of war"; **Teju Cole's *Seven Short Stories about Drones*** (2013), tweets splicing first lines of novels into strike reports, quoted ("I am an invisible man . . . But an unmanned aerial vehicle from a secret location has come for me"), chosen partly for its circulation, since the platform lets the tweets embed "themselves and arguably interrupting other kinds of networks"; **Jo Namy's *Purple Bodies in Translation***, where her own reflection in the purple screen produced "a self-consciousness and **a partial blindness to the content described in the voice-over**", drawing attention "not so much to the failure of representation but instead to **the imaginative and productive space that existed at the edges**". Also **Batchen's looking askance** ("to look obliquely or out of the corner of one's eye, but with an underlying distrust"), adopted and then also held at arm's length, since it "produces a mode of representation concerning the failure of representation".

**Her closing formula for her own position**: "This sense of '**being with**' is also woven through my conceptualisation of my own practice as **existing among multiple others rather than acting as a proposed solution**."

|---|---|
| **A chapter about work that failed, with the failure named** | Three bodies of work, each given its own reason: illustrating an idea rather than embodying the question; producing graphic objects of other people's flesh; black-boxing by focusing on mechanism. **Untitled, because they did not arrive** |

| **A mechanism inventory** | Nine named, sourced ways bodies are removed from an image, from resolution to the caption's choice of nouns |
| **A word with a measurement under it** | "**accuracy and precision are not interchangeable**", with Zehfuss and Cole on why |
| **A sourced four-point answer from a person who would know** | Chris Cole's four reasons the videos are uploaded, obtained by email and quoted |
| **A glitch arriving as a defect and being kept** | Air bubbles in plasterboard confounding the laser head, recognised from radiography as a scan artefact: "Interruptions from inside and outside the body . . . leave their mark as artefacts in the digital image" |

| **Fiction distinguished from lying** | "Both fiction and deception peddle untruths, but **fiction scuppers its own ability to deceive**" |
| **A name for the default mode of a whole field, and its cost** | "**corrective**": uncovering, making visible, revealing, "even if what is revealed is the frameworks of invisibility or invisibility itself" |

**One formal habit, noted without comment**: each of the three chapters ends with a short passage in a different register, set in broken lines, in which she reports something about her own body: the numbness in front of the screen (ch. 1); the tangerine filter over her screen, "'a perceptual processing problem' . . . 'you see patterns in texts which make them difficult to read'", and "I can't touch type. My muscles can't remember" (ch. 2); and the severe migraine, the paralysis and loss of speech, and the scan afterwards, "lying down looking up, my body passing through the machine" (ch. 3). **The last of these returns the thesis's own position, lying under a screen looking up, to its source.**
