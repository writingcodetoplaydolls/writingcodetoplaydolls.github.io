## 3. Walkerdine 2010, read

**Walkerdine, V. (2010). "Communal Beingness and Affect: An Exploration of Trauma in an Ex-industrial Community." *Body & Society* 16(1), 91-116.** DOI 10.1177/1357034X09354127. 13,194 words. Now in the folder.

**Note the place.** This is **Steeltown**, a steelworks that closed in **2002**, not a coal mine. Steel 45 mentions; coal and mining together, 2. Ivinson and Renold's valleys are coal. Walkerdine's own case is the steel town. They treat it as the same post-industrial condition, and it mostly is, but the two should not be conflated when cited.

### Where "second skin" actually comes from

Ivinson and Renold attribute it to "Esther Bick, 1987, cited in Manning, 2013." **Walkerdine gets it directly from the psychoanalysis**, and it is the spine of her article: skin 34, Anzieu 13, Bick 11, Winnicott 5, Klein 4, Tustin 3.

> "Bick argued that failure to contain terrifying anxieties could result in the development of a muscular shell or a muscular holding, designed to hold everything in, which she called a second skin."

She then scales it from infant to community, through Anzieu:

> "Anzieu argued that 'groups suffer from not having a body and consequently imagine one' (1984: 214). This fantasy of a body provides an organizing principle and a nostalgic dream of symbiosis between its members."

> "a second skin could be said to hold the townspeople in a rigid containment, which provided a profound containing function and a strong sense of continuity of being. But there was a cost to this, which was a strict set of rules about conduct, resistance to outsiders and the inability of people to leave the town."

And the steelworks itself as the lost object: "geographically placed at the centre of the town," a "material object and an object of fantasy," razed within a month of closing, leaving "a huge material and affective empty space where there had once been an object."

### She names the limitation that the dance workshops answer

This is the most useful thing in it for the methodology chapter. Walkerdine, about her own method:

> "The method we used, while it explores affective relations in the psychoanalytic sense, does tend to be very language-based, as it uses the form of an interview. I need to make it clear then that what I am exploring here is a sense which emerged from my reading of the interviews and **the whole approach could be much further developed if data of a more embodied kind were to be collected.**"

Eleven years later, Ivinson and Renold open "What More Do Bodies Know?" with interviews failing because the girls could not speak, and move to improvised dance. **That is one researcher naming a limitation and two others building the method that answers it.** A clean, short, citable methodological genealogy: language-based interview → stated insufficiency → embodied movement → video slowed and sped up.

### And she pushes back on Deleuze and Guattari

Deleuze 8 mentions, Massumi 0, and the engagement is critical, not adopting:

> "in relation to Deleuze and Guattari's notion of the assemblage, I think we need to think much more carefully about the dynamic aspects of inter-relations which do not reduce to the linear concept of a flow."

and in the conclusion:

> "While Deleuze and Guattari stress the importance of understanding what can be created by the forces of desire, they perhaps underestimate the central significance of the production of an affective community as a defensive possibility of continuity in a sea of enforced change."

So she is a psychoanalytic counter-voice inside the same object: affect yes, assemblage-as-flow no, and change is "a play of light and dark, life and death," not only creative potential. Useful precisely because she is not in the choir.
