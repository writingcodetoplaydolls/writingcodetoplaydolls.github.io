## 3. Chapter 6. Nancy Tuana, "Viscous Porosity: Witnessing Katrina"

The editors call it the chapter that "brings together the key themes of this collection". It is the one to read first.

### The concept, and why it is viscous rather than fluid

Tuana's term is **viscous porosity**, offered as a conceptual metaphor for the interactions through which subjects are constituted out of relationality, and grounded in what she calls an **interactionist ontology** (her own position since 1983). Her reason for the first word is the usable part:

> "Viscosity is neither fluid nor solid, but intermediate between them . . . 'viscosity' retains an emphasis on resistance to changing form, thereby a more helpful image than 'fluidity,' which is too likely to promote a notion of open possibilities and to overlook sites of resistance and opposition."

**That is the answer to the problem of permeability being too easy.** A body described only as fluid loses its resistance, and with it the sites where change is refused. Viscosity keeps them. Distinctions can still be made, she insists, and they are made "for a particular purpose and at a particular time", under what she and Lorraine Code call **epistemic responsibility**: "Knowing well is a matter both of moral-political and of epistemic concern" (Code 1991: 72).

### The bodies

Three examples, in ascending order of use:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Plastic flesh** | Drink from a plastic bottle, then incinerate the bottle and breathe. "The parts of the plastic become as much a part of my flesh as parts of the coke that I drank. Once the molecular interaction occurs, there is no divide between nature/culture, natural/artificial." She puts the workers of cancer alley along the Mississippi beside Haraway's OncoMouse, and argues we do not need a transgenic animal to show the porosity |
| **Breast milk** | Via the ecologist **Sandra Steingraber (1999: 363)**: "When it comes to the production, use, and disposal of PVC, the breasts of breast-feeding mothers are the tailpipe." Dioxin is lipophilic, so breast milk concentrates it; a breast-fed infant receives its so-called safe lifetime limit in the first six months, and the concentration in the milk declines as nursing continues, so "the protective effect of breast feeding on the mother appears to be a direct result of downloading a lifelong burden of carcinogens from her breasts into the tiny body of her infant" |
| **Disability at Katrina** | 23% of New Orleanians reported a disability in the 2000 Census, out of 484,000 people. 28% of US adults aged 25-64 with a severe disability lived in poverty against 8.3% of the general population. Poverty causes disability and disability causes poverty. And yet, where race became visible in the coverage, disability did not. **Michael Bérubé (2005)**: "Who among us can forget that iconic image of the dead woman in the wheelchair outside the Superdome, covered only in a blanket? That might well have been the very symbol of Katrina's devastation in New Orleans, the wheelchair, not the woman, who was not visible, but the wheelchair itself." |

**The breast-milk passage is the exact counterpart to the microchimerism material in `PERMEABLE_BODIES_research_notes.md`, and it runs the other way.** That file has the child's cells persisting in the mother for decades, and maternal cells persisting in the child. This has the mother's accumulated chemical burden downloading into the child through milk, and the mother's own burden lightening as it does. Traffic in both directions, and in one direction it is harm.

### The methodological gift: an epistemology of ignorance

Tuana's framing of why the poverty and the disability were a "shock" to the nation uses **Charles Mills (1997: 18)**: "the Racial Contract prescribes for its signatories an inverted epistemology, an epistemology of ignorance, a particular pattern of localized and global cognitive dysfunctions (which are psychologically and socially functional), producing the ironic outcome that whites will in general be unable to understand the world they themselves have made." Her gloss: "we must also understand the practices that account for not knowing".

**This folder keeps producing absence findings**: stim appearing substantively only in Yergeau across ~21 million words; no first-person art practice with event cameras; porosity dropping from 49 to 0 across a decade. **"Epistemology of ignorance" is the name for studying that**, with a literature behind it, and it converts those findings from gaps in a corpus into objects of study. That is the single most useful import from this book for 2.2.

### Other citations from the chapter

William James (1958: 263), "not things made but things in the making"; Andrew Pickering's **"dance of agency"** (1995) and "in the thick of things" (2001); Searle (1995: 150) on external realism; the 1927 dynamiting of the Poydras levee at Caernarvon and the Lower Ninth Ward's belief in 2005 that the levees had been breached deliberately (Gomez 2000: 120); and a caution she states explicitly, against "positing an Edenic time before colonialization and industrialization and against the persistent myth of passive indigenous peoples who simply lived in but did not transform 'nature'".

## 4. Chapter 8. Stacy Alaimo, "Trans-Corporeal Feminisms and the Ethical Space of Nature"

The original statement of the term, two years before *Bodily Natures*.

**The definition**: trans-corporeality is "the time-space where human corporeality, in all its material fleshiness, is inseparable from 'nature' or 'environment'", and as a theoretical site it is "a place where corporeal theories and environmental theories meet and mingle". It "makes it difficult to pose nature as a mere background for the exploits of the human, since 'nature' is always as close as one's own skin."

**Why "trans"**: because it "indicates movement across different sites", which "opens up an epistemological 'space' that acknowledges the often unpredictable and unwanted actions of human bodies, nonhuman creatures, ecological systems, chemical agents, and other actors." The editors add that the space-time of trans-corporeality is "a site of both pleasure and danger", and that Alaimo's focus is **toxic bodies**, which are "not something to celebrate" but which refuse the choice between romanticising bodies and fleeing them.

**The diagnosis she opens with**: feminist theory's "flight from nature". Nature as feminism's abject, "that which, by being expelled from the 'I,' serves to define the 'I'" (Kristeva 1982: 1-4). **Elizabeth A. Wilson (1998: 15)**: "the body at the center of these projects is curiously abiological". **Lynda Birke (1999: 44-45)** on "the ghost of biology", and on the biological body as "changing and changeable, as transformable", where cells "constantly renew themselves" and bone "is always remodeling". **Tuana (1996: 57)**: "we feminists have been epistemically irresponsible in leaving in place a fixed, essential, material basis for human nature."

**The counter-biology**: **Myra J. Hird, "Naturally Queer" (2004: 85-86, 88)**. "The vast majority of cells in the human body are intersex"; most organisms in four of the five kingdoms do not require sex to reproduce; the schizophyllum "has more than 28,000 sexes". Her conclusion: "we may no longer be certain that it is nature that remains static and culture that evinces limitless malleability."

**Two incidental things worth keeping.** Susan Bordo was once ostracised at a feminist theory conference for uttering the word "material" (Bordo 1998: 88). And Alaimo notes that Mary Austin imagined the desert as "an undomesticated ground for feminist subjects, a lawless place where the landmarks fail, gender unravels, and meanings come undone".

## 5. Chapter 10. Tobin Siebers, "Disability Experience on Trial"

**The chapter that answers the harder version of the first-person objection**, and the reason both gap files have been corrected.

Built on *Tennessee v. Lane* (2004), in which the Supreme Court ruled that states failing to make courtrooms accessible could be sued under Title II of the ADA. George Lane, a wheelchair user summoned on a minor traffic charge, had to crawl up two flights of stairs while the judge and court employees stood at the top and laughed; "On a pain scale from 1 to 10, it was way past 10". When he refused to crawl a second time after lunch he was arrested for failing to appear and jailed. Beverly Jones, a court reporter, had turned down work in twenty-three Tennessee courthouses.

**Against Joan Scott.** Scott's "The Evidence of Experience" (1991: 777-80) holds that using experience as evidence "weakens the critical thrust of histories of difference", "reproduces rather than contests given ideological systems", and that "It is not individuals who have experience, but subjects who are constituted through experience". Siebers's question: "Is the banishment of experience, for example, radical or reactionary?" And his diagnosis of why it happened:

> "One of the legacies of poststructuralism is the desire for absolute critique, one in which the ability to turn critique against itself is valued above all others and critique as such is defined as a process of subtraction in which knowledge claims have fewer and fewer foundations on which to base themselves."

He adds Lyotard's ***différend*** (1988): a situation in which victims are denied the means to demonstrate that they have been wronged, so they are doubly victimised.

**The apparatus argument, in a body.** "When a disabled body enters any construction, social or physical, a deconstruction occurs, a deconstruction that reveals the lines of force, the blueprint, of the social rendering of the building as surely as its physical rendering . . . when a different body appears, the lack of fit reveals the ideological assumptions controlling the space."

**That is Garland-Thomson's misfit, three years before "Misfits" (2011)**, which is held in this folder. And it is a grating argument: the body is the instrument that makes the structure legible. Alongside it, Linda Alcoff (2000: 337): "Identities are indexical entities", "real within a given location".

**Nancy Mairs, and the occupied lane.** Siebers quotes her at length (1996: 54, 59; 1999: 44, 48, 50). *Waist-High in the World*, and "Sex and the Gimpy Girl". Her account of a Pap smear at the Arizona Health Sciences Center: she cannot reach higher than "3" on the elevator buttons so must wait for another passenger; the clinic doors are not automated, so another wait; the counter is so high she cannot see the receptionist to ask for help; after thirty-five minutes a nurse puts her in a windowless cubicle with a standard examining table, although she had specified a model that lowers and tilts. On marginality: it "is never taken to mean that those on the margin occupy a physical space literally outside the field of vision of those in the center".

**This is a first-person disabled writer on the material conditions of being examined, cited in a book held since comps.** `Posthuman_FirstPerson_Gap_Sources.md` called that lane open and unoccupied.

## 6. Chapter 12. Suzanne Bost, "From Race/Sex/Etc. to Glucose, Feeding Tube, and Mourning"

Does the first-person, disability and decolonial lanes at once.

Reads **Anzaldúa's diabetes** and **Moraga's premature son in the neonatal intensive care unit** as the reason their politics became unbounded: "their (often ignored) autobiographical writings about their unraveling bodies explain the increasingly unbounded subject of their politics." Anzaldúa (2000b: 289-90): "I get dizzy and mentally foggy when I'm having a hypo. I lose my equilibrium and fall. Gastrointestinal reflex has me throwing up and having diarrhea. . . . Things like these change your image of yourself, your identity."

**The conflict about whether to put your own body in the text, documented.** Linda Singer, in *Erotic Welfare* (1993: 25), refuses: "Who am I and where am I in writing this . . . ? Why the first person to begin, when the question of identity is at stake?" Her final chapter, on hospitalisation, is the only one in the book not to use "I". Butler's introduction to the posthumous volume overrides that, saying "I cannot write of this text without letting you, the reader, know something of the kind of person she was" (1993: 1). **An author and her editor disagreeing in print about whether the sick body belongs in the argument.**

**Sedgwick** (1993: 12-13) on her own cancer: "an adventure in applied deconstruction"; "How could I have arrived at a more efficient demonstration of the supposed oppositions that structure an experience of the 'self'?"; cancer leaving the body in a "free-fall interpretative panic". And her method line, which is in `METHODS_proposed_alternatives.md`: application should go in both directions.

Also **Lennard Davis, *Bending Over Backwards* (2002: 5)** on disability's "malleable and shaky foundation" as the beginning of a new way of thinking about identity categories; **Wendy Brown, *States of Injury* (1995: 50)**, political spaces that "must be heterogeneous, roving, relatively noninstitutionalized, and democratic to the point of exhaustion"; **Deena González, "Speaking Secrets: Living Chicana Theory" (1998: 46-47, 56)**; and Bost's own line, "Identity politics often protect their boundaries by not analyzing their interiors."

**The Mesoamerican material.** Anzaldúa and Moraga invoke not the Aztec warrior ideal but the fertility goddesses who also represent destruction and shape-shifting, with **Coatlicue** as the model of a corporeality that exceeds individual identity. Corporeal boundary states, pain, bleeding, shape-shifting, were sacred; public rituals centred on puncturing bodies; priests flayed the skins of sacrificial victims and wore them as symbols of regeneration; nagualism let humans externalise their souls and incarnate other beings. Cited through Gonzalo Aguirre Beltrán (1963: 42-43), quoted in Spanish with translation.

Epigraph, from Inga Clendinnen's *The Tiger's Eye*: "Illness granted me a set of experiences otherwise unobtainable . . . I, and therefore you, are unequivocally physical constructs, if spectacularly complicated ones."

## 7. Chapters 9 and 14, the other two first-person accounts

**Catriona Mortimer-Sandilands, "Landscape, Memory, and Forgetting: Thinking Through (My Mother's) Body and Place."** Weaves environmental philosophy, Jane Urquhart's novel *A Map of Glass* (2005), scientific accounts of Alzheimer's, and a personal account of her mother's life with Alzheimer's. Against David Abram, she argues we have not "lost all traces of the environmental physicality of our memories". Her reading of the novel sets memory in "the complex interrelations among bodies, minds, and landscapes", and the landscape is "a site of forgetting as well as remembering".

**One thing to flag.** The novel's central character, Sylvia, has "an unspecified and unnamed 'condition' (probably a form of autism)" and is described as living "the physical, sensuous world in almost unbearable thickness", easily overwhelmed, counting things. **An autistic-coded character read in sensory terms by a non-autistic critic, in 2008, through a novel.** For 1.4 that is a divergence worth recording rather than a source: it is exactly the move Yergeau and Jonker would object to, and it is the only occurrence of autism in the book (count: 1).

**Susan Bordo, "Cassie's Hair."** First person throughout, on learning to care for her adopted biracial daughter's hair. The braids that came home from day care; her friend Annice's indignation on her behalf; "get inside this world, truly inside, or remain a clueless white mom"; the new vocabulary of pink lotion, braid spray, do-rags, tender-headed, and **the kitchen**, "that bit of hair at the back of the neck most prone to kinks and tangles", with Henry Louis Gates (1994: 42) on the kitchen as the one part of the African past that resisted assimilation.

**Its method is material incompetence.** Her fingers are less agile; she has "almost a bodily aversion" to using as much lotion as is needed; her patience runs out and she sends Cassie off with braids that are "only passable". And the conclusion she draws from the failure: "my lack of anxiety over my child's being seen as less than perfectly coiffed . . . was a privilege of my race." **A first-person account where the knowledge arrives through a practice the writer does badly.** For practice-as-research that is a more interesting structure than competence.

## 8. The other seven chapters, read

### Chapter 3. Susan Hekman, "Constructing the Ballast: An Ontology for Feminism"

**The field-mapping chapter, and the one with the most directly usable method proposal in the book.**

Her frame is two publications twenty years apart: Haraway's "Manifesto for Cyborgs" (1985), which set out to deconstruct the discourse/reality dichotomy, and Latour's "Why Has Critique Run Out of Steam?" (2004), which concludes it failed. Hekman's verdict on Haraway is blunt: "it seems fair to conclude that Haraway's project has failed", because feminism went to the discursive pole instead.

**The Latour this chapter supplies is the constructive half**, which Barnwell's 2017 chapter leaves out. Not just that critique ran out of steam, but why and what instead:

> "The question was never to get away from facts but closer to them, not fighting empiricism but, on the contrary, renewing empiricism." (2004: 231)

Plus "a second empiricism", a "stubbornly realist attitude", and the critic redefined "not as one who 'debunks,' but as one who 'assembles'" (2004: 246). And the opening question at 229: "What if explanations resorting automatically to power, society, discourse have outlived their usefulness and deteriorated to the point of now feeding the most gullible sort of critique?"

**Her own proposal: disclosure rather than constitution.** Language "structures how we apprehend the ontological but it does not constitute it." And she does the work of specifying which sense of disclosure, rejecting two and adopting one:

| Rejected | "to uncover, to expose to view" (OED). Too close to modernism, implies getting it right |
| Rejected | Heidegger's sense, which has "a mystical component, the showing forth of Being throughout the ages" |
| **Adopted** | **The intransitive definition: "to show itself, to come to light" (OED)** |

> "Disclosure does not entail uncovering, getting reality right, but bringing to light. Different aspects of reality can be disclosed from different perspectives. One of these aspects is not right and the others wrong, but rather they represent different aspects of the same reality. But, importantly, **it is possible to compare the consequences of the different disclosures** of the same reality."

**This is the position on divergent readings, stated precisely, with an added move.** Readings do not have to agree and none is the correct one, and you can still compare what each discloses by its material consequences. "We cannot appeal to an objective reality to trump the argument. But we have something to argue about." A 2008 published formulation, with an OED definition behind it.

**Two other things from this chapter.**

**Pickering's mangle, which is mess as method nine years before Law.** Andrew Pickering, *The Mangle of Practice* (1995): "Scientists are human agents in a field of material agency which they struggle to capture in machines. Further, human and material agency are reciprocally and emergently intertwined in this struggle . . . the reciprocal tuning of human and material agency, tuning that can itself reconfigure human intentions" (1995: 21). Mangle as noun and verb (23). "the world makes us in the same process by which we make the world" (26). Nature's **resistances** occur "in the boundary of the two realms", neither in the instruments nor in the concepts (92). "mangles mix everything up and produce unpredictable results"; **"mangle realism"**: "how the material world is leaks into and infects our representations of it in a nontrivial and consequential fashion" (183). Pickering is also the source of the **"dance of agency"** that Tuana uses in chapter 6, so he appears twice in this book.

**Barad's sonogram, which is the best apparatus case in the folder.** From Barad's 1998 *differences* article (110-15): fetal imaging makes the early fetus matter, because "it did not exist as matter before the technology could 'see' it", and in the same movement makes it a political actor. "The construction of the fetus as a self-contained, free-floating object under the eye of the scientific observer reinforces this presupposition of the subject's autonomy and the objectivity of the scientific observer." Against Butler (Barad 2001a: 103-5): Butler explains how discourse comes to matter but not **how matter comes to matter**. An imaging instrument applied to a pregnant body that produces both an object and a politics. Add it to the 2.5 cluster.

Also: Barad 2003: 801, "Language has been granted too much power . . . it seems that at every turn lately every 'thing', even materiality, is turned into a matter of language", and the conclusion that "the only thing that does not seem to matter is matter"; Barad 1998: 124 n. 20, the chapter's title source, "a ballast against current tendencies that confuse theorizing with unconstrained play"; Hacking (1999: 35), "The metaphor of social construction once had excellent shock value, but now it has become tired"; Grosz (1994: 191) on the body as **"open materiality"**; Clough (2003: 2), "our investment in epistemological critique is beginning to yield diminishing returns"; Naomi Scheman on photography (1993: 161), "a photo is a visual trace of an act of seeing", used to explain disclosure, which makes a **camera** the model for how concepts work; and **Foucault's "critical ontology of ourselves"** (1984: 50), "conceived as an attitude, an ethos, a philosophical life in which the critique of what we are is at one and the same time the historical analysis of the limits that are imposed on us and an experiment in the possibility of going beyond them."

### Chapter 7. Vicki Kirby, "Natural Convers(at)ions: Or, What if Culture Was Really Nature All Along?", pp. 214-36

The original of chapter 4 of *Quantum Anthropologies* (2011) and of the 2017 collection's title. The three-stage genealogy is now complete and verifiable in this folder.

**A method that is not in the nine.** Kirby quotes Latour (2004b): "Critique as a repertoire is over. It has run out of steam entirely, and now the whole question is, '**how can we be critical, not by distance but by proximity?**'" And she then demonstrates it: "making yourself at home in the very logic of your opponent's argument and showing how the direction of that argument can comprehend a very different set of implications." The whole chapter is an exercise in it, performed on Butler and then on Latour, and it is closer to diffractive reading than any of the nine proposals. She also states the ethics plainly: "the practice of doing critique involves close encounters with another person's way of thinking, with their intellectual commitments and even the temperament and personal idiosyncrasies that animate their writing style."

**Latour's "Circulating Reference: Sampling the Soil in the Amazon Forest"** (in *Pandora's Hope*, 1999) is the other find. The chain from soil crumbling between researchers' fingers to marks on paper: "No step, except one, resembles the one that precedes it, yet in the end, when I read the field report, I am indeed holding in my hands the forest of Boa Vista" (1999: 61). Latour: "here it is no longer a question of reduction but of transubstantiation" (54); "How do we pack the world into words?" (24); "if I can manage to grasp this invariant, this *je ne sais quoi*, I believe, I will have understood scientific reference" (36). **A documented apparatus that turns matter into notation step by step**, which is what a concordance does.

Also: Serres's **"quasi-object"** via Latour (1993b: 261-2), "It is not that there are a few hybrids; it is that there are only hybrids"; and on vocabulary, "the vocabulary of actor-network theory is voluntarily poor. It is not a metalanguage, but an **infralanguage**" (263). And Latour on the little word "both" (2003: 35-6): "How I despise this little 'both' that obtains so cheaply a veneer of depth . . . Never was critique less critical than when accepting as an obvious answer what should be, on the contrary, a source of utter bewilderment."

Kirby's own **superposition** use is here too, in 2008, predating her 2011 and 2017 ones: "The most counterintuitive arguments about the superposition of matter and ideation, concept and object", and later "The superposition of these differences means that any identity is articulated with and by all others." She also applies **"intra-actions"**, in quotation marks with a footnote, to Derridean *différance*. The Butler interview is originally from **Breen et al. 2001: 13**, not from the 2011 book where it is reprinted.

### Chapter 1. Elizabeth Grosz, "Darwin and Feminism"

Argues Darwin may be as productive for feminism as Freud has been, and for the same reason: not because he is unbiased but because he "opened up a new way of thinking". What his work gives: a critique of both essentialism and teleology; "the irreversibility of time within the natural sciences, the centrality of chance"; and a refusal "to assume that the temporal movement forward can be equated with development or progress". She calls him "perhaps the most original thinker of the link between difference and becoming".

**Two things to take.**

**Artificial selection as a model of what an experiment is.** "Rather than being construed as polar opposites, as cultural and natural binaries, natural and artificial selection are regarded as two versions of the same thing . . . The artificial illustrates the natural because it is subjected to its forces and principles though it simplifies it and renders explicit the selective criteria utilized." An apparatus argument in Darwinian terms: the artificial case makes the selective criteria visible.

**Aesthetics as a selective force.** Sexual selection is "an intricate feedback loop" that adds aesthetic criteria and "deviates natural selection through the expression of the will, or desire, or pleasure, of individuals". The peacock is disadvantaged in survival and advantaged in selection. **Set this against Colebrook's chapter, which argues art can turn against beauty.** Two chapters of one book disagreeing about whether beauty is adaptive.

Also: "monstrosities, teratological variations, may be regularly produced, but only those that remain both viable and reproductively successful . . . help induce this proliferation", which touches the monster-and-mother thread. And the Stengers epigraph, that Darwin succeeded "in constituting [living beings] as witnesses to a history whose interest lies in the fact that one does not know a priori what history it is a question of."

### Chapter 2. Claire Colebrook, "On Not Becoming Man"

A critique of the new vitalism from inside it, including "some of the best feminist work on life". Observes that "just as critical theory is recognizing that poststructuralism, far from being a theory of language, was a rigorous philosophy of life, the life sciences themselves, through new imaging technologies, are turning to problems of emotion, affect, distributed cognition, and emergence."

**It closes on a theory of art criticism, and it is the only one in the book.** Art criticism "has always tended to be vitalist and historicist, returning a work to its condition of emergence in order that it might live, for us, again. By contrast, a criticism liberated from the concept of 'man' would acknowledge that fragile materiality of a work that resists comprehension, inclusion, recognition, and interpretation."

**A criticism that attends to what in the work refuses to be understood**, rather than restoring the work to its moment of making. For an arts-based dissertation that is a position worth arguing with. She pairs it with the claim that art "has the capacity to recognize its origin in the lived and, far from striving to maintain itself, turn against beauty, pleasure, and the proliferation of relations", and ends on "the potentiality of nonrealization, of dispersion, of remaining inert and refusing to be oneself that frees matter from the human, through the human."

Note that Colebrook also wrote *Death of the Posthuman* (2014), which Hinton cites in the 2017 collection for Agamben's impotentiality. The refusal-to-actualise argument runs from here to there.

### Chapter 5. Donna Haraway, "Otherworldly Conversations, Terran Topics, Local Terms"

Reprinted from *The Haraway Reader* (2004). Nature as "that which we cannot not desire" (after Spivak): "something we cannot do without but can never 'have'. We must find another relationship to nature besides reification, possession, appropriation, and nostalgia."

**An etymological argument, which makes five in the two Kirby-related books.** Nature as **topos**, a rhetorician's place, "strictly, a commonplace", and she notes that "in seventeenth-century English the 'topick gods' were the local gods, the gods specific to places and peoples". And nature as **trópos**, a trope: "figure, construction, artifact, movement, displacement . . . Faithful to the Greek, as trópos, nature is about turning."

**And a New Mexico fact worth having**: GenBank, the US depository for DNA sequence data, "is located at the national laboratories at Los Alamos, New Mexico, site of the Manhattan Project and a major U.S. weapons laboratory since the Second World War." The archive of human genetic diversity, at the weapons lab.

The chapter's spine is Barbara Noske's *Humans and Other Animals* (1989): the "animal-industrial complex"; animal life-time converted "into round-the-clock production" (17); "animal exploitation cannot be tolerated without damaging the principle of inter-subjectivity" (38); and biology lacking "the methodological equipment to recognize 'things socially and culturally created and which in turn shape the creators'" (86). Haraway's question out of it: "**How do we designate radical otherness at the heart of ethical relating?**" And on the wolf-children literature, Noske's reframing: not whether the children can be "de-animalized", but "what kind of social thing happened when a human child acquired a specific nonhuman socialization?", so that the case is "animal-human communication" rather than the reverse. "None of the partners is the same afterwards."

Epigraphs worth keeping: Robert Young, "Nothing is ultimately contextual; all is constitutive"; Barbara Noske, "Animals are not lesser humans; they are other worlds"; and Naomi Mitchison's *Memoirs of a Spacewoman*, on realising "how much we ourselves are constructed bilaterally on either-or principles. Fish rather than echinoderms . . . It was quite a problem to get through to those radial entities."

### Chapter 11. Michael Hames-García, "How Real Is Race?"

Opens on the epigraph that does the work: the Racial Privacy Initiative submitted to the California Attorney General in 2001, which would have banned the state from classifying anyone by race while explicitly preserving police description of persons and freeing departments from keeping records of the race of those they arrested. "The ballot measure, promoted using liberal, antiracist rhetoric, would have frustrated all attempts to demonstrate discriminatory patterns of surveillance, arrest, or harassment by police."

**A document that abolishes the category and keeps the practice, by removing the records that would prove it.** For anyone who works on what records do and do not hold, that is a clean case.

His argument: "We need creative racial identity projects more than we need philosophical arguments against race." Race "emerges from the **intra-action** of history, culture, economics, and material human bodies", so Barad's term is applied to race here in 2008. Via Castells's "project identities", with the Zapatistas as the example, and Quijano and Mignolo (2002: 245) on coloniality. The listed projects: the Harlem Renaissance, Negritude, Pan-Africanism, Pan-Indigenism, and contemporary Asian American and Chicana/o identities.

### Chapter 13. Elizabeth A. Wilson, "Organic Empathy"

Opens on Gardiner's 1995 anecdote: a feminist meeting that assumed a social constructionist consensus and criticised work as "essentialist", where over meals and breaks several women discussed taking Prozac, some for their children too. "The potential contradiction between such private solutions and the publicly avowed ideology of social constructionism was never voiced" (Gardiner 1995: 501-2).

Her method sentence: "**My interest lies less in the deconstruction of biology than in learning how biology itself deconstructs.**" And she quotes Kirby (2003: 438): biology is "as actively literate, numerate, and inventive as anything we might want to include within Culture."

**A barrier passage that parallels the skin exactly.** The blood-brain barrier is "the single most significant factor limiting drug delivery to the CNS" (Begley 2003: 85); serotonin cannot cross it, so the brain must synthesise its own; SSRIs are small and lipophilic and do cross. But the data "elucidate not simply the defensive nature of the barrier, but also its function as a system of communication with the outside". Tryptophan, the building block, is an essential amino acid and must come from the diet, and a carbohydrate-heavy diet raises insulin, which clears competing amino acids and lets disproportionate tryptophan through (Wurtman et al. 2003). So "the level of serotonin in the brain is dependent on a number of extra-cerebral systems . . . serotonergic activity is a network of relations amongst organs and between biological and cultural systems."

**A selective barrier that leaks in both directions and whose function is communication.** That is the structure of the skin in `PERMEABLE_BODIES_research_notes.md`, and Wilson states the conclusion the skin material does not: "To borrow from Winnicott, there is no such thing as a brain; there is always a brain and another system."

Also: "The physiological itinerary of an antidepressant takes in every organ of the body", including the skin; "the liver and the gut provide the bioaffective tone of depressions"; and her headline claim, that **transference operates in biological as well as psychological registers**, because the two robust findings in the treatment literature are that combined treatment works better and that the strongest predictor of outcome is the quality of the relationship rather than the modality. "It is the transference that cures." Kramer (1993: 103): "We might say that the medication acted like an interpretation in psychotherapy." Her term for the result is **organic empathy**.

Same author as *Gut Feminism* (2015), in the reading plan's get-hold-of list, and as the "antibiologism" argument Davis uses in the 2017 collection.
