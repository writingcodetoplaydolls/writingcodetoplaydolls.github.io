## 2. The thing to know first

**This essay is built on Plato, and it reads matter as derivative.** Its argument runs from Pythagoras through Plato to Kepler, and its claim is that ideal mathematical form pre-exists and that the material world imitates it. Paragraph 7:

> Material things are the imitations, the shadows of ideal real structures.

Paragraph 8, continuing:

> And thus – we would be tempted to continue – these ideal structures are real because and insofar as they are realizing in material events.

This is flagged at the top, and not further down, because of the route by which the essay is likely to enter the project: **through Molderings, who cites it.** A citation to Molderings's note that is never read back to the essay inherits this reading of matter along with it.

**What follows from that is a question left open here**, because it belongs to the project and not to this file: this essay reads matter as the imitation and the shadow of a pre-existing ideal structure, and new materialism reads matter otherwise. The difference is stated, not resolved, and nothing below treats either reading as the correct one.

## 3. Art, explicitly

**The whole essay is addressed to artists.** It was a lecture to the Bayerische Akademie der Schönen Künste. It opens by disclaiming the authority to speak about art at all, paragraph 1:

> When a representative of natural science has to address a session of the Academy of the Fine Arts, he can hardly dare to express opinions about the topic art.

### 3.1 The passage that matters most, where physics after Planck is compared to modern art

This is the most usable set of sentences in the essay for an art dissertation, and the one least likely to be anticipated from the title. Paragraph 28:

> I do not know whether it is permissible to compare the situation of physics in those 25 years after the Planckian discovery – which I still experienced as a young student – to the conditions of contemporary modern art. But I must confess that such a comparison forces itself upon me, time and again. The helplessness before the question what one should do with bewildering phenomena, the affiction over the loss of interconnections which still appeared so convincing – all this dissatisfaction has certainly determined the traits of the two so different realms and epochs in a similar way.

Same paragraph, later:

> I do not see any reason why something similar should not happen one day in art. But one must add the warning: something like that man cannot make, it must happen by itself.

**What is claimed there.** Heisenberg puts the twenty-five years of bewilderment in physics between 1900 and 1925 and the condition of contemporary modern art under one description: helplessness before the phenomena, and grief at the loss of interconnections that had been convincing. He calls it a necessary intermediate stage that cannot be skipped. He then says he sees no reason the resolution should not come in art too, and warns that it cannot be made to happen.

**This is the physicist of the uncertainty relation naming modern art and quantum physics as the same historical condition.** It is also the one place in the essay where he commits to a parallel rather than to an analogy.

### 3.2 Art and science given one and the same process

The mechanism the essay offers for how new form emerges is called the unfolding of abstract structures, and it is introduced for mathematics and art together. Paragraph 17:

> Perhaps at this point one should begin by recalling a phenomenon which can be called the unfolding of abstract structures. It can be clarified through the example of the theory of numbers which has already been mentioned at the beginning, but one can also point to similar processes in the development of art.

The same paragraph gives the art case in full:

> In a similar way, also at the beginning of an art style, for instance in architecture, certain simple basic forms are found, e.g. the semicircle and the square in the Romanesque architecture. Out of these basic forms emerge – in the course of history – new, more complicated, even changed forms which, however, can in some way be conceived as variations on the same theme. Thus, from such basic structures, a new manner, a new style of construction is developed. One has the feeling that the possibilities of development could be detected in these primordial forms already at the beginning. Otherwise it would be hardly understandable that many gifted artists decided quite rapidly to pursue these new possibilities.

**What is claimed there.** A few simple basic forms are laid down at the start of a style, in architecture the semicircle and the square of the Romanesque, and the history of that style is the unfolding of what was already latent in them. Heisenberg says the same of the axioms of arithmetic, of Newtonian mechanics, and of relativity and quantum theory. **One account of historical development, applied to a style of building and to a physics without distinguishing them.**

### 3.3 Method is not the content of the work

Paragraph 29:

> Of course rational thinking and accurate measuring do belong to the work of the investigator of nature – just as hammer and chisel belong to the work of the sculptor. But they are in both cases just tools, not content of the work itself.

### 3.4 The reason he gives for the whole lecture, and the sentence it ends on

Paragraph 29:

> I have described to you this aspect of exact natural science because in it the kinship to the fine arts becomes most clearly visible and because here the misunderstanding can be forstalled which sees science and technology as concerned only with exact observation and rational, discursive thinking.

Paragraph 29, the last sentence of the lecture:

> And let us realize that beauty is – in exact natural science just as in the arts – the most important source of illumination and clarity.

### 3.5 Making as something many people do together

Paragraph 19:

> Incidentally, this process has also – in science as well as in art – an important social and ethical aspect. For many persons can actively participate in it.

The paragraph goes on to the medieval cathedral with its architects and craftsmen, and the next two to the technicians who build an aircraft. **The claim is that a shared underlying form sets a standard against which an individual contribution can be judged, and that this is where the satisfaction of the work comes from.** Whether that bears on the project depends on whether the practice in question is collaborative.

## 4. Knowing that comes before rational formulation

This is the other thread bearing on practice as research, and it runs on three quoted authorities rather than on Heisenberg's own voice.

**Plato, paragraph 8**, the fright at beauty, which the essay returns to twice more:

> In one passage of the Phaedros is the thought expressed: “The soul is frightened, it shudders at the sight of beauty for it feels that something is conjured up in it which has not come to it from the outside through the senses, but has always been present in it, in a profoundly unconscious area

**Kepler, paragraph 21**, on the soul power that recognises proportion:

> It does not think in discursive way, that is deductively – as philosophers do – and does not employ any lofty method.

**Pauli, paragraph 25:**

> The archetypes function as ordering operators and shapers of this world of symbolic images. They act as the sought-for bridge between sense perceptions and ideas and therefore are also a necessary prerequisite for the emergence of a scientific theory.

**What is claimed there.** Recognition of the right form happens before, and not by means of, discursive reasoning; it is unconscious, image-bound and emotionally charged; and it is a necessary condition for a scientific theory to emerge at all. Heisenberg endorses this by quoting it at length and building his answer on it.

**Three cautions about using it.** First, the claim is routed through **Jung's archetypes**, named three times, which is a specific and contested commitment, not a neutral description of intuition. Second, the images are said to be **innate**, which is Platonic reminiscence again in a psychological register. Third, Heisenberg immediately adds a limit, paragraph 27:

> Here are the natural sciences in a happier position than the arts because for science there is an inescapable and inexorable criterion of value from which no piece of research can subtract itself.

**That sentence cuts against practice as research rather than for it**, since it makes an external criterion the thing science has and art does not. It belongs in the reading of the essay whether or not it is convenient.

## 7. My reading, marked as mine

Everything above is what the essay says. This section is mine and is not a finding.

**The essay may be more useful as a problem than as a support.** Its Platonism is not incidental decoration on an otherwise usable argument about art and science; it is the argument. If the essay is cited for the art and science kinship, the ground that kinship stands on in Heisenberg's own text is that art and science both recognise pre-existing ideal form.

**The two strongest pieces of it are the modern art comparison in paragraph 28 and the unfolding of abstract structures in paragraph 17.** Both can be used without taking on the Platonism, because both describe how form develops historically rather than where form comes from.

**One term the essay offers that has not been applied to anything here.** "The unfolding of abstract structures" is Heisenberg's phrase for his own mechanism. It has not been used in this file to describe any material in this project.
