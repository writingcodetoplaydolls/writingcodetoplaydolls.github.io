# Permeable Bodies: Research Notes

Amy Traylor, 28 Oct 2026

## Held thoughts

Stretch marks are a trace of a body that expands and contracts. I can feel my body pushing back against my skin.

The research below follows three lines out from this: the skin as a boundary that records pressure, the electrical body the skin contains, and the ways bodies get inside one another.

## Skin that grows under pressure

A stretch mark records skin meeting force faster than it can rebuild. Dermal elastic fibres let skin stretch and return; when stretching outruns them, they rupture, and local fibroblasts fail to replace the matrix ([Elsedfy 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/7569590)). The marks form perpendicular to the direction of tension, so each one is a line drawn by the force that made it ([BJMP review 2016](https://www.bjmp.org/files/2016-9-3/bjmp-2016-9-3-a919.pdf)).

Pregnancy shows the process at the scale of fibres. In new, red pregnancy striae, Wang and colleagues found the elastic network badly disrupted and replaced by thin, disordered tropoelastin-rich fibrils that persisted after birth without forming working fibres ([Wang et al. 2015](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100028134746_0x000001)). Their follow-up found collagen bundles pulled apart, with fibroblasts sitting beside newly made, disorganized fibrils; the marks mature over months to years into pale, lax bands and affect 50-90% of pregnant women ([Wang et al. 2018](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/bitstream/handle/2027.42/142909/bjd15895.pdf;sequence=1)).

Skin also grows when stretched, and surgeons use this with inflatable expanders placed under the skin. Tracking single cells in stretched mouse skin, Aragona and colleagues showed that stretch pushes one population of epidermal stem cells toward self-renewal while another keeps differentiating, so the tissue enlarges and keeps its barrier ([Aragona et al. 2020](https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/da/publications/mechanisms-of-stretch-mediated-skin-expansion-at-single-cell-reso/)).

Read together, the epidermis answers pressure by making more of itself while the dermis beneath tears and scars. The surface stays continuous and the depth keeps the record. "Pushing back against my skin" has a tissue-level referent: a living layer negotiating pressure from inside, with the stretch mark as its residue.

## Skin as a permeable boundary

The skin's barrier is selective, and it leaks in both directions. Its main seal is the stratum corneum: flattened, dead corneocytes set in stacked lipids, the "bricks and mortar" model associated with Peter Elias, though Schmitt and Neubert trace its first proposal to Michaels and colleagues ([Schmitt & Neubert 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7592940)). They call the model rudimentary: the layer hydrates, shifts its lipid packing, and hosts microbes that one reviewer proposes adding as a third component of the wall ([Bosko 2019](https://jddonline.com/articles/dermatology/S1545961619S0063X)).

What enters is mostly small. Bos and Meinardi argued that a molecule must weigh under about 500 daltons to cross intact skin, reasoning from the fact that common contact allergens and transdermal drugs all fall below that mark ([Bos & Meinardi 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10839713/)). The threshold comes from inference over known compounds, and broken or diseased skin admits more.

What leaves is continuous. People shed on the order of a million particles an hour; in a sanitized chamber, Meadow and colleagues detected each seated occupant's airborne bacteria within 1.5-4 hours and could tell most occupants apart by the mix ([Meadow et al. 2015](https://peerj.com/articles/1258)). Each body extends past its skin as an identifiable cloud of microbes and skin fragments.

## Electrical traces and their containment

Skin contains the body's electricity by resisting it, and every electrical trace taken from a living body is read through that resistance. The stratum corneum insulates; sweat ducts are the main conductive paths through it ([Posada-Quintero & Chon 2020](https://biosignal.media.uconn.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2503/2020/01/01_Posada_2020_Sensors.pdf)). When sympathetic nerves fill the ducts with sweat, conductive channels open through the resistant layer, so the skin's electrical boundary opens and closes with arousal ([Aldosky & Bari](https://core.ac.uk/download/322445323.pdf); [Tronstad et al. 2022](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10143525/1/Tronstad%2Bet%2Bal_2022_Physiol._Meas.pdf)).

Intact skin also holds a charge. The epidermis maintains a voltage across itself; a cut short-circuits it and current flows out of the wound, a phenomenon Emil du Bois-Reymond described about 150 years before the modern work. Zhao and colleagues showed these endogenous fields steer cells into the wound, and that reversing the field drives the wound open ([Zhao et al. 2006](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature04925.pdf)).

What crosses the skin arrives smoothed. ECG and EEG electrodes record sources deep in the body after their currents spread through tissue acting as a volume conductor, which blurs the pattern on its way to the surface ([Ni et al.](https://sci.utah.edu/~macleod/papers/isce00-quan/paper.html); [Clinical Neurophysiology, volume conduction chapter](https://academic.oup.com/book/35514/chapter/305240425)). The heart's magnetic field passes through tissue largely undistorted and can be measured outside the chest at 50-100 picotesla, roughly a millionth of the Earth's field; the first fetal magnetocardiogram, reported in 1974, recorded a child's heart through the mother's body ([Roth 2023](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/23/9/4218)).

Bioelectric pattern can precede form. In frog embryos, Vandenberg, Morrie, and Adams imaged voltage patterns on the surface that mark out the face before it forms; disrupting the ion flux altered both the pattern and craniofacial development ([Vandenberg et al. 2011](https://now.tufts.edu/2011/07/18/face-frog-time-lapse-video-reveals-never-seen-bioelectric-pattern)). Whether an equivalent "electric face" exists in human development is an open question.

Coupling between bodies runs through behavior. Mothers and 3-month-old infants coordinate heart rhythms within lags under a second, and the coordination rises during moments of shared affect and vocal synchrony ([Feldman et al. 2011](https://cris.biu.ac.il/en/publications/mother-and-infant-coordinate-heart-rhythms-through-episodes-of-in-3/)). The channel is gaze, voice, and touch; nothing in this literature supports direct field-to-field transmission between bodies.

## Scent and chemosignals

Bodies change one another through airborne chemistry, though the claim that humans have pheromones has not held up. Wyatt finds no robust bioassay-led evidence for the four steroids most often called human pheromones (androstenone, androstenol, androstadienone, estratetraenol), and names as the most promising lead a secretion from the areolar glands of lactating mothers that prompts sucking in any newborn ([Wyatt 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4375873)). Menstrual synchrony, reported by McClintock in 1971, has failed to replicate; Schank's long-term data showed only chance overlaps, and a 2013 systematic review concluded the evidence warrants doubt ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/do-women-who-live-together-menstruate-together/); [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menstrual_synchrony)).

Chemosignals are a broader category with better support. Women's emotional tears, which have no perceptible smell, lowered men's testosterone and physiological arousal when sniffed, and reduced activity in brain regions tied to sexual arousal ([Gelstein et al. 2011](https://cris.biu.ac.il/en/publications/human-tears-contain-a-chemosignal/)). Sweat collected during fear can induce fear in the person who smells it ([de Groot et al. 2012, via Semin & Farias 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4345820)).

Touch moves the chemistry from one body to another. A handshake transfers skin chemicals; after a same-gender handshake, people more than doubled the time spent sniffing their own right hand, and after a cross-gender handshake they sniffed their left, non-shaking hand more ([Frumin et al. 2015](https://doaj.org/article/492fa932888d409aaf6e43776a03176c)).

Infant odor engages reward circuitry. The body odor of unfamiliar two-day-old newborns activated reward-related brain areas in women, with a stronger dopaminergic response in new mothers than in women who had not given birth ([Lundström et al. 2013](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00597/full)). Odor binds infant and caregiver before language does.

## Microchimerism

Pregnancy leaves living cells of the child inside the mother for decades, in blood, skin, and brain. Bianchi and colleagues found male fetal progenitor cells in 6 of 8 women who had borne sons between 6 months and 27 years earlier ([Bianchi et al. 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8570620/)). Khosrotehrani and colleagues then found male cells in maternal tissues taking on the character of the tissue they settled in ([Khosrotehrani et al. 2004](https://embryo.asu.edu/pages/transfer-fetal-cells-multilineage-potential-maternal-tissue-2004-kiarash-khosrotehrani-et-al)).

The brain and the skin both hold them. Chan and colleagues detected male DNA in 37 of 59 autopsied female brains (63%), across multiple regions; the oldest woman carrying it was 94 ([Chan et al. 2012](https://www.fredhutch.org/en/news/releases/2012/09/men-on-the-mind--study-finds-male-dna-in-womens-brains.html)). In healed caesarean scars, Mahmood and O'Donoghue found male presumed-fetal cells in the epidermis of women whose first child was a boy, expressing collagen types I and III ([Mahmood & O'Donoghue 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4199806)); in mice, fetal cells travel to maternal skin wounds and lay down collagen ([Seppanen et al. 2013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3641113/)).

The relationship is mixed. Boddy and colleagues frame fetal cells as cooperating with the mother where help costs little, such as tissue repair, and competing where resources are contested, with autoimmunity as one possible cost ([Boddy et al. 2015](https://news.asu.edu/node/65321)). Traffic runs both ways: maternal cells persist in children and help educate the fetal immune system ([Bianchi et al. 2021](https://vufind-test.katalog.k.utb.cz/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c495t-79cd35ccc36af98e3e0258cb0e3c3e8297bacb7b792ce84ebd787214f7325d593)).

On partners. The documented route for a partner's genes into a woman's body runs through the fetus, which carries half the father's genome. Male DNA also appears in about 13% of healthy women who never bore a son ([Lambert et al. 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1755528)) and in 21% of women without sons in a larger sample ([Yan et al. 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16084184/)); proposed sources include unrecognized miscarriage, a vanished twin, an older brother via the mother's circulation, transfusion, and intercourse. One study found male DNA in 13.6% of girls and estimated that intercourse could account for at most about half of that, so other sources must exist ([Müller et al., Chimerism](https://www.informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/19381956.2016.1218583)). Intercourse remains a hypothesis; the child is the established carrier of the partner into the mother.

## Shared microbes

People who live together converge microbially, and most strongly on the skin. Sampling 159 people and 36 dogs across 60 households, Song and colleagues found that household members, couples above all, shared more microbes than people from different homes, with the largest effect on skin; dog owners shared more skin microbes with their own dogs than with other dogs ([Song et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3628085)). Occupants' microbes show up across a new home within days ([Lax et al. 2014, cited in Meadow et al. 2015](https://peerj.com/articles/1258)).

Kissing moves microbes in bulk. A ten-second intimate kiss transfers about 80 million bacteria, and the similarity of a couple's saliva tracks how often and how recently they kiss ([Kort et al. 2014](https://biomedcentral.com/about/press-centre/science-press-releases/17-nov-2014)). Tongue communities were more alike within couples regardless of kissing frequency, which points to shared diet and daily habits.

## Threads for practice

This research describes a body whose edge depends on the instrument used to find it. Y-chromosome PCR finds a son in his mother's brain; 16S sequencing finds a couple's skins growing alike; a voltage dye finds a face before the face exists; a magnetometer finds the heart outside the chest. Each instrument works as a grating in the sense of the comps definition, co-created with material, and each produces a pattern of the body the others miss.

Theory doing specific work:

- Stacy Alaimo. Her term is trans-corporeality (hyphenated, in *Bodily Natures*, 2010), for the movement of substances across bodies and environments. The 500-dalton threshold, the microbial cloud, and the kiss give that movement measured dimensions.
- Karen Barad. The phenomenon is the basic unit, and the boundary between a body and its outside is drawn within a phenomenon by a specific apparatus. Skin-conductance electrodes, magnetometers, and Y-FISH are such apparatuses, and each draws the cut in a different place.
- Aryn Martin (STS, York University). She tracks how microchimerism researchers describe fetal cells through geopolitical metaphors of foreignness, invasion, and migration, and how the phenomenon strains immunology's self/non-self categories ([Martin 2010, Body & Society](https://dc2.philarchive.org/rec/MARMIT-8)). Her "Your Mother's Always With You" reads fetomaternal microchimerism through material feminism and critiques framings that naturalize mother-child bonding and exclude fathers and non-genetic parents ([Martin, Resources for Feminist Research](https://profiles.laps.yorku.ca/publications/151745/)). Records give this piece as both 2010 and 2011; check the issue's copyright page.

Material leads:

- Stretch marks as drawings of force. They run perpendicular to tension and keep in the dermis a record the epidermis smooths over.
- The scar as a shared repair site. Cells presumed to come from a son sit in the epidermis of his mother's caesarean scar, making collagen.
- Skin conductance as live material. The skin's electrical permeability shifts with arousal and can be sensed in real time.
- The microbial cloud as duration. A seated body becomes identifiable in its air within 1.5-4 hours, a timescale an installation can hold.
- One body read through another. Fetal magnetocardiography records a child's heart through the mother.
- The partner arrives through the child. The documented path for a partner's genes into a mother's tissues is the fetus.
