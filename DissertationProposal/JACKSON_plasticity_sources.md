## 1. The word appears exactly twice in the introduction, and the pair is the argument

**First, the definition (p. 3):**

> "I reinterpret Enlightenment thought not as black 'exclusion' or 'denied humanity' but rather as the violent imposition and appropriation, inclusion and recognition, of black(ened) humanity in the interest of **plasticizing** that very humanity, whereby 'the animal' is one but not the only form blackness is thought to encompass. **Plasticity is a mode of transmogrification whereby the fleshy being of blackness is experimented with as if it were infinitely malleable lexical and biological matter, such that blackness is produced as sub/super/human at once, a form where form shall not hold: potentially 'everything and nothing' at the register of ontology.**"

**Second, the thesis of chapter 1, on Morrison:**

> "*Beloved* identifies the violation of slavery not in an unnatural ordering of man and beast but in **its transmogrification of human form and personality as an experiment in plasticity and its limits therein**, while also exploring what potential opacity holds for a generative disordering of being."

So the two words are not synonyms in her usage. **Plasticity is the imposed condition; transmogrification is what gets done under it.** The experiment, and the form that will not hold.

The chapter is **"Losing Manhood: Plasticity, Animality, and Opacity in the (Neo)Slave Narrative,"** read across Douglass's 1845 *Narrative*, his speech "Kindness to Animals," and Morrison's *Beloved*.

## 2. Where the content of the definition comes from

Not from the plasticity literature. From a historian of the Middle Passage.

> "**Plasticity in *Becoming Human* describes what Stephanie Smallwood**, in her study of the Middle Passage and slavery, **identifies as 'an enduring project of the modern Western world': the use of black(ened) flesh for 'probing the limits up to which it is possible to discipline the body without extinguishing the life within'** (36)."

> Smallwood, Stephanie E. (2007). *Saltwater Slavery: A Middle Passage from Africa to American Diaspora*. Harvard University Press.

"Probing the limits up to which it is possible" is the operative phrase. The definition is a description of an experimental procedure.

## 3. The lineage she names and then refuses

In one sentence, immediately after the Smallwood quotation:

> "**'Plasticity' has been, as concept and thematic, taken up by a range of thinkers including Hegel, Lévi-Strauss, Darwin, and most notably Catherine Malabou. I distinguish my concept from these alternatives in chapter 1.**"

| Named | Where she deals with it |
|---|---|
| **Hegel** | ch. 1, and 26 mentions in the introduction: "the African," ahistorical, barred from spirit, imprisoned by immediacy |
| **Lévi-Strauss** | ch. 1 |
| **Darwin** | ch. 1 |
| **Catherine Malabou** | ch. 1, named as the most significant alternative |
| **Kyla Schuller, *The Biopolitics of Feeling*** | dealt with in the introduction itself, at length, on **impressibility** |

On Schuller, the distinction she draws:

> Schuller argues "the conception of life's plasticity was grounded in the notion of mutable inheritance rather than determinism, and that somatic potential was qualified by purported degrees of binary sex differentiation... By comparison, black(ened) people appeared to be inert and undifferentiated, in other words, excessive to the domain of sexual difference."
>
> "In contrast, **the concept of plasticity in *Becoming Human* indexes a mode of domination that conditions the discourse and practices of optimization**... by suggesting that racial slavery fleshed out its imagination and provided the experimental means for exploring the possibilities and boundaries of the kind of optimization Schuller elucidates. **Plasticity's telos, I argue, is not the optimization of life per se but the fluidification of 'life' and fleshly existence.**"

And the full positive statement:

> "**Plasticity is a praxis that seeks to define the essence of a black(ened) thing as infinitely mutable**, in antiblack, often paradoxical, sexuating terms as a means of hierarchically delineating sex/gender, reproduction, and states of being more generally."

## 5. The sentence that places Barad and Alaimo, and subordinates them

This is the one with the most direct bearing on the proposal, because it names transcorporeality and intra-action together and then says they do not go far enough.

> "With sociogeny, Wynter joins other critics of nature-culture binarism, perhaps most notably **Haraway's natureculture**, which has been recently extended by ecofeminist and feminist materialist conceptions such as **Samantha Frost's 'bioculture,' Stacy Alaimo's 'trans-corporeality,' and Karen Barad's 'entanglement' and 'intra-action.'** **But Wynter raises the stakes of these critiques** by arguing that affect and desire are determinant of both nature and culture as their coproduction (matter and meaning) is given dynamic expression by biocentrism's raciality, **which is to say our studied critiques of nature-culture oppositions and the phenomenon itself are inside of the economies of affect and desire generated by raciality.**"

Barad appears once in the whole introduction, and it is here, inside a list that Wynter is said to exceed. Alaimo appears once, in the same list.

## 6. The passage on form, which is where scale enters

> "black(ened) people are... cast as **sub, supra, and human simultaneously** and in a manner that puts being in peril because the operations of simultaneously being **everything and nothing** for an order (human, animal, machine, for instance) constructs black(ened) humanity as **the privation and exorbitance of form**. Thus the demand placed on black(ened) being is **not that of serialized states nor that of the in-between nor partial states but a statelessness that collapses a distinction between the virtual and the actual**, abstract potential and situated possibility, whereby the abstraction of blackness is enfleshed via an **ongoing process of wresting form from matter** such that raciality's materialization is that of a **dematerializing virtuality**."

Related, two pages earlier, she names the stake in exactly the vocabulary of new materialism:

> "Such a frame raises the stakes of recent feminist materialism's inquiry into both the inter(intra)actional relations of discursivity and materiality as well as **the gendered politics of hylomorphism, or the form-matter distinction**."

And the chapter-2 object is a scale argument: Hopkinson's *Brown Girl in the Ring* read against **"Heidegger's still highly influential thought"** and its **tripartite scale of human, animal and stone**, "scales of world among humans, animals, and objects."

## 9. The connection that was already in the folder

**Yergeau's chapter 2 ends with a section headed "Coda: Practicing Plasticity."** It is an argument about ABA, neuroplasticity and stimming, run on Malabou.

Yergeau quotes **Malabou, *What Should We Do with Our Brain?* (2008), p. 5**:

> "**plasticity is also the capacity to annihilate the very form it is able to receive or create.** We should not forget that *plastique*, from which we get the words *plastiquage* and *plastiquer*, is an explosive substance made of nitroglycerine and nitrocellulose, capable of causing violent explosions. We thus note that **plasticity is situated between two extremes: on the one side the sensible image of taking form (sculpture or plastic objects), and on the other side that of annihilation of all form (explosion).**"

And then:

> "**Stims and tics explode off the neuroqueer body.**"

> "We are Malabou's *plastique*, ready to detonate."

Her complaint about ABA is that it takes only half the concept:

> "plasticity in aba discourse is halved, focused almost exclusively on **how the neuroqueer can be shaped rather than how the neuroqueer shapes**"

That section's closing note (n. 159) cites **Erin Manning, *Relationscapes*, pp. 16 and 6.**

So plasticity arrives at this project twice, from two directions that do not meet in the literature: through **Malabou to Yergeau to stimming to Manning**, which is already in the folders, and through **Smallwood to Jackson to transmogrification**, which was not until today. Jackson is explicit that the second is not the first, and says so in chapter 1, which is not in this PDF.
