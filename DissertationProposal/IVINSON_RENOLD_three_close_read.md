## 4. The ticks passage, which is the argument

This is the most important thing in the three papers for the proposal, and it is not what the papers are arguing.

They see the involuntary movement clearly and take it seriously as evidence:

> "In the pauses between exercises, we observed the girls' jerks, ticks and jittery moves such as constant a-rhythmic touching as they pulled their T-shirts over their bottoms, little motions of dragging their hands through their hair and patting their heads and thighs... These body-forming ticks seemed to reach beyond any one girl as if belonging to a collective, embodied memory."

Then the choreographer's own account of her practice, quoted from the video review:

> "To develop clarity of movement, it would be like creating clear lines on a piece of paper. When a person is trying to move, the body is wrapped in habits, so you don't see the clearly defined lines and the audience also does not see it... it is about trying to acknowledge them and then dissipate the busi-ness... the ticks and habits detract from that."

And the authors go with it:

> "By making us commit to a gesture, Jên encouraged us to become like clear lines formed by purpose, not habit."

**The tick is read as evidence and then removed.** The arc of both papers is from habit to intention, from jitter to "clear lines," from unconscious to "mindful movement," ending in Cadi's two-and-a-half minute improvised dance as the achievement. The repetitive movement is the diagnostic; the cure is intentionality.

A method that takes stimming as method does not run that way. The repetition is not the symptom to be dissipated on the way to purposeful movement; it is the thing doing the work. That is a clean, citable, non-hostile difference, and it is visible in their own quoted words rather than something to be inferred.

Two cautions on using it. First, the person doing the dissipating is the choreographer, describing a craft standard for legible performance, not Ivinson and Renold making a theoretical claim; the paper reports her practice. Second, their own framing is liberatory and about girls who had stopped moving, so the argument should be that a different starting point produces a different method, not that they got it wrong.

Also worth noting: **none of the three uses the word "stimming," and none uses "proprioception."** The proposal uses proprioception seven times. These are adjacent texts in a different vocabulary, not precedents.

## 5. What each one gives, specifically

### 2019, "Anticipating the more-than": prehension, and art as "the way"

The concept spine, with sources attached:

> "We use the term prehension to signal knowledge that comes from the past that is 'known-felt' by young people, yet not available to conscious awareness or articulation."

Art, after Manning, is taken from the medieval sense of art as **"the way"**, so that creative process is "always on its way" rather than a technique applied to a subject. And then the sentence that matters for the anthropocentrism problem:

> "This is not about deploying pre-conceived arts-based methods for 'data' that is waiting to be captured."

Empirically: a tweet from a group of teen girls, a mind map with the line "nearly all of us cry at night," a residential adventure weekend the girls chose (zip-wire, giant swing, rock climbing), and arts-based interventions interleaved with the physical ones, producing the "run(a)way," the "anti-body-chair" and "body-forming silks." The paper ends on Morlais Mountain, at a cliff where three young men had attempted suicide and where the girls had once physically restrained a friend from jumping, and the authors read that struggle back into the history of men dragging bodies from collapsed tunnels.

The historical passage in it is also usable on its own: nineteenth-century "basket girls" as young as six working underground, pit lasses harnessed to wagons, and a commissioner's 1854 testimony that the sight of "young females, dressed like boys in trowsers, crawling on all fours, with belts and chains" was disgusting. They read this as a queering of labour obscured by the nostalgic image of the male labouring body.

### 2021, "What More Do Bodies Know?": method born from failure

The opening is a methodological failure, stated plainly:

> "We started with a problem encountered during conventional interviewing in an ex-mining community in south Wales when some teen girls struggled to speak."

The girls slumped across desks; their bodies "refused to engage with our research task." So the method changed: a four-day improvisation workshop, three cameras on tripods, and afterwards video watched with the choreographer, slowed down and sped up, to find micro-gestures.

Five cameos: **room dancing, the hold, the wiggle, the leap, the dance of the not-yet.**

"The hold" is arms folded across the stomach, which they read through Esther Bick's "second skin." "The wiggle" is a barely perceptible hip movement in one girl that the choreographer mimics and exaggerates until it "spread like a contagion across the room."

Two technical points worth taking. They **participated in the workshops themselves** rather than observing, citing Lefebvre's rhythmanalyst who "must first listen to his own body," and they say outright that this was how they could feel the weight of the movement repertoires. And the analysis is **done with the practitioner**, watching footage with the choreographer and using her commentary as part of the account rather than as data.

They also use ***agencement* untranslated**, glossing it as "comings together" (one occurrence). The proposal has 113. Their gloss is casual and does nothing with the etymology, so section 2.5's argument is not pre-empted.

### 2020, "Moving with the folds": one gesture, read across scales

> "The chapter focuses on a gut holding mannerism, observed in an improvised movement workshop with teen girls living in an ex-industrial town in south Wales (UK), as a vantage point from which to explore what more the gesture might be telling us."

Mannerism 27, transdisciplinary 74, speculative 48. The method is to take a single involuntary gesture and treat it as a fulcrum, then unfold outward (hall, school, town, community, geology of the coal beds, the cosmos) and inward (corporeal movement, psycho-dynamic experience, the organ itself).

The concept of mannerism is taken from **Leibniz as read by Deleuze, *The Fold: Leibniz and the Baroque* (1993)**. The question they put to it is not interpretive but ontological:

> "The mannerism might have been experienced as a reassuring feedback loop of self on self confirming a presence, a beingness, an existence. What was the fold reassuring a girl of? Was it to reassure her that she still existed or belonged?"

And the stated political purpose:

> "We don't want to settle matter; we want to unsettle matters."

This is the nearest published model for taking a repetitive bodily act as the unit of a whole study.

## 6. On the Fold, and on Merleau-Ponty

The chapter routes the Fold through **Leibniz, Deleuze, and the Baroque** (Baroque twice, Leibniz throughout). It does not mention Merleau-Ponty at all: zero occurrences, and zero for "chiasm" and "flesh." Art appears four times, in passing.

So the actuation of the Fold through Merleau-Ponty and through art is not in this text. If that line is going to be made it has to be sourced elsewhere, and two places in the folder already hold the material:

- **Ravisankar 2019** carries Merleau-Ponty 211 times and Heidegger 181 across an entire chapter, with no Leibniz and no Fold. It is the phenomenological half with the Deleuzian half missing.
- Deleuze's *The Fold* is itself a book about the Baroque, so the art lineage is internal to the source rather than added to it.

Neither *The Fold* nor Merleau-Ponty's *The Visible and the Invisible* is in the corpus in any form.

## 7. On the gut, the brain, and Star Trek

Three separate facts, stated as facts:

**The gut.** The chapter's gut theory is **Elizabeth A. Wilson's "thinking gut"**, cited 17 times, from **Wilson, E. A. (2015). *Gut Feminism.* Duke University Press.** The chapter quotes her on disciplinary narrowness: "the social sciences are too monochromatic in their ambitions." **Wilson's book is not in the corpus**, in any form, and nothing else in 804 readable documents works the gut as a thinking organ. The chapter uses no physiological vocabulary at all: enteric, serotonin, vagus, microbiome and "second brain" all return zero.

**Star Trek.** The folder's Star Trek text is **Porter, C. (2013). "'Engaging' in Gender, Race, Sexuality and (dis)Ability in Science Fiction Television through *Star Trek: The Next Generation* and *Star Trek: Voyager*."** MA, Women's Studies, University of Ottawa. 41,764 words. Counts: Borg 219, disability 108, cyborg 42, embodiment 42, autism 36, brain 2, **gut 0**, Deleuze 0, Barad 0. It is a feminist disability media studies thesis. It holds the Star Trek and the autism, and it does not hold the gut, the brain or the new materialism.

**The brain.** Nothing in the corpus joins gut and brain. The join would have to come from Wilson 2015, which is where the chapter gets it.

So of the three terms, one has a named source that is missing from the folder, one has a text in the folder that does not do that work, and the third has nothing. That is a gap list, not a verdict on the grouping, which is yours.

## 8. Three honest cautions

**They are not art practice as research.** "Arts-based" appears 6, 1 and 0 times; "practice as research" and "research-creation" appear zero times in all three, against 76 in the proposal. They are post-qualitative social scientists who commission artists (a choreographer, two filmmakers). The artist is a collaborator; the researchers are not the makers. The proposal's position, where the researcher is the artist, is structurally different and that difference carries the anthropocentrism problem with it in a way theirs does not.

**The subject is always someone else's child.** All three are research with young people in a community the researchers do not come from. The first-person problem the proposal is built around does not arise for them in the same form, because the body being read is never the reader's. The 2021 paper comes closest, since they danced too, but even there the analysis is of the girls.

**"Speculation" is doing heavy lifting.** 16, 26 and 48 occurrences, and the 2021 paper's own footnote concedes the method "does not attempt to gather data." Their claims about intergenerational transmission into girls' gestures are offered as speculation throughout, with "we speculate" repeated as a hedge. That is honest, and it is also a vulnerability a committee could press. Anything borrowed from them should be borrowed with the hedge attached.
