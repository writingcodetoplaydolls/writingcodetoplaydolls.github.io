# Yergeau, "Coda: Practicing Plasticity" (close read)

M. Remi Yergeau, *Authoring Autism: On Rhetoric and Neurological Queerness*
(Durham: Duke University Press, 2018). The section is the closing part of
chapter two, "intervention," pp. 128-134. Notes 138-160, pp. 242-243.

"Coda" here is Yergeau's own printed section title. Every chapter in the book
ends with one: "Coda: The Primacy of Meaning" (ch. 1), "Coda: Practicing
Plasticity" (ch. 2), "Coda: Elliptic Gazes" (ch. 3), "Coda: Revisiting Autism
and Rhetoric" (ch. 4).

---

## 1. Where it sits

Chapter two is on Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA). The sections before this
one are headed "Practicing Recovery" and "Practicing Surveillance." This one
closes the sequence. The chapter's running argument: ABA does not erase
neuroqueerness, it overwrites it, and it succeeds because its subjects are
primed to self-check, self-regulate and self-doubt.

---

## 2. The argument, in sequence

**p. 128.** Neuroplasticity is named as "ABA's newest rhetorical truism,
wherein ABA changes the brain." The opening citation is clinical: Jocelyn
LeBlanc and Michela Fagiolini, "the developing brain is remarkably malleable,
capable of restructuring synaptic connections in response to changing
experiences" (n. 138). The challenge is posed: "if autistic brains are rigid
where allistic brains are flexible, then how might rewiring take place? And
thus, Lovaas reenters the fold: repetition, intensity, work as life."

**p. 128.** The limit of the claim: ABA "does not claim to transform the
autistic brain into a nonautistic brain." What it claims to build is "neural
pathways that result in normative-seeming behavioral profiles: we are not
in-wiring straightness or allism, but are rather in-wiring a masquerade of
straightness and allism. Passing has become a kind of cognitive-behavioral
therapy."

**p. 129. The halving.** This is the hinge of the whole section:

> "The plastic, Catherine Malabou tells us, both gives and receives shape. As
> with rhetoricity, plasticity in aba discourse is halved, focused almost
> exclusively on how the neuroqueer can be shaped rather than how the
> neuroqueer shapes (and fucks, and defies, and writhes)."

Plasticity in ABA is a one-way verb. The same structural move Yergeau makes
about rhetoricity (autistics are granted a "demi-rhetoricity," the receiving
half) is made about plasticity.

**p. 129. Skinner's pigeons.** The bowling pigeons are the illustration of
shaping: "we decided to reinforce any response which had the slightest
resemblance to a swipe." Shaping is "a series of approximations," each
differentially reinforced, "halving behavioral distances until they reach
target behaviors." Yergeau names this **Zeno's paradox** and asks what lets
the behaviorist avert it: motivation plus environmental prosthesis.

**pp. 130-132. The two existing rhetorical approaches to plasticity.** Set as
a mirrored pair, and the formulation is quotable:

| | |
|---|---|
| Davi Johnson Thornton, *Brain Culture* | **the rhetoric of plasticity** |
| Christine Skolnik, "Rhetoric and the Plastic Brain" | **the plasticity of rhetoric** |

Thornton: the brain as "the residence of subjectivity, identity, and free
will"; self-help literature quantifying and pathologizing agency; the
neoliberal productive subject. Yergeau's own addition is **"a kind of reverse
Zeno's paradox"**: neurological improvability "can always double, can always
expand, yet can never exceed." Thornton quoted: brain-based therapy discourse
"individualizes social problems ... and articulates social norms as natural,
biologically and scientifically true" (n. 147).

Skolnik: "doing rhetoric is tantamount to changing one's brain. Every
utterance, motion, and interaction alters our neurochemistry." Then the aside:
"Even in reading this book, your brain is physically changing. (My dearest
hope is, upon reading this book, you will come away from it somewhat more
impaired than when you began.)"

Attached to Skolnik: Anne McGuire's **"developmentalism"**, which supplies the
urgency of early intervention. LeBlanc and Fagiolini call autism a "critical
period disorder." ABA's preferred subjects are "those whose brains are most
acutely shapeable."

**pp. 131-132. The identity turn.** "To claim that I am autistic is one thing;
it is another to claim that autism is my selfhood." Then the reversal: the
clinic itself names affect, intent, concept of self, concept of others,
empathy, sensation, cognition, motor coordination, mental processing,
interests and hobbies, relationality, communication. "If these are the items
my doctor terms autism, how am I not to say that autism is me?"

**pp. 132-133.** Autism "supposedly resists plasticity, at least in ABA
discourse." Lifespan intervention, NIMH funding, the figure of the child.
Victoria Pitts-Taylor on plasticity heralded as "neuronal liberation."

**p. 133. The explosion.** Malabou again, quoted at length from *What Should
We Do with Our Brain?* p. 5:

> "But it must be remarked that plasticity is also the capacity to annihilate
> the very form it is able to receive or create. We should not forget that
> plastique, from which we get the words plastiquage and plastiquer, is an
> explosive substance made of nitroglycerine and nitrocellulose, capable of
> causing violent explosions. We thus note that plasticity is situated between
> two extremes: on the one side the sensible image of taking form (sculpture
> or plastic objects), and on the other side that of annihilation of all form
> (explosion)."

Yergeau's application: "Stims and tics explode off the neuroqueer body;
sensory overload can propel autistics into meltdown and self-injury, and
sometimes gravely so."

**p. 133. The refusal to make it pretty.** Stated flatly:

> "In relating these examples, I do not mean to suggest that autistic plastique
> is always of necessity positive, shiny, or cheery. Rather, I mean to suggest
> that plasticity signals rhetoricity in all of its potentials, causes, and
> directions: it is the unfolding of being and coming-to-being; it is the
> precondition and potential of what might become, in all of the infinite
> variance of autistic becoming."

James C. Wilson is cited for self-injury, "even self-injury that can result in
retinal detachment or death," as "a rhetorical act, worthy of our attention
and ethical deliberation."

**p. 133. Manning.** "In like kind, Erin Manning tells us that 'pure plastic
rhythm is dynamic form in potential,' that the plastic signals a
'becoming-body that is a sensing body in movement, a body that resists
pre-definition.' These are the entelechies, embodied schematics, and time
scales toward which the neuroqueer strive."

**p. 134. Close.** Dawn Prince-Hughes: autism "is simply being human, but
without the skin." Then the last line of the chapter: **"Oo. Oooo. Let us
queerly practice."**

## 3. The "oo" that the section turns on

The sentence on p. 133 reads: "The rhetorics of plasticity and self-governance
in aba compel the neuroqueer to drop the **nq** in favor of a nonphonetic
**oo**."

This is set up earlier in the same chapter, in "Practicing Recovery." **oo** is
the clinical abbreviation for **optimal outcomes**, from Deborah Fein et al.'s
study of thirty-four "oo children" with "a clear documented history of asd"
who no longer met diagnostic criteria. Yergeau then writes:

> "I am tempted to suggest that, in an act of neuroqueer defiance, readers
> sound out the oo abbreviation of optimal outcomes as a self-stimulatory
> ooooo, and repeat the refrain loudly and impassionedly, preferably while in
> a public space. Ooo, oooo, ooooo!"

So the ending is not a flourish. The abbreviation for the cured child is
re-voiced as a stim. "nq" (neuroqueer) appears exactly once in the book, in
that one sentence, as the thing being traded away.

## 6. What sits behind Yergeau's Manning quote

Yergeau cites *Relationscapes* pp. 16 and 6 for "pure plastic rhythm."

In Manning's own text, **"pure plastic rhythm" is Umberto Boccioni's term**,
not Manning's. Manning, p. 16:

> "The body-room series opens the way for thinking the 'pure plastic rhythm'
> of bodies. For Boccioni, 'pure plastic rhythm' is not 'the construction of
> bodies, but the construction of the action of bodies' (1964, 48). ... To
> sculpt movement is to prolong a spiral potential that is already inherent to
> matter. This prolongation of the spiral is what Boccioni calls dynamism, or
> dynamic form. Pure plastic rhythm is dynamic form in potentia."

And p. 6:

> "Throughout *Relationscapes*, I refer to bodies as pure plastic rhythm. I
> propose that we move toward a notion of a becoming-body that is a sensing
> body in movement, a body that resists predefinition in terms of subjectivity
> or identity, a body that is involved in a reciprocal reaching-toward that
> in-gathers the world even as it worlds."

Boccioni appears 45 times in *Relationscapes*. Manning's bibliography lists
three Boccioni items, all Futurist sculpture texts: "Plastic Dynamism 1913"
and "Technical Manifesto of Futurist Sculpture 1912," both in Apollonio ed.,
*Futurist Manifestos* (1970), and "Futurist Sculpture," in Herbert ed.,
*Modern Artists on Art* (1964), pp. 50-57.

So the plasticity that reaches Yergeau through Manning arrives from Futurist
**sculpture**, by way of Bergson and Simondon, and not from Malabou's
neuroscience. Two different plasticities meet on p. 133 without being
introduced to each other.

## 8. Where this meets Jackson

Both Yergeau and Jackson use plasticity to name an **imposed** condition, and
both then name a second operation performed under it.

| | Yergeau 2018 | Jackson 2020 |
|---|---|---|
| plasticity is imposed by | ABA, behaviorism, the clinic | antiblack thought, "ontologized plasticity" |
| the half that is granted | being shaped | being made infinitely malleable, "sub, supra, and human simultaneously" |
| the half that is denied | shaping | self-determination of form |
| the operation under it | plastique, explosion, the stim | **transmogrification** |
| the source behind it | Malabou, *What Should We Do with Our Brain?*, p. 5 | Smallwood, *Saltwater Slavery*, p. 36 |

The symmetry is in the structure, not in the lineage. Jackson explicitly
distances her concept from Malabou's, and says so in her introduction, but she
does the distinguishing in her chapter 1, which is still not in hand.

   MIT Press, **2012**. The copy in the folder is the **2009** edition. Pages

3. **Manning's own page reference.** Manning gives the Boccioni quote as
   "(1964, 48)", but her bibliography lists the 1964 Boccioni item as pp.
   50-57 of Herbert ed., *Modern Artists on Art*. Page 48 falls outside that
   range. Unresolved.

## 10. Things I noticed (mine, not tags)

Kept separate so nothing here lands on the material itself.

- The halving figure runs through the section twice: Zeno's paradox for ABA
  shaping, then "a kind of reverse Zeno's paradox" for neoliberal
  improvability. Both are halving without arrival.
- The "oo" is a single mark that is both the clinical abbreviation and a stim,
  depending on who voices it.
- The Boccioni genealogy puts a sculptor at the base of the plasticity Yergeau
  uses for stims. That is an art-practice lineage, not a neuroscience one.
