## 1. What the article sets out to do, in its own words

The abstract states the charge:

> autoethnographers run the risk of simply replacing one privileged center with another, making similarly narrow claims to truth, authority, and authenticity as objectivism: autoethnography has exchanged transcendency for transparency.
>
> (printed p.299, PDF p.1)

The disclaimer immediately after it matters as much as the charge, because it rules out a reading of this article as hostile to the genre:

> We are not interested in leveling similar previous critiques against autoethnography, critiques that position autoethnography as, for example, non-analytic and an abuse of privilege (Delamont, 2007), self-indulgent (Sparkes, 2002), and “irreverent, self-absorbed, sentimental, and romantic” (Ellis & Bochner, 2000).
>
> (printed p.299, PDF p.1)

And the conclusion repeats it:

> our aim is not to dismiss autoethnography, nor to abandon it for more so-called rigorous forms of qualitative inquiry.
>
> (printed p.314, PDF p.16)

What they want instead of more reflexivity:

> we argue that autoethnographers might question what they ask of voice (or the narrative “I”), confront what they hear and how they hear (their own privilege and authority in listening and telling), and deconstruct why one story is told and not another.
>
> (printed p.300, PDF p.2)

## 2. Their account of what autoethnography assumes

The account is built almost entirely on Carolyn Ellis and Art Bochner. Ellis is named 38 times in the body, Bochner 20. Their summary of the assumption:

> The first person “I” is unproblematically incited to evoke a narrative that is used to make meaning of experience (Bochner & Ellis, 1996), an “easy sense” (Mazzei, in press) that fails to transgress what we are accustomed to hearing, knowing, and naming.
>
> (printed p.300, PDF p.2)

The phrase "easy sense" is credited to "Mazzei, in press", which is Mazzei's own chapter in the forthcoming book. The same phrase later becomes the title of the 2014 article already in this folder, `mazzei-2014-beyond-an-easy-sense-a-diffractive-analysis.pdf`.

## 3. Experience under poststructuralism

The move is from phenomenology, where experience is the origin of knowledge, to poststructuralism, where it is not. Fuss carries the hinge:

> Experience is never as unified, as knowable, as universal, and as stable as we presume it to be
>
> (printed p.302, PDF p.4)

Their sharpest formulation of the alternative is a question, borrowed from Visweswaran and Lather:

> What would happen if autoethnography were to disrupt identity, discourage identification, and refuse understanding?
>
> (printed p.303, PDF p.5)

They then put experience under erasure, with Derrida's warning about the impossibility of standing outside what one contests:

> There is no sense in doing without the concepts of metaphysics in order to attack metaphysics. We have no language — no syntax and no lexicon — which is alien to this history; we cannot utter a single [deconstructive] proposition which has not already slipped into the form, the logic, and the implicit postulations of precisely what it seeks to contest
>
> (printed p.304, PDF p.6)

> To put experience under erasure, rendering it inaccurate yet necessary, is to engage in a deconstructive practice of examining familiar things by which “we come to such unfamiliar conclusions that even our very language is twisted and bent even as it guides us
>
> (printed p.304, PDF p.6)

## 4. Experience, voice and truth

> In these paradigms, voice lingers close to the true and the real, and because of this proximity, voice has become almost a mirror of the soul, the essence of the self.
>
> (printed p.306, PDF p.8)

MacLure's formula, which the article adopts outright:

> We accept MacLure’s assertion that voice is always “too much and never enough,” and urge autoethnographers to think of new ways of attending to this insufficient voice, this excessive voice, this opaque voice, that cannot speak truth and that cannot be contained in/by the narrative
>
> (printed p.307, PDF p.9)

## 5. The deconstructive autoethnography, and the two questions

This is the part the subsection would draw on. The method is two questions, not a procedure.

**First question**, printed p.309:

> Our first deconstructive question is: How can autoethnography exploit the limit of experience, not through an overcompensation that presents an “I” that is easily understood, but by positioning an “I” that is becoming through the telling?
>
> (printed p.309, PDF p.11)

**Second question**, printed pp. 311 to 312, taken from Foucault:

> We maintain that autoethnography’s new subject can avoid modernist questions like “What is my present?” and 312 alecia y. jackson and Lisa A. mazzei “What is the meaning of my present?” and instead pose the question: “What am I doing when I write about/speak of the present?” (Foucault, 1988, p. 89).
>
> (printed p.311, PDF p.13)

The Foucault passage behind it:

> I wish to know how the reflexivity of the subject and the discourse of truth are linked — How can the subject tell the truth about itself? . . . If I tell the truth about myself, as I am now doing, it is in part across a number of power relations which are exerted over me and which I exert over others
>
> (printed p.312, PDF p.14)

What the first question yields is not more voices but more difference:

> an assemblage of multiplicities that occur in the act of becoming. Not more versions of me, or more versions of the story (VanMaanen, 1988) but more differences, contradictions, and folds.
>
> (printed p.309, PDF p.11)

And the ethics they take from Colebrook reading Deleuze and Guattari:

> not to point to a better or more real image . . . but to insist that if life is a potential for imaging then that potential can only be maximized by not allowing any single image to govern all others. This is an ethics of life, defined not by what life is, but by the potential of life to open itself to what is not already given
>
> (printed p.310, PDF p.12)

## 6. The worked example, and it is a reading of one named scholar's account of her own mother

Three of the twenty pages, printed pp. 310 to 313, are a sustained reading of Carolyn Ellis's "With Mother/With Child: A True Story" (*Qualitative Inquiry*, 2001). They quote Ellis's own statements about what she decided to tell her mother and what she omitted, and they ask what her "I" is doing in making those decisions.

> we wish to consider how the tellings might be different and more productive, perhaps even ethical in Colebrook’s sense of “what is not already given.
>
> (printed p.310, PDF p.12)

**This is worth knowing before citing the article in a methods chapter.** The article does not demonstrate its method on its own authors' material. It demonstrates it by reading someone else's published autoethnography about a living relative, closely and by name. Whatever the merits, it is a different act from the one a methods section proposes when it says it will trouble its own narrating "I".

## 7. The narrative "I" and the performative "I"

The distinction the article is remembered for:

> This performative “I” is a becoming “I” that, rather than predetermined, is constructed in the process of attempts at truth-telling, and uses experience as a provisional strategy to “offer a way out of the closure of knowledge
>
> (printed p.305, PDF p.7)

Why they keep the first person rather than replacing it:

> We retain the “I” in order to trouble the

> (printed p.308, PDF p.10)

> To make a new word is to run the risk of forgetting the problem or believing it solved
>
> (printed p.308, PDF p.10)

## 9. What follows for the subsection, stated as a difference and not settled

The subsection worries about doing first-person work inside posthuman theory without the result reading as anthropocentrism. This article answers a **different** question: how to do first-person work without the result reading as humanism. Those are not the same worry, and the article never takes up the first one.

What the two have in common is the target, a sovereign narrating subject who knows its own experience. What they do not share is where the decentring pressure comes from. In this article it comes from **discourse, power and language**. In a posthuman or new materialist account it comes from **matter and the nonhuman**. The article contains nothing of the second.

**Both readings are recorded here. Neither is adjudicated.** Whether a deconstructive decentring of the "I" is sufficient for a new materialist methods chapter, or whether it decentres the subject only toward discourse and so leaves the human in place, is the kind of question that belongs to Amy, not to this file.

## 10. Su's quotation checked against the source

Su's long abstract cites this article twice. The first is inside quotation marks and keyed to p.308.

| | |
|---|---|
| **What Su writes**, at her p.1 | "shifts the analysis away from the narration itself, and troubles the narrating 'I's" (Jackson & Mazzei, 2008, p.308) |
| **What the article says**, printed p.308, PDF p.10 | "we urge autoethnographers to shift their analysis away from the narration itself, and away from narrating “I”s that question not only what they know but also how they know something (about themselves, about experience)." |

**The quoted words are not on that page in that form.** The article says to shift analysis *away from* narrating "I"s that question what they know; Su's version has it *troubling* them. The article does elsewhere say it retains the "I" in order to trouble the "I", on the same printed page, so Su's sense is defensible as a gloss of the article as a whole. It is not defensible as a quotation. **Do not pass Su's quotation on. Quote the article.**

Su's second citation, at her p.3, is unquoted and gives no page: that reflexivity does little to disrupt the hierarchies between researcher and researched, and may continue to privilege the ethnographer's voice and experience. That is a close and accurate paraphrase of the article's printed pp. 301 to 302, not of p.308. **Note one thing before repeating it:** at that point in the article the sentence about continuing to privilege the ethnographer's voice and experience carries a citation to Delamont (2007), so the point as Jackson and Mazzei state it is partly Delamont's.

Su also misspells the name as "Mazzi" in that second citation.
