## 1. The six chapters in one table

Hayles says in the Introduction that the literary chapters are ordered by **ascending resistance** to the field concept. Read through, the order is better described by what each writer does when the field will not go into words.

| ch | pp. | Writer | What the field concept is taken to be | The strategy | What defeats it |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 63-84 | **Pirsig** | Quality, "the moment when subject and object meet," the instant of "preintellectual awareness" | embrace it, and split the narration into present-tense scene and past-tense Chautauqua so the reader experiences Quality while hearing about it | the split narration cuts the unity it is meant to convey, and the ending's synthesis "sabotages the text's rhetorical strategy" by leaving nothing unsaid |
| 3 | 85-110 | **Lawrence** | the "unconscious," reached through paired body centres in a "frictional to-and-fro" | approach and avoid at once, and check linearity with "continual, slightly modified repetition" | the goal is the same state he fears, undifferentiation from the mother, so "its putative goal . . . is never achieved" |
| 4 | 111-137 | **Nabokov** | broken symmetry, and with it the possibility that time is reversible | build a world of mirror correspondences and let slight asymmetries wrench them | relation is not unity: what is left is "the atomistic universe of the Cartesian dichotomy compressed into two nearly perfect mirror images" |
| 5 | 138-167 | **Borges** | a set that contains itself, the whole both containing and contained by the part | subvert, by colliding the new "loss of certainty" with old certainties | by design: he does not want a new reality either |
| 6 | 168-198 | **Pynchon** | the holistic field itself, and the impossibility of representing it | explore the limits of imaging | cognition itself, which is binary, so what is rendered is "fallen, preterite versions" of the field |

The question all five are measured against is Hayles's own, from pp. 25-26: "can the representation of a holistic field be accomplished within the linear flow of words, or is the attempt inherently limited by the fragmentation of the medium?"

## 2. Chapter 2, Pirsig, pp. 63-84

Epigraph from Plato's *Phaedrus*, on the madness of the Muses.

**The shape.** Three voices, not two: the author, the unnamed narrator, and Phaedrus, "the shadowy other self that the narrator used to be," whose personality was destroyed by twenty-eight sessions of what the text calls "Annihilation ECS." Hayles reads the narrator as the personality that emerged afterwards, and Phaedrus as what cannot be spoken: "**Phaedrus is the rhetorical analogue to the Quality that cannot be spoken.**"

**Why Phaedrus fails,** p. 67, quoting Pirsig: "His original goal was to keep Quality undefined, but in the process of battling against the dialecticians he has made statements, and each statement has been a brick in a wall of definition he himself has been building around Quality. **Any attempt to develop an organized reason around an undefined quality defeats its own purpose.**"

**Why the narrator fails,** p. 69. The present-tense scene is not experience: "such descriptions are not experiences at all, but verbal reconstructions of sensory stimuli which may or may not have happened in the first place . . . when the narrator writes as a person describing a world 'out there,' he has already bifurcated that fluid, dynamic whole into a subject regarding an object." And then the general statement: "**Between any verbal construct and the Quality event is a difference that is by its nature not sayable, because to speak inevitably implies that one is not the Quality moment but separate from it. At best language can only describe what has been, not what is.**"

**The knife, and who holds it.** Hayles catches the narrator describing Quality as an "absence of any sense of separateness of subject and object" occurring at "the cutting edge of consciousness," and notes that the knife imagery is his own figure for Aristotelian analysis: "the narrator too wields a knife when he speaks." The passage she builds it from, Pirsig p. 69, is the one already in the reading plan: "**The application of this knife, the division of the world into parts . . . is something somebody does. From all this awareness we must select, and what we select and call consciousness is never the same as the awareness because the process of selection mutates it. We take a handful of sand from the endless landscape of awareness around us and call that handful of sand the world.**" And, italicised by Pirsig: "it is necessary to see that part of the landscape, inseparable from it, which must be understood, is **a figure in the middle of it, sorting sand into piles**. To see the landscape without seeing this figure is not to see the landscape at all." Hayles's turn on it: "The figure in our landscape, however, the figure we must see if we are 'to see the landscape at all,' **is not the narrator but the author**."

**The ending is a failure of a particular kind,** p. 82, and this is the sentence worth keeping: "The completion of the design has been accomplished by **moving what had been peripheral into center consciousness, but at the cost of losing the periphery that had been the text's greatest strength**. As Phaedrus joins with Pirsig, and as they speak again with one voice, there is nothing left unsaid, no aspect or part of Quality that has not been drawn into the realm of discourse. Hence the synthesis that allows formal closure also sabotages the text's rhetorical strategy."

**Consensus requires speaking, and speaking spoils it,** p. 70: "Consensus can be established, then, only by speaking; but speaking distorts the essence of the Quality that we are presumed to share. As the voice continues to enlarge the area of discourse . . . more and more 'bricks' (words, definitions, statements) stand between us and the Quality moment."

**One footnote worth having, n. 4, p. 66.** Of Pirsig's ignorance of the philosophy of science: "That he should nevertheless be concerned with issues that have dominated the philosophy of science in this century is **striking evidence that the cultural matrix is capable of guiding individual inquiry in parallel directions, even when there is little or no direct influence between the different inquiries.**" That is the climate-of-opinion claim from p. 22, applied to a case and buried in a footnote.

**And one more.** Hayles notes, from Rodino, that the narrator "makes an a priori acceptance of the limitations of motorcycle travel" when he decides "you don't make great conversations on a running cycle," and turns from the real child on the back of the bike to "the hypothetical and abstract audience of the Chautauquas," and that the book's resolution undoes exactly that: at the end "father and son remove their helmets and talk together naturally." The cost of the theorising is a child, and the repair is taking the helmet off.

## 3. Chapter 4, Nabokov, pp. 111-137

Epigraph: Einstein, "**As far as the laws of mathematics refer to reality, they are not certain; and as far as they are certain, they do not refer to reality.**"

**The physics, pp. 112-114**, and this is the chapter that carries the unambiguous-time-line point from the Introduction. Symmetry becomes intrinsic rather than accidental once the field rather than the particle is the real thing, and Heisenberg is quoted from his memoirs: "'**In the beginning was the symmetry**' is certainly a better expression than Democritus' 'In the beginning was the particle.'" Then the three symmetries of particle physics and their fall: **parity** (C. S. Wu, 1956, electrons from decaying cobalt emitted preferentially one way, so "there are events on the particle level . . . that cannot occur in mirror-reflected form"); then **CP** (a 1964 K-meson experiment); and so **T** by implication, since only CPT is left to hold. Wigner's reason why CPT will not be given up is not aesthetic: "the stubborn fact that we cannot formulate equations of motion in quantum field theory that lack this symmetry and still satisfy the postulates of Einstein's special theory of relativity." And Wigner's unsettling residue: "although '**two absolutely equally simple laws of nature are conceivable, nature has chosen, in its grand arbitrariness, only one**.'"

**The source Nabokov read.** Martin Gardner, *The Ambidextrous Universe* (1964), and probably his 1966 *Scientific American* piece "Can Time Go Backward?" Hayles shows the exchange: Gardner quoted "Pale Fire" attributing it to "the poet John Shade" without naming Nabokov, and Nabokov in *Ada* quotes the same two lines attributing them to an invented philosopher, "Martin Gardiner." The **Ozma Problem**, from Gardner, is the chapter's hidden centre: how to communicate what right and left mean to another planet using words only, with no shared pictures or reference points. Parity's fall answers it.

**The reading.** Van organises his memoir by repetition, because repetition supplies the similitude that measuring time needs while defeating its linearity; the asymmetries, which he resists, are what make the events distinguishable at all and so keep time in the book. "**Symmetry represents Van's triumph over time; asymmetry, the inevitable admission that time passes, people change, people die.**" Terra and Antiterra mirror each other with a fifty-to-a-hundred-year gap, "not all the no-longers of one world corresponding to the not-yets of the other," so Terra is "a distortive glass of our distortive glebe."

**The spiral.** From *Speak, Memory*: the circle "set free" in a spiral, thetic, antithetic, synthetic, each arc ampler than the last. "The spiral, then, accommodates the demands of symmetry, yet at the same time allows for a slight asymmetry that, in twisting the spiral upward, liberates the return from the prison of a closed circle." Hayles adds, against Zeller, that it is a spiral of **increasing** diameter, not a helix of constant diameter.

**The reader is conscripted,** pp. 119-120, and this is the part most useful outside Nabokov studies. Because so much of the book reflects earlier detail, "the reader who fails to remember these superabundant details will find subsequent passages increasingly unintelligible," so "**understanding the text, even on a literal level, thus requires that we duplicate Van's 'tense-willed' mind and his dedication to accurate recall**." And then the consequence: "by entering into this dynamic, we are also valorizing Van's philosophical speculations on the nature of time . . . **We, no less than he, are forced into a nostalgic stance if the present or future is to have any meaning. As we read through *Ada* the past is constantly expanding as an organizing principle, while the unknown, unforeseeable potential that we call the future is contracting.**" A formal device that makes the reader hold the author's metaphysics in order to follow the sentences at all.

**True and false memory.** Van's distinction is between sensual recall and "movie-like distancing," and he narrates his shameful episodes in camera directions ("Thus seen from above . . . we have the large island of the bed illumined from our left"). Hayles reads the camera as the register he retreats into: "**if the false memory of movie-like distancing leads to the purgatory of non-personhood, the true memory of vivid sensual recall can sometimes lead straight to hell.**" His rage at Kim's photographs is that they "posit another version of the reality that Van recollects, a version not under his direct control."

**The sentence that undoes the memoirist,** p. 134: "**To recollect faithfully and completely is to include the memory of counter-recollections which deny that the memoirist's reality is the only one. The very comprehensiveness of the mnemonic endeavor insures that the means for it unraveling are contained within it.**"

**The verdict,** p. 136, in two parts, and the second is the sharper. First: "The metaphysic of *Ada* may thus be defined as follows: **what can be controlled is never completely real; what is real can never be completely controlled.**" Then: "Yet there is a subtler implication of the field concept that Nabokov misses, or perhaps ignores. **The desire for control is predicated on the assumption that it is possible to make an unambiguous separation between the one who controls and that which is controlled.** It relies, in other words, on the Cartesian dichotomy . . . To accept that control can never be complete is to modify that world view, not to reject it; **the revolutionary view would be to propose that the control is a chimera, and the desire for it is induced by the illusion that we are separate from the world and each other.**"

So the same phrase, "unambiguous separation," does the work twice in this book: at p. 22 of a time-line for spatially separated events, and at p. 136 of the controller and the controlled. Hayles does not join them. The join is available.

## 4. Chapter 5, Borges, pp. 138-167

Epigraph from Edgar Morin, "Beyond Determinism," and it is the one epigraph in the book that asks a question worth keeping: "But can we not ask ourselves whether there do not exist, in the real universe, things which are **non-algorithmizable, non-reducible, non-unifiable** . . . . From that moment on, does the problem become, **not to eject and repress uncertainty, randomness, disorder, antagonism outside its royal domain, but to seek dialogue with them?**"

**The opposition she opens with,** p. 138: "whereas Nabokov is drawn to it because its asymmetries promise to rescue art from being merely a game, **Borges is attracted to it because its discontinuities reveal that everything, including itself, is no more than a game**." And the formal consequence: "What stasis is to Nabokov, **sequence** is to Borges." Nabokov writes hundreds of pages to close a pattern; Borges writes five or six with "at least one open-ended catalogue capable of indefinite expansion," because "**for Borges stasis is impossible because art is not an object to be framed, but a continuing process whose permutations are inexhaustible.**"

**The mathematics, pp. 139-142**, and this is the clearest short account of it in the book. Sequence, series and set distinguished; convergent against divergent series; then **Cantor**, who argued that **actually** infinite sets (as against potentially infinite ones) are legitimate objects, that their relative sizes can be compared, and who therefore called them transfinite numbers rather than infinities, writing them with the aleph. The move that matters for the field concept: "Cantor's unique contribution was to consider all the elements of an infinite set to be present (as he said) '**at once**,' and thus to posit the number system as **a pre-existing, interrelated totality**. In making this shift, Cantor introduced into mathematics ambiguities and indeterminacies that we have seen to be characteristic of the field concept." Then the paradox he could not get out of: the set of all sets must be the largest, but the set of all its subsets must be larger still, because "the set of all sets contains itself as a member." Cantor's answer was only to call self-referential sets inconsistent.

**The counting demonstrations, pp. 154-155**, which Hayles sets out in full because Borges uses them. One-to-one correspondence shows the odd numbers alone to be as many as the odds and evens together, and Borges's own example: "**there are as many multiples of 3018 as there are numbers, without excluding from these 3018 and its multiples.**" What he takes from it: "**in these elevated latitudes of enumeration, the part is no less copious than the whole: the exact quantity of points there are in the universe is the same as in a meter, or in a decimeter, or in the farthest stellar trajectory.**" So "infinities can be opened within any element, no matter how small, simply by suggesting that it is part of an infinite series."

**The strategy, p. 143, in four steps**, which is the most portable thing in the chapter: "The first step in his strategy is **to transform a continuity into a succession of points**, and to suggest that these points form a sequence; there follows the insinuation that **the sequence progresses beyond the expected terminus to stretch into infinity**; then **the sequence is folded back on itself**, so that closure becomes impossible because of the endless, paradoxical circling of a self-referential system . . . The final step is to suggest that **our world, like the fiction, is a self-contained entity whose connection with reality is problematic or nonexistent.**"

**The numbers inside "Tlon, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius," pp. 144-146**, read as that strategy working. Tlon counts in base twelve, and the story leans on **eleven** (the eleventh volume of the encyclopedia, eleventh-century heresies, hronir of the eleventh degree), which "leads us beyond the decimal system, yet falls short of the expected terminus of the duodecimal system"; our world conceives Tlon in the eleventh volume, Tlon conceives us in the parable of the **nine** coins, nine being the penultimate term of base ten. "Through the conjunction of the penultimate terms of the two number systems, **each world seems to be evoking the other as the final, inevitable, and yet almost unimaginable last term of the alien number system it entertains**." Then a third system, sexagesimal, is waiting behind both in Ashe's conversion tables, so "the progression implies a sequence of worlds, each calling its successor into being in an increasingly unimaginable sequence that has no end."

**And the contradiction she finds in it, p. 147:** Tlon's metaphysics is "extreme idealism," in which each state of being is unconnected to the ones before and after, so "**Tlonist philosophy would thus deny the possibility of a sequence** . . . Borges has posited a sequence which contains within it a term, Tlon, that denies the possibility of sequence." The result is not a stable progression but a state "where nothing is certain," and the Borges sentence for it: "Now, in all memories, a fictitious past occupies the place of any other. **We know nothing about it with any certainty, not even that it is false.**"

**The labyrinth test, p. 148**, from the famous passage: "Useless to reply that reality, too, is ordered. It may be so, but in accordance with divine laws, I translate: inhuman laws, which we will never completely perceive. **Tlon may be a labyrinth, but it is a labyrinth plotted by men, a labyrinth destined to be deciphered by men.**" Hayles's gloss: "**All we can be sure of is that if we may decipher its principles, then it is not reality but a labyrinth, that is to say, an artifact.**" And then the reversal, which is the subtlest thing in the chapter: because the fictional world is internally inconsistent, "we cannot be sure if even the artifact is ordered, **which in a curious way makes it again verisimilar to reality** . . . **its very inaccessibility rendering it mimetic.** The reasoning is, of course, circular."

**The reader is enclosed, pp. 151-152.** In "The Library of Babel," if the Library holds all possible books then the text in our hands is one of its volumes, not the narrator's handwritten account, so "**we, no less than the narrator, are contained within one of the books we peruse**." With Borges's own sentence from "Partial Enchantments of the *Quixote*": "those inversions suggest that **if the characters in a story can be readers or spectators, then we, their readers and spectators, can be fictitious**."

**Order is not meaning.** The Librarian's solution is that after the permutations are exhausted the Library repeats, becoming "limitless and periodic," and repetition is itself an order. Hayles: "**While it suggests a way to transform randomness into ordered sequence, it contains no hint of how that sequence may be rendered intelligible or meaningful. Even if order is assured, sense is not** . . . The narrator's solution is significant not because it is satisfying, but because it is **inevitable**. It reveals how desperate is the human need to find meaning somewhere, even if only in the repeated order of meaningless sequence."

**Sequence as a form, p. 154.** Borges excises narrative's "middle distance" with ellipses and sudden breaks, so that "**the spaces between terms are as important as the terms themselves**," and the gaps "create gaps that make it impossible even to estimate where the bottom might be."

**"The Aleph" and the Axiom of Choice, pp. 156-159.** Daneri, owning an object that literally contains everything, writes a poem meant to "set to verse the entire face of the planet," which is composition as one-to-one correspondence: "**when literature is a series of signs to be put into one-to-one correspondence with the world, choice is reduced to deciding which sign to match with which object**." The narrator's fixation on Beatriz is the opposite, choice made absolute, and "by testifying to the inexplicability of this choice, **Beatriz's imperfections emphasize its absoluteness**." Behind both sits Cantor's **Axiom of Choice**, and Hayles reports its consequence straight: the **Banach-Tarski paradox**, by which any two solid spheres can each be cut into finitely many pieces so that each part of one is congruent to exactly one part of the other, so "one can divide the entire earth up into little pieces and merely by rearranging them make up a sphere the size of the ball." Her aside: "**I hasten to remind the reader that this is not Borges's fancy, but a logical consequence of the Axiom of Choice as it is used in modern set theory.**"

**Where Borges parts from Cantor, pp. 159-161.** Cantor retreated to Platonism: the alephs were real because they were ideal objects, and so the paradoxes were resolvable in principle. Borges thinks nothing of the kind, and Hayles quotes him from a *Paris Review* interview saying "I think I'm Aristotelian, but I wish it were the other way." So: "**The Alephs interest Borges not because they are real, but because they allow him to suggest that nothing is 'real.' . . . Whereas Cantor wanted to extend logical analysis, not destroy or compromise it, Borges wants to use logical analysis to show how profoundly illogical its results can be.**"

**The chapter's own best finding, pp. 161-167: the subverter is subverted.** Borges's refutation of time needs time to be a series, and series is the Newtonian form, so "**the vocabulary of denial is also the language of affirmation**." The word "series" pivots in his own sentence: first the temporal series, then the sequence of idealist arguments, so that "**the disruption of (temporal) series is thus made to form the final term of the series proceeding from idealist premises. If the temporal series has been refuted, the series of philosophical conjectures has been extended.**" And the price of the method, which is the sentence to keep: "Borges can arrive at a felt sense of 'the inconceivable word eternity' **only by momentarily suppressing the knowledge that the moment he participates in is not a unique event, but one of an almost endless series of moments, most of which are inimical or indifferent to this feeling**," so that, quoting Butler, what is essential to such an experience "**is its capacity to exclude** . . . its credibility depends ultimately on the success with which Borges suppresses other felt states which militate against it."

Then the double bind stated as a rule, p. 166: "**The very action that allows choice to operate also assures that the choice will be less than absolute.**" Because positing a series is what lets a single term be lifted out, and positing a series is what guarantees the other terms will come back and push it out of mind. "The circular dialectic, then, is not merely an arbitrary requirement imposed by Borges's skepticism. Rather, it is a profound recognition on Borges's part that '**really, what I want to do is impossible**.'"

Closing, p. 167: "**His strategy of claiming that there are no final answers is not final, either.**" And Borges's own last line on it: "Time is the substance I am made of. Time is a river that carries me away, but I am the river; it is a tiger that mangles me, but I am the tiger; it is a fire that consumes me, but I am the fire."

## 5. Chapter 6, Pynchon, pp. 168-198

Epigraph: Wittgenstein, *Tractatus*, "**Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent.**"

**The claim, p. 168:** "More than any other writer in this study, Pynchon grasps the full implications of the field concept, including both its promise of a reality that is a harmonious, dynamic whole and the problem it poses of how to represent that reality in the fragmented medium of language. Pynchon's response to this dilemma is **to create a text that at once invites and resists our attempts to organize it into a unified field of meaning.**"

**The double bind, p. 169**, and it is the chapter's spine: "The patterns of *Gravity's Rainbow* tend toward self-obliteration **because the focus for the text's anxiety is precisely the cognitive thought that seeks to organize diverse data into coherent patterns** . . . The perceived patterns imply that self-conscious cognition is skewing our society and driving us toward destruction; **but in order to receive this message, we had to tame the unruliness of the text into cognitive patterns we understand, thus exercising over the text the same kind of control that created the problem in the first place.**" Restated at p. 179: "**To be able to decode the text is to be cognitively conscious, and to be cognitively conscious is to deny the message implicit in that decoding.**"

**What the unruliness is for, p. 169:** "The text's unruliness makes the reader acutely aware that **patterns are not merely perceived but constructed**, thereby alerting us that as we read, we are building a cognitive structure. At the same time, the unruliness insures that **this cognitive structure cannot be complete or perfect.** For all its frustration for the reader, the unruliness **offers a way out** of the central dangers of authoritarian control and life-denying organization." So the obstruction is the ethics, not an accident of style.

**The principle of equal distance, pp. 174-175.** This is the formal invention in the chapter and the thing most worth having. The traditional novel encodes a difference between significant event and irrelevant detail, which "valorizes our usual modes of cognition," because subordinating most of the incoming data to background is what cognition does: "**Meaning is made possible, indeed, defined, by its distinction from non-meaning.**" Pynchon refuses the distinction: "In *Gravity's Rainbow* everything is placed at an equal distance from the reader, so that background and foreground collapse into the same perceptual plane . . . hardly any plot in the conventional sense of the word, or rather, more accurately, **everything is plot**." And therefore: "**With everything at an equal distance from the reader, meaning can no longer be achieved by discerning difference. Rather, it emerges as a result of seeing that everything is at the same distance. Meaning thus becomes a function not of difference but of similarity, arising not from distinguishing parts but from seeing the interconnection of the whole.**"

She names who reads this way: "We tend to experience meaning in this text as **a paranoid, or someone dropping acid, or a religious visionary who believes in Providential design** might experience it; in these views, **there are no irrelevancies**. These very different ways of organizing experience are isomorphic in the sense that they all presuppose the pervasiveness of pattern, that is to say, they all suppose a field view of reality." And the consequence for readers: meaning "arrives as a gestalt, precipitating into awareness; **either one sees the whole design, or one doesn't see it at all**," which is why the book divides its readers into those who find chaos and those who find pattern everywhere. "*Gravity's Rainbow* is thus both a narrative and an initiation."

**The equivocal figure, pp. 175-176**, as the limit on all of it: a figure-ground reversal can be seen either way, and "it is apparent that one's mindset, rather than the picture itself, determines which figure is primary. Because neither could exist without the other, to designate either as ground or figure is arbitrary; they mutually define each other. The relevant point is that **human cognition is such that only one figure can be brought into focus at a time, even though we know that they are interconnected. As a consequence, we can never see the whole picture at once, because half of it will always be obscured from our vision at any given time.**"

**The interface, pp. 179-181, and the permeable membrane.** The pun on **frame** comes first: "by placing the narrative within a frame, we view it as essentially separate and distinct from the cognitive faculty that brought it into being. Thus we have 'framed' it in another sense, that is, **defined it in such a way that what we assert of it, even though false, cannot be proven to be false because the falsity is contained in the very assumption that it is an object of discourse.**" Then film, whose sprocket holes the narrative's square breaks resemble, and which shows that "**that 'reality' is itself a reconstruction** . . . One meaning of the interface, then, is that 'reality' exists on neither side, so that penetrating the interface merely takes us from one kind of reconstruction to another."

And then the sentence already flagged in the other close read, which is on **p. 181**:

> "**From a field perspective, interfaces are not barriers, but points of exchange, surfaces through which two orders of being can interpenetrate.** This raises the possibility of a holistic field that transcends and includes the interface. **As the interface is thus transformed from the boundary that cognitive consciousness perceives it to be to the permeable membrane it can become in the field view, Return returns again as a possibility.**"

How a film becomes one: the rape filmed in *Alpdrucken* fathers a real child, and the men watching go home and father children of their own, so "despite our divisions of the field into This Side and the Other Side, art and reality, connections exist that join all sides into a single field of interaction." Hayles's rule for the text, p. 182: "**In a narrative where black and white are joined by gestalt perception into a single figure, the interfaces are always permeable, given the right perspective.**"

**Naming, pp. 184-186.** The Word is the other permeable membrane, and the problem is that its wielder is "fallen, preterite, of diseased imagination": "**What then if his acts of naming, like von Goll's films, have the power not just to reveal the patterns but actually to create them? What rough beast will come breaking through the Text as interface into this world?**" Pynchon's position is not Adamic: "**Being of the post-structuralist generation, Pynchon grants that names in themselves are arbitrary.** But he reasons that once humankind has assigned meaning to sounds, how we choose to deploy those sounds (the act of naming) reveals deep patterns of correspondence that the namers sense and to which they respond." Hence the refrain: "Names by themselves may be empty, **but the act of naming** . . ."

**And here the positivist programme of pp. 37-40 comes back as politics, p. 185.** The New Turkic Alphabet committees, with Radnichny "on a megalomaniac project to replace every spoken vowel in Central Asia" with "a neutral uh," are read as "the imperialism of the entire project, which aims to impose on '**the lawless, the mortal streaming**' of native speech the letters and words that the bureaucracy decides they should have." Once the spoken word is broken into discrete written symbols it "can be modulated, broken, recoupled, re-defined, copolymerized one to the other in worldwide chains." Then Hayles's own sentence, which uses the word:

> "**The positivist program to force multiform indeterminacies into specific slots, whether through dictionaries, chemical synthesis, or Pavlovian experiments, breaks the original Whole into shards that can then be recombined to reflect the face, not of God, but of fallen man.**"

That is the only place in the book where the positivist programme is described as doing something to anybody. Section 6 below sets the pp. 37-38 account beside Trinh; this sentence is Hayles's own nearest approach to the same ground, 147 pages later, and it arrives as a reading of a satire about an alphabet imposed on Central Asia.

**The way out of the double bind, p. 187**, offered and not taken: "first language is seen as a process of recovery . . . Then, language is an instrument not of recovery but of creation, actually bringing the patterns into existence. **The next step is to recognize that the distinction between 'reality' and 'created pattern' is meaningless.** This step implies a redefinition of the essential relationship between art and reality. **Instead of asking, 'What is breaking through the text as interface?' the appropriate question is, 'What makes you think there is an interface?'**" With the stadium whose "Outside and Inside interpiercing one another too fast, too finely labyrinthine, for either category to have much hegemony any more."

**The cost of taking it, pp. 187-188.** Slothrop's dissolution is that step taken: "**if to overcome the subject-object duality completely is to merge with the 'mind-body' of the cosmos, it is also to cease to exist as a person localized in time and space, and thus to be unable to influence the temporal unfolding of events.**" Those who cross over stop caring how secular history unfolds. "If from one perspective this is salvation, from another point of view it is a betrayal of the revolution by becoming incapable of effective social action. **To stay at the barricades, however, is to remain in the realm of cognitive thought**, thus contributing, through the very act of remaining conscious, to Their enterprise." And the dream in which "JAMF" is defined as "I": "**To try to fight Them is to become Them.**"

**The cosmology, pp. 188-196**, which is where the chapter ends up. Centrifugal against centripetal; the Big Bang and the **Friedmann point**, "the infinitely dense point from which the present Universe expanded"; the oscillating universe, in which Return is possible but "**Return also means annihilation**, for the universe can be reborn only by going through the absolute gravitational collapse that means not only the death of all life, but the destruction of all matter as we know it." Then **singularities** defined properly, as points where the derivative becomes discontinuous and the rate of change goes to infinity, so that a singularity "represents a point where the behavior of the function ceases to be mathematically expressible, except in a purely formal way . . . **the point at which the function escapes from the delta increments of rational analysis into the unknown**." Two figures are printed, a cusp and its differentiation. Then **black holes**, the event horizon, the Schwarzchild radius (and "Schwarzchild" as Jamf's code name for the infant Slothrop), and the second solution of the same equations, the **white hole**, reached by reversing the sign of time, so that "annihilation and rebirth are simply two sides of the same coin." And why the title names gravity: it is the one force responsible both for the flight from the centre that a black hole represents and for the cosmic return to it.

**The last words of the book, p. 197**, quoting Pynchon and then closing on it: the rest of us "must go on blundering in our front-brain faith in Kute Korrespondences . . . trying to string them all together like terms of a power series hoping to zero in on **the tremendous and secret Function whose name, like the permutated names of God, cannot be spoken** . . ." Hayles: "**Like the real nature of gravity, our relation to the field of the cosmos is a 'tremendous and secret Function' whose meaning finally 'cannot be spoken,' even by Pynchon.**"

So the book that opens by asking whether a holistic field can be represented in a linear flow of words ends by not answering the question, in a sentence built out of the novel's own figure for the unsayable.

## 6. The positivists, pp. 37-40, read through Trinh

Amy's note of 29 Oct, recorded as hers, not rewritten and not developed:

> Read Trinh again thru page 37-38 and the goal of the positivists to purify language and declares some statements as without cognitive significance or as nonsense. Its like genocide of thought

### What Hayles says, pp. 37-38, in full

> "In the wake of the great successes of Newtonian mechanics, it seemed to many scientists that all physical phenomena would eventually yield to mechanical descriptions. Considerable attention was therefore devoted to **refining scientific discourse so that it would establish unambiguously the link between this predictable reality and the theory that predicted it. The goal of the positivists was to 'purify' language by removing from it anything that could not be empirically verified or logically demonstrated, in short, anything suspected of being 'metaphysical.'** Statements that had '**cognitive significance**' were to be composed of three, and only three, kinds of terms: observational statements taken directly from experiment; theoretical terms; and logical terms indicating how the other two kinds of terms should be combined. **Statements that did not fulfill these criteria did not possess 'cognitive significance,' or in plain words, were nonsense.**"

> "It was thought possible to extend the program beyond the experimental sciences into related fields such as the philosophy of science, **and indeed to any field that proposed to engage in cognitively meaningful discourse.**"

And the footnote attached to the word "nonsense," n. 10, p. 38, which narrows the exemption to nothing:

> "**Positivists did recognize a genre called 'emotive discourse,' but whether this could be said to have meaning was considered problematic.**"

### How it was defeated, pp. 38-40

Not by argument about what it excluded, but because the purification could not be carried out on its own terms. Hayles's sentence: "Like the Hilbert program, **the positivist program failed when it was recognized that language creates a field that encompasses the observer as well as the observation.**"

The three who broke it, in her order. **N. R. Hanson**: what the Received View called "observational terms" were "in fact not sensory data per se, but sensory data as interpreted, at the very least, through an experimental apparatus that already had certain assumptions built into it, as well as through the unconscious perceptual sets of the observer." **Kuhn**: scientists "absorbed a set of more or less unconscious assumptions about how science was 'done'," and "there are always known facts that contradict accepted theories; but these will be ignored as long as the paradigm allows enough other data to be correlated satisfactorily. **It is only when the paradigm begins to break down that anomalies will be noticed, or even reported.**" **Polanyi**: tacit knowledge, "knowledge which is in some sense known, but which cannot be formulated explicitly," is what "guides him to the interesting fact, the one datum or experiment out of thousands that will prove useful."

The conclusion, p. 40: "**It has proven impossible to create such a language because the terms that comprise it already contain assumptions that cannot be validated independently of the language.**"

### The same operation in Trinh, and under a different description

Trinh is not writing about logical positivism. She is writing about who is permitted to be clear, and the structure she describes is the same structure: a criterion of admissible language, applied to everything, with what fails it declared not to mean.

| the operation | Hayles on the positivists, pp. 37-38 | Trinh, *Woman, Native, Other* (1989) |
|---|---|---|
| the criterion | three and only three kinds of term: observational, theoretical, logical | "**Clear expression, often equated with correct expression**, has long been the criterion set forth in treatises on rhetoric, whose aim was to order discourse so as to persuade" |
| what language is for | to "establish unambiguously the link" between reality and the theory | "To write is to communicate, express, witness, impose, instruct, redeem, or save, at any rate **to mean and to send out an unambiguous message**" |
| what that makes writing | a medium to be purified | "**Writing thus reduced to a mere vehicle of thought** may be used to orient toward a goal or to sustain an act, but it does not constitute an act in itself" |
| the verdict on what fails | "did not possess 'cognitive significance,' or in plain words, **were nonsense**" | "**Clarity is a means of subjection**, a quality both of official, taught language and of correct writing, two old mates of power: together they flow, together they flower, **vertically, to impose an order**" |
| the scope claimed | "**indeed to any field that proposed to engage in cognitively meaningful discourse**" | "To use the language well, says the voice of literacy, cherish its classic form. Do not choose the offbeat at the cost of clarity. Obscurity is an imposition on the reader. **True, but beware when you cross railroad tracks for one train may hide another train**" |

**Trinh then names who the criterion catches, which the positivist account does not.** Two kinds of speech, in her list, both perfectly intelligible and both failing the test:

> "The language of **Taoism and Zen**, for example, which is perfectly accessible but rife with paradox does not qualify as 'clear' (**paradox is 'illogical' and 'nonsensical' to many Westerners**), for its intent lies outside the realm of persuasion. The same holds true for **vernacular speech**, which is not acquired through institutions, schools, churches, professions, etc., and therefore not repressed by either grammatical rules, technical terms, or key words."

The word that arrives in both texts is the same: **nonsensical**. In Hayles it is the positivists' technical verdict on statements lacking cognitive significance. In Trinh it is what paradox is called by those for whom clarity is the criterion. Hayles gives the verdict in the vocabulary of the people who issued it. Trinh gives it in the vocabulary of the people it lands on.

**And Trinh describes the purification itself, not just its verdict**, in the Nativism chapter:

> "Not his language, **which he wishes to render transparent, believing he could erase himself in his writing** while clinging to the author's mastership. **The claim to objectivity subjects words to a willed meaning-intention.** Do I make sense? **Meaningful language is confined to 'expression'** and what appears significant to him is its reduction to pure instrumentality: a minimum presence and yet an effective defensive weapon. **Words are solicited only for their effacement from the page. Their materiality, their glaring bodies must somehow sink and disappear from the field of visibility.**"

That is a description of purification as a removal: the words' bodies are to be made to disappear. It sits exactly on Hayles's "the goal of the positivists was to 'purify' language by removing from it anything that could not be empirically verified."

**On the anthropologist as the holder of the criterion**, the same chapter: he "pretends to the precision of a zoologist or a botanist. **As purveyor of 'truth,' he has moved from the absolute to the relative** and now assumes the role of purveyor of 'certain truths,' pursuing a 'perspectivistic knowledge' while keeping an eye profoundly glued on 'scientific objectivity' as methodological goal." And: "**The purveyor of Truth or certain anthropomorphic truths can equally be called the purveyor of Error or certain anthropomorphic errors.**"

### Where the two accounts part (recorded, not adjudicated)

- **Hayles narrates a programme that failed.** Her positivists are defeated in four pages, by Hanson, Kuhn and Polanyi, on the ground that the purified language could not be built. The exclusion is a feature of an attempt, and the attempt collapsed. Nothing in her account asks what was excluded while it stood, or who was speaking the excluded statements.
- **Trinh describes a criterion still operating.** Hers is not a programme in the philosophy of science but the everyday rule of "official, taught language and of correct writing," and she does not report it as defeated. In the Chen interview, 1992, she is still saying that "the ideology of what constitutes 'clarity' and 'accessibility' continues to be largely taken for granted."
- **One is about whether a language can be made exact. The other is about what a demand for exactness does to the people who do not speak it.** The two do not contradict each other, and the gap between them is not a gap to be closed by deciding which is right.
- **The held texts allow the pair to be made.** Hayles's four pages and Trinh's chapter are both in these folders, and neither cites the other: Trinh does not appear in *The Cosmic Web* (she is writing five years later), and Hayles does not appear in *Woman, Native, Other*.
