## Tools for diffraction/transposition

## The camera

**Camera**

Framed/Bounded by time/space/place/theory/perseverations

I sit down to write angry. Irritated. The words I need to type were already composed, pouring out since I woke up this morning. So typing the words after the words were so carefully composed already is a second order version, an imperfect recollection of a perfect story, because all stories are singularly perfect through each frame/time/space through/with which they were composed.

As a photographer, my first art form/medium/expression with explicit tool, I was vehemently opposed to the crop. Maybe this was an attitude adopted from a professor I admired or was well considered originally in my own mind, but nonetheless I hold to it. A famous image of Irving Berlin was used as an example of the perfect crop of an image originally composed through the lens differently. Berlin sits at his huge black piano and the crop is tight around his body and the edges of the piano, with the rest of the behemoth of a thing left out of the printed black and white image. I believe the polemic was whether the photographer, who I can’t even remember, was a cheat or a genius.

Not so much with my 35mm camera, but religiously with my 6cm twin lens reflex, the camera that became an extension of my thoughts, my eyes, through which I situated myself and considered/pondered existence in three dimensional space, I never cropped. Never. Frequently not even the position of the camera was moved from the exact place I first put it down, heavy on its sturdy metal tripod. My unspoken/unwritten process/rule was that my first intuition of where to set the camera down, how to adjust the knobs and the legs of the tripod, just where to point the double stacked lenses of the clunky camera box, was the correct place/time/space/crop of the first frame of the 12 frame roll of medium format film.

I feel the same way about thinking, about writing, remembering, wondering, building networks/weaving together. Editing, or remembering afterwards and attempting to reconstruct the original through stream, is like cropping. Lazy. And also aggressive, disrespectful, unseemly, and maybe a bit haughty, as if the thing originally created could be wiped out, as if it didn’t exist, or didn’t deserve to exist somewhere/time/why in the way it was first conceived/or conceived itself.

So this will be the second time I have told this story/made this accounting/with homage and respect to the original thoughts, now gone, fleeting, flown out and not captured as they flew.

My camera sits beside me now, on the long high table that I use as a desk, that my husband made to use as a tool bench, but I sequestered, as I frequently do with things. The camera is similar in shape to a somewhat squatter saltine cracker box, black (in some places still shiny, in other places dull because the material has worn more over time and use), heavy, heavier than   would imagine a thing with a neck strap to be. I might have bought it in 1995, maybe 1996, while I was an undergraduate student at Louisiana State University. This camera, a Mamiya C3,  is my second iteration of a twin lens model, the first was much lighter, a Yashica D, and less robustly built. I don’t remember exactly when it died, but for a very long time I carried around its parts, thinking I might someday revive it, as it had been my first introduction to the medium format image and I owed some allegiance to it. My memory of that time is deeply marked by where I was living and who I was living with, as the years between high school and moving to Arizona in 1999 were splotchy/not tumultuous, that word implies more smoothness/gradient between events/places/people. I had taken black and white darkroom photography courses while I was in high school with Dr. Bill McBride, a professor at the local university who graciously also shared with us his passion for cameras and film. I have never been one for precision or meticulousness, so it took me two semesters to realize that I kept getting double exposures on the photo paper because I turned the enlarger bulb on and then placed the paper under the projected image. At some point an upperclassman pointed out my error, it bothers me that I can’t remember if his advice was unsolicited or not. My photo of a small spotted dog/maybe a puppy, shot from the perspective of me lying down on the ground to be level with the dog’s gaze, the owner’s legs/shows, and with the fence lines in the background maintaining a retrospective perfect parallel (I could check because the resulting art portfolio/printed anthology/book from high school is still on my shelf, but I won’t, because I prefer to remember my lines as perfectly parallel). I took pride in that image making the cover of something created at an art school (math, science, and the arts) because I did not consider myself a bonafide member of that student camp. I was a lurker, across subjects/people/ideas/ideologies. I also remember (maybe erroneously) a feeling that the self appointed real art students were not happy about my photo being on the cover when there were other more accomplished (in their perception) mediums like paint/paintings available. I was an interloper and not welcome.

I started college as a German/Russian language major, then anthropology (falling in love with cultural anthropology after the first intro course), and then later adding a photography/art minor, not a major because I could never get thru the required fastidiousness of the design courses which required Dantean levels of iteration through exacting lines in pencil, ink, gouache, and eraser. My first photography course was certainly black and white and most likely still with 35mm, but quickly shifted to my still now 30 years later color medium format 6cm square, sometimes positive, sometimes negative film.

Both the Mamiya and the Yashica were purchased at a camera shop that happened to be in the geography locus of my then world, on Government Street, near the garden district in Baton Rouge, across the street from the high school for gifted/talented students. The Yashica came first, then the Mamiya, and last the heavy sturdy steel tripod that I used to carry with me everywhere, running through airports, when   could still get to the gate at the last minute, with the legs tucked close in front of my body, I always imagined like a rifle would be carried, running into or from battle.

The twin-lens is a special camera for multiple reasons, but most pointedly because of the unique peculiar (even to me now 30 years after first looking through at the viewing glass) way it elongates and separates space, resulting in an image that feels three dimensional. I do not know if it is because of the lens mechanism, that there is one lens through which the viewer composes the scene, and another lens through which the shutter allows light to hit the film, or because of the resulting shape of the image, a perfect square. I do not know and nor have I ever even thought to investigate whether the square is a necessary companion to the twin-lens or some other configuration of image could be fashioned. Knowledge might kill the wonder.

Imagine the spatial feeling of looking at multiple layers of an old-fashioned animation cell, where each layer is given its own distance from the others. Imagine a diorama   made as a child or as an adult helping a child, because I don’t recollect any adults in my sphere of existence making a diorama that was not demanded of them. The multiple objects placed in the showbox scene are viewed through the front of the box and each object seems to have its own space just so. Cameras (I won’t say all) have an aperture and a shutter that together fashion the spatial illusion/resulting reality on the film. By opening up and closing down the aperture, the light is made to enter the hole either more directly, in parallel rays, or more diffusely/diffractively. When the aperture is smaller the resulting distance that can be in focus is longer. When the aperture is larger, the diffuse/diffracting light causes only a smaller portion of the depth of field to be in focus. My perception of a 35mm camera and film, and its rectangular composition, is that regardless of the aperture and the focal range/depth of field, the resulting image spatial illusion feels flat. In stark contrast, to my visceral impression, a twin-lens square, regardless of the aperture, creates spatial resonance/depth/and a feeling that the composition is always swirling into itself, like light into a black hole.

My inclination to use the camera is driven by a perception in a space that there is already a swirling/a vortex, some kind of harmonious constellation of objects/color/light that viscerally feels complete, attuned, vibratory, resonating. Immanence? I feel that swirling, I pull my camera case from the shelf, press the metal push button latch that releases the top of the cracked faux leather veneered case, check the side of the hefty awkward camera box for a tiny number on a silver dial that indicates whether there are frames left. I pull open the unwieldy tripod legs, click the camera into its perch on top, and open the viewing box on top of the camera. The twin-lens has a viewing glass on top of the camera, more similar in feeling to looking at a phone camera screen now, than looking through the viewfinder of a typical through the lens (TTL) style camera. The image floats, ephemeral, delicate, flipped horizontally backwards from the view from   naked eye. Most cameras have/had a pop up contraption, like the flaps of a cracker box, that shield the image and the eye from intruding light. If   were to watch someone take a photo from a twin-lens camera, they would frequently be holding the camera, cradled between both hands, held tight to their abdomen between their belly button and sternum. They would be looking down as they directed   to move a little left/right, and fiddling with a knob/knobs, a crank, and a number of long sliding pin-like apparatus that were surprisingly buttons. I always thought, when on occasion I was the watcher and not the photo taker, that users of the twin-lens camera seemed privy to some other/maybe even otherworldly perspective. They aren’t casual users, who just wanted to snap a quick photo, there was some affinity with the object that made the image possible, some connection of body, eye, contraption. If I didn’t have my own twin-lens camera, I might be envious.

### The 110 Camera

The 110 Camera

My first camera experience was my mother’s 110, circa sometime between 1982 and 1985. 110 cameras were long and flat, like a woman’s wallet, or a block of cheese. The film was tiny in comparison to 35mm and absolutely engulfed by 6cm, not that I have ever seen the physical comparison in person. I remember three images I know I took. The potato bin. The curtains. My little brother wearing my mother’s bra. I think I remember my mother making comment about the photo of the potato bin, otherwise there was no outward expression, no clearly delineated event of before and after, but the potato bin stands out as my first inclination towards an object strong enough to induce my need to compose its capture.

### Spurts

Spurts

I don’t think I have used the Mamiya since my  ngest was born in 2020. I vaguely, might have imagined, driving the Mamiya and its tripod to capture film records of my MFA thesis exhibit in March of 2020, but I might have decided the installation in the black box space was just too dark, or even though a few months pregnant I had carried the camera across town and up the stairs into the space, maybe I just didn’t feel the swirling required to inspire placing the camera on its perch and popping open the lid.

There are two iconic images from the first months of our relationship/partnership/kinship/kindling, both taken in the apartment of high school friends Richard and David. Richard was prone to coming down the stairs announcing waftingly somehow with fanfare that didn’t seem too much, that he had taken someone else’s mood altering pills. He is now an outspoken Episcopalian minister. David was a striver, always pushing towards something without an apparent motivator, that later became an allergy doctor, with a wife and kids, that he may or may not still have (the wife, not the kids). The pair of images, with no preferred order of presentation, is on the left a red kite floating over a barren brown wooden table against a washed out dirty white cheap apartment wall. The red is slightly washed out in some places from the strong light coming from the window. The second image, on the right, is a later art critic christened “through a doorway” image of their kitchen, organized by the stacked/shelved/structural components transposed against the foreground Han Solo cardboard cutout. There is a structure of the space coinciding with the structure of time coinciding with the structure of the person, and so on, and so on. See frame/movement/transposition.

The creation of both images tied a thread between a decade of separation between a kite, a kitchen, and a potato bin.
